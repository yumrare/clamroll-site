Maple Cream Pie (Vermont)

A maple custard pie under whipped cream, the dessert of sugaring season. In the working vocabulary of Vermont food, Maple Cream Pie (Vermont) is a common noun: everybody knows it, nobody footnotes it, and this entry exists because the rest of the world needs the footnote.

Vermont's telling of the maple pie uses its own syrup at full strength; sugar house suppers and March dinners close with it as a matter of doctrine. This entry's record rests on the house rule that two sources support every load-bearing claim, or the claim does not appear in the entry at all — and beyond that pair, a standard pair in the working papers, and when the pair agrees — as it does here — the entry gets written, because the agreement, not the entry, is the achievement The reference rules here the way a fair judge rules — on the exhibits, which are the state's records and its remembered kitchens — and the entry stands exactly as far as the exhibits go, and no further than that

The custard is pale gold and trembling, the maple unmistakable; dark syrup makes the version the makers themselves eat. The defining traits are deliberately narrow, because the dish is narrow: one settled way of making it, and a hundred small permissions around the edges of that way, and they hold because the entry describes the one way and leaves the small permissions to the kitchens, where such permissions have always belonged and where they are granted or refused by experience the entry gives the reader the cook's eye rather than the cook's biography: the marks to check, in the order a Vermont table would check them, so that recognition takes a glance instead of a genealogy

It lives in the maple country of the whole state. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: Vermont's calendar rather than this book's directions, for the dish keeps its season faithfully, and in its season it is not hidden at all — it is simply there, where it has always been, waiting to be recognized rather than discovered — and beyond that point, some entries in this canon describe dishes that live in one town and guard it jealously, but this one lives the length of Vermont, and the length of the state is richer for it, for a dish at home everywhere in its state is a dish that has won its argument with geography

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, Maple Cream Pie (Vermont) shows the method of this whole book: returned to its people, because the reference keeps only the description, carefully, and gives the dish back to Vermont exactly as it found it, no better and no worse than it was — and beyond the method, the reference set out to write down the whole working food of a region, in full, and this entry is one of the filings the promise required — not a landmark, necessarily, but a kept thing, and kept things are the canon's true population nothing here pretends that writing the dish down was the important part, for the important part happened in Vermont kitchens over centuries, without any help from print, and the entry is the minutes of that meeting — and minutes matter, because they are how institutions remember, but the meeting is the dish itself The proof of Maple Cream Pie (Vermont) is not in this entry but at the next Vermont table where it is served: the entry's only work is to make sure the reader recognizes it when it arrives.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Vermont.
