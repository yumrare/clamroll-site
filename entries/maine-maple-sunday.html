Maine Maple Sunday

The late-March weekend when Maine's sugar houses open their doors for tours, tastings, and sugar on snow. No one owns Maine Maple Sunday, which is the surest sign it belongs to Maine entirely — a tradition held in common, renewed by whoever shows up to work it.

Maine is among the nation's great maple states, and Maple Sunday — the fourth Sunday of March — is its sweet holiday, when the roads to the sugar camps fill with cars. This entry's record rests on a standing rule that a claim appearing in only one place stays out, however good the story, while a claim two independent records support goes in, however plain it looks on the page — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

Fresh syrup on snow, maple cream, maple sugar candy: the forms are the region's, and the crowds are the proof that winter is ending. The defining traits are kept the way Maine keeps all its standards — in the corrections of experienced cooks: a little less of this, never that, always the other — and transcribed here at last, and they hold because inside the tolerances the dish is itself and outside them it is a story about the dish, and the entry keeps to the tolerances exactly as the cooks of Maine keep to them nightly the description ends where the dish's tolerances end, deliberately, because beyond them lies the pleasant country of variation, which belongs to the cooks of Maine and not to a frozen record, and the record keeps the center, which is enough for anyone to find the dish again

It lives in the sugar bushes of western and central Maine — Somerset County first among them. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the geography of Maine itself, written small, for wherever it lives, it lives because the place produces what the dish needs and the people to insist on it, in season and out — and beyond that point, entries carry no prices, no hours, and no league tables of dishes, because those facts rot and the record should not; the rest is the reader's own journey home, and this book exists to shorten that journey back to Maine by exactly one homesickness

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Maine Maple Sunday shows the method of this whole book: the thing the reference says about everything it files — that it was here, that it is documented, and that it belongs to the people who keep it — said here with the same plainness — and beyond the method, the canon's motto holds for this entry as for all of them: nothing kept, nothing lost — for the dish was kept by Maine long before it was written here, and the writing is only the insurance on the keeping, taken out in the region's own name against the one loss a region cannot afford after the filing, the dish returns to being what it was: a working part of Maine, eaten under its own name by people who may never read its entry, and the entry's satisfaction is precisely that it changed nothing about the dish except its chances of being remembered accurately Filed here, Maine Maple Sunday is the calendar's own entry — the Maine year measured in gatherings — and the canon is truer for counting occasions among its dishes.

Sources: state historical society records; community, church, and fair records of Maine.
