Apple Pie (Rhode Island)

A double-crust pie from the state's orchards, with the Rhode Island Greening in its proudest form. Apple Pie (Rhode Island) is one of the dishes by which Rhode Island knows itself: a food with a birthplace, a season, and a standard that the home cooks enforce without ever writing it down.

The state's pie tradition runs through its orchard towns and church suppers; the Greening gave Rhode Island a pie apple of its own from the colonial period, and the pie has been the proof of it. This entry's record rests on the archive of Rhode Island on one side and the living kitchens of Rhode Island on the other, with the entry written in the narrow ground where the two agree — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

Made with Greenings, it is the tartest pie in this canon; the state's bakers serve it with the region's cheddar or, in the Italian shore towns, with coffee. The defining traits are stated flatly, because at home they are not in dispute: ask in Rhode Island and the answer returns with the confidence of long practice, and flat statement, in a reference, is a form of respect, and they hold because variations exist, as they do for every living dish, but the standard is the standard — the version a Rhode Island cook would set in front of family and call by this name without a word of explanation, and the version against which every variation is quietly measured the traits have been argued at more Rhode Island tables than any entry can count, and the argument itself is evidence — nobody argues the marks of a dish nobody cares about — and the description here is the argument's settled residue, what survived the arguing

It lives in the orchard towns and at the May breakfasts' pie tables. For the reader who left Rhode Island and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: home ground where the record is thickest, though the dish respects no fence and no town line, living wherever in Rhode Island its conditions are met — and its conditions are the state's own weather, water, and habit — and beyond that point, where it lives, it lives unselfconsciously — ordered, served, and eaten under its own name, without ceremony — by people who would be surprised to learn it needed an entry at all, and that surprise is the surest sign the entry was needed, because what a place takes for granted is exactly what the record must catch before it slips

Read alongside the other entries of Rhode Island, Apple Pie (Rhode Island) shows the method of this whole book: written for the distance — across an ocean, across a generation — by a reference that believes distance is where trust in a record is actually tested, and wrote accordingly — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold Rhode Island at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that Rhode Island food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation A dish like Apple Pie (Rhode Island) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Rhode Island keeps it.
