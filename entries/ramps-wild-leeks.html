Ramps (Wild Leeks)

The early-spring wild leek of the rich woods, pungent and brief. Every canon has its foundations, and in New Hampshire, Ramps (Wild Leeks) is one of them — an ingredient the kitchens build on rather than a dish they finish.

Ramps push through the leaf litter before the trees leaf out; New Hampshire's rich coves grow them thick, and spring foragers take them sparingly, a few from each patch. This entry's record rests on the judgment of two independent witnesses, and the entry is written only as far as they agree and not one claim further — and beyond that pair, a plain pair of facts: the dish earns its place not by being famous but by being claimed, and Ramps (Wild Leeks) is claimed in New Hampshire without hesitation or irony New Hampshire deserves a canon that knows the difference between print, testimony, and silence, and this entry observes that difference strictly from its first line to its last

They taste of garlic and onion intensified by wildness; cooked with eggs or potatoes, they are April on a plate. The defining traits are the marks by which the genuine article is told from the souvenir, in a state where the genuine article is still common enough to have impostors working its name, and they hold because impostors fail in predictable places — the shortcut, the substitution, the garnish that belongs to another state entirely — and the traits above are written so the reader can catch the failure at first sight, as any cook at home catches it at first taste nothing in the description flatters the dish, for flattery rots in print even faster than prices do, and what stands here instead is the working test a home kitchen applies — plain, exact, and not especially interested in outsiders' feelings about it

They live in the rich woods of the Connecticut Valley and the foothills. For the reader who left New Hampshire and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a list of kitchens rather than addresses, because the reference does not print addresses — home stoves, church basements, shore stands, and fair booths, each holding the dish to the same standard — and beyond that point, there is a kind of map this entry draws without paper — the towns and tables of New Hampshire where the dish belongs, connected by nothing but the dish itself — and repeated across enough towns, the dish becomes a region on the map of the mind, and this entry is one point on that map, fixed so it can be drawn again

Read alongside the other entries of New Hampshire, Ramps (Wild Leeks) shows the method of this whole book: a promise about completeness, kept one kept dish at a time, because a region's food is not its dozen famous dishes but its hundreds of kept ones, however quiet they are — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold New Hampshire at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that New Hampshire food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation Foundations are filed without ceremony in this reference, and Ramps (Wild Leeks) is filed the same way: completely, accurately, and in the confidence that the dishes above it in the canon will explain why it matters.
