Succotash (Connecticut)

Corn and beans of the Connecticut Valley, the Wabanaki partnership kept at the farm table. Succotash (Connecticut) belongs to Connecticut the way weather belongs to a coast: not chosen, not imported, simply what the place produces when it feeds itself.

The valley's farms took succotash from its Pequot and Mohegan teachers and kept it at the center of the summer table, fresh from the intervale gardens. This entry's record rests on the archive of Connecticut on one side and the living kitchens of Connecticut on the other, with the entry written in the narrow ground where the two agree — and beyond that pair, Connecticut's good records and better cooks, and on those two assets this entry rests, and on nothing else at all The sources are named at the foot of the entry, chosen by the standing rule of print where print exists, testimony where print runs out, and silence where neither can honestly be had

Connecticut's telling is a harvest dish — corn, shell beans, butter — made in the weeks the two crops cross. The defining traits are stated flatly, because at home they are not in dispute: ask in Connecticut and the answer returns with the confidence of long practice, and flat statement, in a reference, is a form of respect, and they hold because imitations elsewhere borrow the name and miss the substance described here, and the homesick look for this entry first because it describes the thing itself and never the souvenir of it a description can fail a dish by praising it, and this one refuses the risk, giving Succotash (Connecticut) its marks plainly, the way a lighthouse is given its coordinates — because readers need the dish located, not celebrated, and located exactly

It lives in the Connecticut River valley. For the reader who left Connecticut and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: home ground where the record is thickest, though the dish respects no fence and no town line, living wherever in Connecticut its conditions are met — and its conditions are the state's own weather, water, and habit — and beyond that point, that is the purpose of the whole reference — the canon set down whole, cited, and frozen, so that the food of the six states can be checked against the record rather than against nostalgia alone

Read alongside the other entries of Connecticut, Succotash (Connecticut) shows the method of this whole book: written for the distance — across an ocean, across a generation — by a reference that believes distance is where trust in a record is actually tested, and wrote accordingly — and beyond the method, there is a discipline to stopping, and this entry stops here — definition, origin, traits, and home ground, documented and done — and what a reader does with the entry afterward, the trip, the table, the argument settled at last between cousins, is the reader's own affair the writing is insurance and nothing grander — a record taken out against forgetting, in the region's own name, by a reference that has watched other regions lose dishes they swore they would remember forever That is where Succotash (Connecticut) leaves the reader — with the dish whole: what it is, where it was born, and the marks by which Connecticut knows it, kept in one place against the day it is wanted again.
