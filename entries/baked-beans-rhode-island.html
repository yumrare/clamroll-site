Baked Beans (Rhode Island)

Beans baked the regional way, with the state's own accent of the shore's molasses trade. In the working vocabulary of Rhode Island food, Baked Beans (Rhode Island) is a common noun: everybody knows it, nobody footnotes it, and this entry exists because the rest of the world needs the footnote.

Newport and Bristol were molasses ports of the triangle trade, and the bean pot took the harbor's sweetness early; brown bread rides alongside, as everywhere. This entry's record rests on the contents of the reference's own working file: one source that can be shelved in a library, and one that can be tasted at a table — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

The beans are the small white kinds, baked dark and glossy; the pot's crust is the cook's signature, and the brown bread is sliced thick to carry them. The defining traits are each one the part that, removed, would make a Rhode Island cook say without hesitation that it is no longer the thing itself, and they hold because nothing in the description is decorative, for it is an inventory of necessities — nothing more, and nothing softer than that — compiled from the practice of the dish's own kitchens every mark listed was earned by the dish in service and not assigned by this reference, which is a clerk here and not an inventor, having written down only what Rhode Island enforced, and enforcement repeated across enough kitchens and years is what a standard actually is

They live at bean suppers across Rhode Island, in the church halls and grange kitchens of the smallest state. For the reader who left Rhode Island and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a landscape and a season rather than a door number — find the landscape, in the season, and the dish is there waiting, as it has been every year within memory — and beyond that point, picture it where Rhode Island keeps it — in use, on tables, in season, among people who would find the idea of preserving it faintly comic, because from where they sit it is in no danger whatsoever — and then remember that the reference has seen regions forget, and so it writes, and the writing is this entry's whole apology and excuse

Read alongside the other entries of Rhode Island, Baked Beans (Rhode Island) shows the method of this whole book: filed level with the emblem itself, because no entry is the point and every entry is, the clam roll on the door being one sandwich and the four hundred filings behind it being the reference proper — and beyond the method, the canon's motto holds for this entry as for all of them: nothing kept, nothing lost — for the dish was kept by Rhode Island long before it was written here, and the writing is only the insurance on the keeping, taken out in the region's own name against the one loss a region cannot afford after the filing, the dish returns to being what it was: a working part of Rhode Island, eaten under its own name by people who may never read its entry, and the entry's satisfaction is precisely that it changed nothing about the dish except its chances of being remembered accurately A dish like Baked Beans (Rhode Island) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Rhode Island keeps it.
