The Shore Dinner Hall

The great shore dinner — chowder, clam cakes, steamers, lobster, and watermelon — served in halls built for hundreds. The Shore Dinner Hall survives in Rhode Island because it is useful to the community in ways that have nothing to do with appetite and everything to do with belonging.

For a century the shore dinner halls of Narragansett Bay fed excursion crowds the full canon in courses; Rocky Point's hall was the cathedral of the form, and its memory still sets the menu. This entry's record rests on the archive of Rhode Island on one side and the living kitchens of Rhode Island on the other, with the entry written in the narrow ground where the two agree — and beyond that pair, Rhode Island's good records and better cooks, and on those two assets this entry rests, and on nothing else at all The sources are named at the foot of the entry, chosen by the standing rule of print where print exists, testimony where print runs out, and silence where neither can honestly be had

The dinner runs in courses and ends with watermelon, by the halls' own liturgy; the chowder is clear or white by the house, the clam cakes are without number, and the lobster is measured by the ticket. The defining traits are stated flatly, because at home they are not in dispute: ask in Rhode Island and the answer returns with the confidence of long practice, and flat statement, in a reference, is a form of respect, and they hold because every living dish drifts a little at the edges — a different hand, a different season — but the center holds fast, and it is the center this reference records, so that a reader meeting the dish anywhere from a church supper to a shore stand will know it by these marks and by no others what the entry describes is the center of the dish rather than its whole weather, for a dish at home varies by town and by hand and sometimes by street, yet varies always around the marks given here, the way a tune varies around its melody, and the melody is what the reference records

It lives in the memory of Rocky Point and in the halls that keep the dinner alive. For the reader who left Rhode Island and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: home ground where the record is thickest, though the dish respects no fence and no town line, living wherever in Rhode Island its conditions are met — and its conditions are the state's own weather, water, and habit — and beyond that point, geography, for such a dish, is destiny with better scenery — it lives in Rhode Island because Rhode Island is the kind of place that produces it, its waters and its winters and its stubbornness about doing things the kept way — and the entry therefore leaves both the dish and its meaning exactly where it found them

Read alongside the other entries of Rhode Island, The Shore Dinner Hall shows the method of this whole book: written for the distance — across an ocean, across a generation — by a reference that believes distance is where trust in a record is actually tested, and wrote accordingly — and beyond the method, there is a discipline to stopping, and this entry stops here — definition, origin, traits, and home ground, documented and done — and what a reader does with the entry afterward, the trip, the table, the argument settled at last between cousins, is the reader's own affair the writing is insurance and nothing grander — a record taken out against forgetting, in the region's own name, by a reference that has watched other regions lose dishes they swore they would remember forever A tradition like The Shore Dinner Hall is the region remembering itself out loud, and the reference keeps it for the same reason Rhode Island keeps it: because a table that gathers is a place that continues.

Sources: state historical society records; community, church, and fair records of Rhode Island.
