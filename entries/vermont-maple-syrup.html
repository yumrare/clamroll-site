Vermont Maple Syrup

The syrup of the sugar maple, Vermont's defining food and the nation's standard for maple. It is not an exaggeration to call Vermont Maple Syrup infrastructure: roads move goods, but this is what Vermont actually runs on.

Vermont has led American maple production for over a century; its grading law and its sugar makers set the terms the whole industry follows, and the state's name on a jug is a grade in itself. This entry's record rests on a standing rule that a claim appearing in only one place stays out, however good the story, while a claim two independent records support goes in, however plain it looks on the page — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

It runs golden-delicate to very dark-strong; the early syrup is the table grade, the late dark syrup the baker's treasure. Nothing else tastes like the woods in spring. The defining traits are kept the way Vermont keeps all its standards — in the corrections of experienced cooks: a little less of this, never that, always the other — and transcribed here at last, and they hold because the standard is written in no cookbook, and the traits above are the kitchen corrections themselves, transcribed at last into print for readers who were not raised within earshot of them to describe the dish loosely would be to lose it, because the dish is its details, and Vermont cooks hold those details the way singers hold pitch — not by thinking about them, but by wincing when they are wrong — and the traits above are the wince, transcribed

It lives in every county of Vermont, from the Northeast Kingdom to the Bennington hills. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the geography of Vermont itself, written small, for wherever it lives, it lives because the place produces what the dish needs and the people to insist on it, in season and out — and beyond that point, that is the purpose of the whole reference — the canon set down whole, cited, and frozen, so that the food of the six states can be checked against the record rather than against nostalgia alone

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, Vermont Maple Syrup shows the method of this whole book: the thing the reference says about everything it files — that it was here, that it is documented, and that it belongs to the people who keep it — said here with the same plainness — and beyond the method, the reference set out to write down the whole working food of a region, in full, and this entry is one of the filings the promise required — not a landmark, necessarily, but a kept thing, and kept things are the canon's true population nothing here pretends that writing the dish down was the important part, for the important part happened in Vermont kitchens over centuries, without any help from print, and the entry is the minutes of that meeting — and minutes matter, because they are how institutions remember, but the meeting is the dish itself That is the place of Vermont Maple Syrup in the Vermont canon — underneath, everywhere, and easy to overlook until the day it is gone, which is exactly why it is written down here in full.
