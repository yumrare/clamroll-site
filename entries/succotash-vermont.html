Succotash (Vermont)

Corn and beans from the same garden row, cooked together at summer's height. No dish enters the Vermont canon on charm alone, and Succotash (Vermont) did not: it is here because kitchens across the state still make it, name it, and correct anyone who makes it wrong.

Vermont's succotash is the valley garden's August dish — fresh corn and shell beans with butter, in the Wabanaki partnership the colonists learned and never improved. This entry's record rests on the house rule that two sources support every load-bearing claim, or the claim does not appear in the entry at all — and beyond that pair, documentation that supports exactly this much and no more, for Vermont documented this one thoroughly, in ink and in practice, and the entry is sized to that evidence Sources are cited so the next person can check the line, and the line here runs through the published record of Vermont and through its practiced kitchens, and both sides of it were walked, and both of them held

A little cream finishes it in the dairy country; the dried winter version belongs to the storehouse and another entry of the year. The defining traits are deliberately narrow, because the dish is narrow: one settled way of making it, and a hundred small permissions around the edges of that way, and they hold because imitations elsewhere borrow the name and miss the substance described here, and the homesick look for this entry first because it describes the thing itself and never the souvenir of it a description can fail a dish by praising it, and this one refuses the risk, giving Succotash (Vermont) its marks plainly, the way a lighthouse is given its coordinates — because readers need the dish located, not celebrated, and located exactly

It lives in the valley gardens of the whole state. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: Vermont's calendar rather than this book's directions, for the dish keeps its season faithfully, and in its season it is not hidden at all — it is simply there, where it has always been, waiting to be recognized rather than discovered — and beyond that point, picture it where Vermont keeps it — in use, on tables, in season, among people who would find the idea of preserving it faintly comic, because from where they sit it is in no danger whatsoever — and then remember that the reference has seen regions forget, and so it writes, and the writing is this entry's whole apology and excuse

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, Succotash (Vermont) shows the method of this whole book: returned to its people, because the reference keeps only the description, carefully, and gives the dish back to Vermont exactly as it found it, no better and no worse than it was — and beyond the method, the book files by state and treats all states alike, refusing fame, fashion, and the editor's appetite as arrangements, and this entry benefits from the refusal exactly as the emblem does, for in this book, unusually, the footnote and the headline weigh the same the record rests there, not because more could not be said, but because a reference earns its trust by stopping where its evidence stops, and the evidence for this entry stops exactly here, complete and checked A dish like Succotash (Vermont) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Vermont keeps it.
