The Quahog

The hard clam of Narragansett Bay — littleneck, cherrystone, and chowder clam by size — and the state shell of Rhode Island. The first cooks of Rhode Island built on what the place gave them, and the place gave The Quahog in quantities that amounted to an instruction.

The quahog is the bay's foundation: Indigenous Rhode Islanders made wampum of its purple shell, and the state named the shell its own; its meat fills the chowders, cakes, and stuffies of this whole canon. This entry's record rests on the documentary record of Rhode Island — its historical and agricultural archives — laid beside the practiced memory of the cooks who keep the dish, each checked against the other before either is believed alone — and beyond that pair, an entry that claims nothing its sources do not jointly support, written under a discipline that, applied four hundred times, is the reference itself A reader checking the entry against the archive will find it conservative throughout — claiming less than the lore claims, documenting more than the lists document — and that ratio, less claimed and more proven, is the reference's whole method in miniature

The little ones go raw on the half shell; the giants, tough and flavorful, are born for the chowder pot and the stuffing bowl. The defining traits are the test this entry sets for the genuine article, drawn from the place the dish comes from and from nowhere else on earth, and they hold because imitations elsewhere borrow the name and miss the substance described here, and the homesick look for this entry first because it describes the thing itself and never the souvenir of it a description can fail a dish by praising it, and this one refuses the risk, giving The Quahog its marks plainly, the way a lighthouse is given its coordinates — because readers need the dish located, not celebrated, and located exactly

It lives in Narragansett Bay, dug and dredged the length of the state. For the reader who left Rhode Island and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: sitting among its equals in the canon, neither lifted nor buried, which is the reference's founding rule: the emblem on the door is one entry among hundreds, and the hundreds are the point of the exercise — and beyond that point, some entries in this canon describe dishes that live in one town and guard it jealously, but this one lives the length of Rhode Island, and the length of the state is richer for it, for a dish at home everywhere in its state is a dish that has won its argument with geography

Read alongside the other entries of Rhode Island, The Quahog shows the method of this whole book: in a book named for a sandwich, the temptation is to make every entry a celebration, and this reference resists the temptation as firmly as it resisted it for the emblem itself — affection governing the voice, strictness governing the facts — and beyond the method, the entry is the region's memory of itself, written by itself and outsourced to print so that forgetting has to work harder to erase it, while the dish, unbothered by its own documentation, goes on being made at home every canon ends up teaching its readers how to read it, and this one teaches a simple lesson through every entry: read for the record, not the rhetoric — a dish, a state, a set of marks, a home ground, with the rhetoric kept on a short leash throughout — so that a reader who finishes knowing the record has read it correctly The entry for The Quahog ends where the ingredient begins — at the Rhode Island shore, field, or sugar bush where it is gathered — because an ingredient's biography is its ground, and the ground is still there.
