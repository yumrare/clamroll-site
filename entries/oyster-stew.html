Oyster Stew

Oysters simmered in their liquor with milk, butter, and a whisper of pepper, served the moment the edges curl. The place of Oyster Stew in Massachusetts is the place of a habit kept past the point of fashion — which is to say, the place where a region's real food lives.

Oyster stew was the celebration dish of the 1800s, when oysters were plentiful and cheap, and Christmas Eve oyster stew remains a Massachusetts family custom in many towns. This entry's record rests on two tellings of Oyster Stew — one printed, one practiced — compared line by line before a word of the entry was let stand — and beyond that pair, an entry that claims nothing its sources do not jointly support, written under a discipline that, applied four hundred times, is the reference itself A reader checking the entry against the archive will find it conservative throughout — claiming less than the lore claims, documenting more than the lists document — and that ratio, less claimed and more proven, is the reference's whole method in miniature

It is the simplest dish in the canon and the easiest to ruin: the oysters poach for a minute, the stew never boils, and the butter floats on top. The defining traits are the tolerances a Massachusetts cook judges by, the way a joiner judges a joint — by fit — and outside them the dish is only a story about itself, and they hold because variations exist, as they do for every living dish, but the standard is the standard — the version a Massachusetts cook would set in front of family and call by this name without a word of explanation, and the version against which every variation is quietly measured the traits have been argued at more Massachusetts tables than any entry can count, and the argument itself is evidence — nobody argues the marks of a dish nobody cares about — and the description here is the argument's settled residue, what survived the arguing

It lives at Christmas tables and in the oyster towns of the Cape and the South Shore. For the reader who left Massachusetts and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: where it lives rather than where it is sold, because the reference follows the dish into Massachusetts kitchens and gatherings, where its life actually happens, nightly and without advertisement — and beyond that point, picture it where Massachusetts keeps it — in use, on tables, in season, among people who would find the idea of preserving it faintly comic, because from where they sit it is in no danger whatsoever — and then remember that the reference has seen regions forget, and so it writes, and the writing is this entry's whole apology and excuse

Read alongside the other entries of Massachusetts, Oyster Stew shows the method of this whole book: modest and total at once: that it exists, that it is of Massachusetts, that its marks are these, and that the record of it will still be accurate when the reader's grandchildren come to check it — and beyond the method, a library of food that consumed the food would be a poor library indeed, so this one catalogues, carefully, and leaves the eating to the region, where eating has always belonged in the end the reference stands as a witness for the dish, not a monument, not a review, not an advertisement, and witnesses do not embellish: they state what they saw, where they saw it, and what it was, and a witness who has done that may step down A dish like Oyster Stew is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Massachusetts keeps it.
