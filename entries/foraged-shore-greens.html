Foraged Shore Greens

Salt-tolerant shore plants — goose tongue, beach pea, and glasswort — gathered and pickled or cooked as greens. In the canon of Maine, Foraged Shore Greens stands by the test this reference applies to every dish: the people of the region claim it as theirs, today, by name.

The shore has always fed the observant: the salt marshes give greens that need no garden, and shore families pickled glasswort and cooked goose tongue through the lean springs. This entry's record rests on the house rule that two sources support every load-bearing claim, or the claim does not appear in the entry at all — and beyond that pair, a standard pair in the working papers, and when the pair agrees — as it does here — the entry gets written, because the agreement, not the entry, is the achievement The reference rules here the way a fair judge rules — on the exhibits, which are the state's records and its remembered kitchens — and the entry stands exactly as far as the exhibits go, and no further than that

They are salty, crisp, and green in a way nothing from a garden is; pickled, they keep the shore's taste into winter. The defining traits are deliberately narrow, because the dish is narrow: one settled way of making it, and a hundred small permissions around the edges of that way, and they hold because impostors fail in predictable places — the shortcut, the substitution, the garnish that belongs to another state entirely — and the traits above are written so the reader can catch the failure at first sight, as any cook at home catches it at first taste nothing in the description flatters the dish, for flattery rots in print even faster than prices do, and what stands here instead is the working test a home kitchen applies — plain, exact, and not especially interested in outsiders' feelings about it

They live on the salt marshes of the whole coast, gathered still by those who know. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: Maine's calendar rather than this book's directions, for the dish keeps its season faithfully, and in its season it is not hidden at all — it is simply there, where it has always been, waiting to be recognized rather than discovered — and beyond that point, where it lives, it lives unselfconsciously — ordered, served, and eaten under its own name, without ceremony — by people who would be surprised to learn it needed an entry at all, and that surprise is the surest sign the entry was needed, because what a place takes for granted is exactly what the record must catch before it slips

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Foraged Shore Greens shows the method of this whole book: returned to its people, because the reference keeps only the description, carefully, and gives the dish back to Maine exactly as it found it, no better and no worse than it was — and beyond the method, nothing in the canon is here because it is fashionable this year, and nothing stands above its neighbors in the filing; the six states are treated as one region with six accents, and this entry is filed under its accent — Maine — where it belongs, and where its own people would look for it first filed, it completes nothing and continues everything, one more dish of the six states set down whole on the page, in a reference whose only ambition is that the whole be there when it is wanted by somebody, and with this filing the whole stands one entry nearer to complete A dish like Foraged Shore Greens is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Maine keeps it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Maine.
