Maple Sugaring (New Hampshire)

The late-winter boiling season, when the sap runs and the sugar houses steam. There is no ticket to Maple Sugaring (New Hampshire) in the way outsiders expect, no spectacle arranged for them; New Hampshire does it for itself, and the doing is the whole performance.

Sugaring is the first farm work of the New Hampshire year; families and commercial camps alike boil through March, and the sugar house door stands open to visitors. This entry's record rests on the judgment of two independent witnesses, and the entry is written only as far as they agree and not one claim further — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

The season is governed by the freeze-thaw swing; a warm March can end it in a week, which is why the whole state watches the weather like a crop report. The defining traits are the marks by which the genuine article is told from the souvenir, in a state where the genuine article is still common enough to have impostors working its name, and they hold because variations exist, as they do for every living dish, but the standard is the standard — the version a New Hampshire cook would set in front of family and call by this name without a word of explanation, and the version against which every variation is quietly measured the traits have been argued at more New Hampshire tables than any entry can count, and the argument itself is evidence — nobody argues the marks of a dish nobody cares about — and the description here is the argument's settled residue, what survived the arguing

It lives in the maple towns from Pittsburg to the Monadnock region. For the reader who left New Hampshire and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a list of kitchens rather than addresses, because the reference does not print addresses — home stoves, church basements, shore stands, and fair booths, each holding the dish to the same standard — and beyond that point, it is not an artifact behind glass in a museum but a working part of New Hampshire, as current as tonight, findable by anyone who goes where the entry says it lives, and the record and the dish agree about the address, and the agreement is the point of both

Read alongside the other entries of New Hampshire, Maple Sugaring (New Hampshire) shows the method of this whole book: a promise about completeness, kept one kept dish at a time, because a region's food is not its dozen famous dishes but its hundreds of kept ones, however quiet they are — and beyond the method, the canon's motto holds for this entry as for all of them: nothing kept, nothing lost — for the dish was kept by New Hampshire long before it was written here, and the writing is only the insurance on the keeping, taken out in the region's own name against the one loss a region cannot afford after the filing, the dish returns to being what it was: a working part of New Hampshire, eaten under its own name by people who may never read its entry, and the entry's satisfaction is precisely that it changed nothing about the dish except its chances of being remembered accurately The reference records Maple Sugaring (New Hampshire) lastingly and holds it lightly: the entry belongs to the book, the tradition belongs to New Hampshire, and the book knows the difference.
