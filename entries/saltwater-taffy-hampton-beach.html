Saltwater Taffy (Hampton Beach)

Pulled taffy in a dozen flavors, boxed as the Seacoast's souvenir. Ask in New Hampshire what belongs on the table and Saltwater Taffy (Hampton Beach) will be named — not as a novelty, but as a habit of the place, kept by the people who live there.

Taffy came to Hampton Beach with the boardwalk era; the pulling machines in the shop windows are as much the attraction as the candy. This entry's record rests on two independent sources for every claim that carries weight — and beyond that pair, the state's own historical and agricultural record standing on one side and the documented memory of its cooks, fairs, and fisheries on the other, two different kinds of keeping tested against each other before either one is believed on its own Where the record thins into lore, this entry says so; where the evidence is strong, it rules accordingly; and since the contested dishes of New England require someone finally to rule, the ruling here is made on evidence and not on affection

It is soft, sweet, and faintly salty in name more than fact; the box assortment is the unit of purchase and of memory. The defining traits are the form described here is the one the home kitchens of New Hampshire recognize, allow no substitutes for, and pass down by correction at the stove from one generation's hands to the next, and they hold because nothing in the description is decorative, for it is an inventory of necessities — nothing more, and nothing softer than that — compiled from the practice of the dish's own kitchens every mark listed was earned by the dish in service and not assigned by this reference, which is a clerk here and not an inventor, having written down only what New Hampshire enforced, and enforcement repeated across enough kitchens and years is what a standard actually is

It lives at Hampton Beach and the Seacoast's summer main streets. For the reader who left New Hampshire and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the dish as it is kept at home, described while home still keeps it, in the kitchens and seasons named above — and beyond that point, the homesick reader will read the home ground the way earlier generations read letters from home — slowly, and more than once — and the entry was written to bear that kind of reading, naming the ground plainly and refusing to embroider the naming, because plain naming, repeated kindly, is the whole comfort on offer here

Read alongside the other entries of New Hampshire, Saltwater Taffy (Hampton Beach) shows the method of this whole book: a dish is admitted on living identity, documented on two sources, and described at the length its record earns, and not one line further — and beyond the method, the entry is the region's memory of itself, written by itself and outsourced to print so that forgetting has to work harder to erase it, while the dish, unbothered by its own documentation, goes on being made at home every canon ends up teaching its readers how to read it, and this one teaches a simple lesson through every entry: read for the record, not the rhetoric — a dish, a state, a set of marks, a home ground, with the rhetoric kept on a short leash throughout — so that a reader who finishes knowing the record has read it correctly A dish like Saltwater Taffy (Hampton Beach) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as New Hampshire keeps it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of New Hampshire.
