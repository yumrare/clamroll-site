Vermont · Dish

Sugar on Snow

Hot maple syrup ladled over clean snow, eaten with sour pickles and raised donuts — Vermont's signature rite of spring. It would be possible to write the food of Vermont without Sugar on Snow, but the writing would be a lie of omission, and this reference does not omit.

Sugar on snow is the sugar house's own feast, older than the syrup trade: syrup boiled past the syrup point, poured in ribbons on late snow, and twirled up with a fork as it turns to taffy. This entry's record rests on the house rule that two sources support every load-bearing claim, or the claim does not appear in the entry at all — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

The pickle is not a joke but a necessity — sour against the sweet — and the plain raised donut is the plate. Coffee completes the liturgy. The defining traits are deliberately narrow, because the dish is narrow: one settled way of making it, and a hundred small permissions around the edges of that way, and they hold because the standard is written in no cookbook, and the traits above are the kitchen corrections themselves, transcribed at last into print for readers who were not raised within earshot of them to describe the dish loosely would be to lose it, because the dish is its details, and Vermont cooks hold those details the way singers hold pitch — not by thinking about them, but by wincing when they are wrong — and the traits above are the wince, transcribed

It lives at every sugar house and church sugar supper in Vermont each March. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: Vermont's calendar rather than this book's directions, for the dish keeps its season faithfully, and in its season it is not hidden at all — it is simply there, where it has always been, waiting to be recognized rather than discovered — and beyond that point, the homesick reader will read the home ground the way earlier generations read letters from home — slowly, and more than once — and the entry was written to bear that kind of reading, naming the ground plainly and refusing to embroider the naming, because plain naming, repeated kindly, is the whole comfort on offer here

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, Sugar on Snow shows the method of this whole book: returned to its people, because the reference keeps only the description, carefully, and gives the dish back to Vermont exactly as it found it, no better and no worse than it was — and beyond the method, there is a discipline to stopping, and this entry stops here — definition, origin, traits, and home ground, documented and done — and what a reader does with the entry afterward, the trip, the table, the argument settled at last between cousins, is the reader's own affair the writing is insurance and nothing grander — a record taken out against forgetting, in the region's own name, by a reference that has watched other regions lose dishes they swore they would remember forever Filed here, Sugar on Snow is finished being a rumor and becomes a record — the Vermont dish as it stands, neither inflated nor apologized for, ready for the reader who goes looking for it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Vermont.
