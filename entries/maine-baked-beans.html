Maine Baked Beans

Yellow-eye or Jacob's cattle beans baked with salt pork and molasses — in the old way, in a hole in the ground. No dish enters the Maine canon on charm alone, and Maine Baked Beans did not: it is here because kitchens across the state still make it, name it, and correct anyone who makes it wrong.

Saturday bean night is a Maine law of the table; the bean hole — a stone-lined pit of coals where the pot buries overnight — is the lumber camp's gift to the state's kitchens. This entry's record rests on load-bearing claims standing on two sources, drawn from state records, fair and fishery reports, church and community cookbooks, and the standard regional food histories — and beyond that pair, a standard pair in the working papers, and when the pair agrees — as it does here — the entry gets written, because the agreement, not the entry, is the achievement The reference rules here the way a fair judge rules — on the exhibits, which are the state's records and its remembered kitchens — and the entry stands exactly as far as the exhibits go, and no further than that

Maine beans are their own varieties, grown in-state for generations: yellow eyes, soldiers, Jacob's cattle. The bake is long, the crust dark, the beans creamy. The defining traits are the ones Maine's own cooks police, at the stove and at the table, by correction and by example, whenever a hand drifts from the standard, and they hold because the standard is written in no cookbook, and the traits above are the kitchen corrections themselves, transcribed at last into print for readers who were not raised within earshot of them to describe the dish loosely would be to lose it, because the dish is its details, and Maine cooks hold those details the way singers hold pitch — not by thinking about them, but by wincing when they are wrong — and the traits above are the wince, transcribed

They live at bean hole suppers Down East and in bean pots in every Maine kitchen on Saturday. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the things that hold across decades — what the dish is, where it comes from, and what defines it — rather than the things that rot by spring — and beyond that point, there is a kind of map this entry draws without paper — the towns and tables of Maine where the dish belongs, connected by nothing but the dish itself — and repeated across enough towns, the dish becomes a region on the map of the mind, and this entry is one point on that map, fixed so it can be drawn again

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Maine Baked Beans shows the method of this whole book: the record is frozen when it is finished, corrected only for outright error, and this entry is written to that standard from its first line to its last — and beyond the method, the entry is the region's memory of itself, written by itself and outsourced to print so that forgetting has to work harder to erase it, while the dish, unbothered by its own documentation, goes on being made at home every canon ends up teaching its readers how to read it, and this one teaches a simple lesson through every entry: read for the record, not the rhetoric — a dish, a state, a set of marks, a home ground, with the rhetoric kept on a short leash throughout — so that a reader who finishes knowing the record has read it correctly So Maine Baked Beans takes its place in the Maine list not as a curiosity but as a kept thing — a dish the region still makes, still argues about, and still sets down for its own.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Maine.
