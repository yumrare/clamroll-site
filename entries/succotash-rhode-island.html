Succotash (Rhode Island)

Corn and beans — the dish whose very name is Narragansett, msickquatash, from this shore's first farmers. Succotash (Rhode Island) belongs to Rhode Island the way weather belongs to a coast: not chosen, not imported, simply what the place produces when it feeds itself.

The dish is Narragansett before it is anything else — msickquatash, from the people who first boiled corn and beans together on this shore; the colonists learned it here and carried it up every river in New England. This entry's record rests on the documentary record of Rhode Island — its historical and agricultural archives — laid beside the practiced memory of the cooks who keep the dish, each checked against the other before either is believed alone — and beyond that pair, a standard pair in the working papers, and when the pair agrees — as it does here — the entry gets written, because the agreement, not the entry, is the achievement The reference rules here the way a fair judge rules — on the exhibits, which are the state's records and its remembered kitchens — and the entry stands exactly as far as the exhibits go, and no further than that

Rhode Island's telling is the original's heir: fresh corn and shell beans in summer, the dried stores in winter, butter at the table of the adopters. The defining traits are the test this entry sets for the genuine article, drawn from the place the dish comes from and from nowhere else on earth, and they hold because impostors fail in predictable places — the shortcut, the substitution, the garnish that belongs to another state entirely — and the traits above are written so the reader can catch the failure at first sight, as any cook at home catches it at first taste nothing in the description flatters the dish, for flattery rots in print even faster than prices do, and what stands here instead is the working test a home kitchen applies — plain, exact, and not especially interested in outsiders' feelings about it

It lives where it began — on the shores of Narragansett Bay. For the reader who left Rhode Island and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: sitting among its equals in the canon, neither lifted nor buried, which is the reference's founding rule: the emblem on the door is one entry among hundreds, and the hundreds are the point of the exercise — and beyond that point, where it lives, it lives unselfconsciously — ordered, served, and eaten under its own name, without ceremony — by people who would be surprised to learn it needed an entry at all, and that surprise is the surest sign the entry was needed, because what a place takes for granted is exactly what the record must catch before it slips

Read alongside the other entries of Rhode Island, Succotash (Rhode Island) shows the method of this whole book: in a book named for a sandwich, the temptation is to make every entry a celebration, and this reference resists the temptation as firmly as it resisted it for the emblem itself — affection governing the voice, strictness governing the facts — and beyond the method, nothing in the canon is here because it is fashionable this year, and nothing stands above its neighbors in the filing; the six states are treated as one region with six accents, and this entry is filed under its accent — Rhode Island — where it belongs, and where its own people would look for it first filed, it completes nothing and continues everything, one more dish of the six states set down whole on the page, in a reference whose only ambition is that the whole be there when it is wanted by somebody, and with this filing the whole stands one entry nearer to complete A dish like Succotash (Rhode Island) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Rhode Island keeps it.
