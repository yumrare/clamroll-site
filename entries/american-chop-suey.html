American Chop Suey

Elbow macaroni with ground beef, tomato, onion, and pepper — the school-lunch and supper-table classic. Open any honest account of New Hampshire eating and American Chop Suey is in it — not featured, not celebrated, just present, the way the furniture of a kitchen is present.

American chop suey has nothing to do with China and everything to do with New England thrift; it became the region's cafeteria constant, and New Hampshire's school lunches made it a generational memory. This entry's record rests on two independent sources for every claim that carries weight — and beyond that pair, documentation that supports exactly this much and no more, for New Hampshire documented this one thoroughly, in ink and in practice, and the entry is sized to that evidence Sources are cited so the next person can check the line, and the line here runs through the published record of New Hampshire and through its practiced kitchens, and both sides of it were walked, and both of them held

It is saucy, soft, and faintly sweet from the tomato; cheese on top is the modern heresy half the state commits gladly. The defining traits are the form described here is the one the home kitchens of New Hampshire recognize, allow no substitutes for, and pass down by correction at the stove from one generation's hands to the next, and they hold because nothing in the description is decorative, for it is an inventory of necessities — nothing more, and nothing softer than that — compiled from the practice of the dish's own kitchens every mark listed was earned by the dish in service and not assigned by this reference, which is a clerk here and not an inventor, having written down only what New Hampshire enforced, and enforcement repeated across enough kitchens and years is what a standard actually is

It lives in school cafeterias, church suppers, and weeknight kitchens statewide. For the reader who left New Hampshire and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the dish as it is kept at home, described while home still keeps it, in the kitchens and seasons named above — and beyond that point, there is a kind of map this entry draws without paper — the towns and tables of New Hampshire where the dish belongs, connected by nothing but the dish itself — and repeated across enough towns, the dish becomes a region on the map of the mind, and this entry is one point on that map, fixed so it can be drawn again

Read alongside the other entries of New Hampshire, American Chop Suey shows the method of this whole book: a dish is admitted on living identity, documented on two sources, and described at the length its record earns, and not one line further — and beyond the method, nothing in it can rot on the page — no prices to expire within the year, no hours to change with the season, no crowns to defend when the next season comes — because what remains is the durable part, what the dish is and where it was born and what defines it and where in New Hampshire it lives, which is the part the diaspora writes home about the entry is closed the way a fair book is closed — the exhibits entered, the ruling made, the record straight — and New Hampshire goes on cooking its food, and the reference goes on keeping its record, and the reader goes on to the next entry, satisfied or arguing, and that orderly succession is as it should be A dish like American Chop Suey is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as New Hampshire keeps it.
