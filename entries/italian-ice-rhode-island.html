Italian Ice (Rhode Island)

Shaved and churned fruit ices from the Italian stands — lemon first, as doctrine requires. It would be possible to write the food of Rhode Island without Italian Ice (Rhode Island), but the writing would be a lie of omission, and this reference does not omit.

Italian ice came up with the Italian soda fountains and pushcarts of Providence; the shore stands took it over as the summer's second sacrament beside frozen lemonade. This entry's record rests on the documentary record of Rhode Island — its historical and agricultural archives — laid beside the practiced memory of the cooks who keep the dish, each checked against the other before either is believed alone — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

It is dense, spoonable, and intensely fruited; lemon is the standard by which the state's stands are judged in the summer talk of the neighborhood. The defining traits are the test this entry sets for the genuine article, drawn from the place the dish comes from and from nowhere else on earth, and they hold because variations exist, as they do for every living dish, but the standard is the standard — the version a Rhode Island cook would set in front of family and call by this name without a word of explanation, and the version against which every variation is quietly measured the traits have been argued at more Rhode Island tables than any entry can count, and the argument itself is evidence — nobody argues the marks of a dish nobody cares about — and the description here is the argument's settled residue, what survived the arguing

It lives at the Italian ice stands of Providence and the shore towns. For the reader who left Rhode Island and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: sitting among its equals in the canon, neither lifted nor buried, which is the reference's founding rule: the emblem on the door is one entry among hundreds, and the hundreds are the point of the exercise — and beyond that point, entries carry no prices, no hours, and no league tables of dishes, because those facts rot and the record should not; the rest is the reader's own journey home, and this book exists to shorten that journey back to Rhode Island by exactly one homesickness

Read alongside the other entries of Rhode Island, Italian Ice (Rhode Island) shows the method of this whole book: in a book named for a sandwich, the temptation is to make every entry a celebration, and this reference resists the temptation as firmly as it resisted it for the emblem itself — affection governing the voice, strictness governing the facts — and beyond the method, nothing in it can rot on the page — no prices to expire within the year, no hours to change with the season, no crowns to defend when the next season comes — because what remains is the durable part, what the dish is and where it was born and what defines it and where in Rhode Island it lives, which is the part the diaspora writes home about the entry is closed the way a fair book is closed — the exhibits entered, the ruling made, the record straight — and Rhode Island goes on cooking its food, and the reference goes on keeping its record, and the reader goes on to the next entry, satisfied or arguing, and that orderly succession is as it should be A dish like Italian Ice (Rhode Island) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Rhode Island keeps it.
