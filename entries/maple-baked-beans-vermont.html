Maple Baked Beans (Vermont)

Beans baked with maple syrup and salt pork, the state's sweetening of the regional pot. There are dishes a state is famous for and dishes a state actually eats, and Maple Baked Beans (Vermont) is the second kind — which, in Vermont, is the only kind this reference counts.

Vermont sweetens its beans with its woods rather than the cane trade's molasses; maple beans are the sugar country's Saturday bake and the sugar house supper's backbone. This entry's record rests on load-bearing claims standing on two sources, drawn from state records, fair and fishery reports, church and community cookbooks, and the standard regional food histories — and beyond that pair, the state's own historical and agricultural record standing on one side and the documented memory of its cooks, fairs, and fisheries on the other, two different kinds of keeping tested against each other before either one is believed on its own Where the record thins into lore, this entry says so; where the evidence is strong, it rules accordingly; and since the contested dishes of New England require someone finally to rule, the ruling here is made on evidence and not on affection

The syrup cooks down to a deep amber glaze; dry mustard keeps the sweetness honest, and the beans are the little white kinds. The defining traits are the ones Vermont's own cooks police, at the stove and at the table, by correction and by example, whenever a hand drifts from the standard, and they hold because every living dish drifts a little at the edges — a different hand, a different season — but the center holds fast, and it is the center this reference records, so that a reader meeting the dish anywhere from a church supper to a shore stand will know it by these marks and by no others what the entry describes is the center of the dish rather than its whole weather, for a dish at home varies by town and by hand and sometimes by street, yet varies always around the marks given here, the way a tune varies around its melody, and the melody is what the reference records

They live at sugar suppers and bean suppers across the state. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the things that hold across decades — what the dish is, where it comes from, and what defines it — rather than the things that rot by spring — and beyond that point, it is not an artifact behind glass in a museum but a working part of Vermont, as current as tonight, findable by anyone who goes where the entry says it lives, and the record and the dish agree about the address, and the agreement is the point of both

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, Maple Baked Beans (Vermont) shows the method of this whole book: the record is frozen when it is finished, corrected only for outright error, and this entry is written to that standard from its first line to its last — and beyond the method, the book files by state and treats all states alike, refusing fame, fashion, and the editor's appetite as arrangements, and this entry benefits from the refusal exactly as the emblem does, for in this book, unusually, the footnote and the headline weigh the same the record rests there, not because more could not be said, but because a reference earns its trust by stopping where its evidence stops, and the evidence for this entry stops exactly here, complete and checked If Maple Baked Beans (Vermont) vanished from Vermont tomorrow, this entry would become its memorial; as long as it is served, the entry is its census. Either way, the record holds.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Vermont.
