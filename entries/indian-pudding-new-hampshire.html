Indian Pudding (New Hampshire)

Cornmeal and molasses baked slow, the hearth pudding of the colonial farm. Indian Pudding (New Hampshire) is one of the dishes by which New Hampshire knows itself: a food with a birthplace, a season, and a standard that the home cooks enforce without ever writing it down.

New Hampshire's farms kept Indian pudding at the center of the winter table; the long bake suited the wood stove, and the molasses came up the Piscataqua in trade. This entry's record rests on the judgment of two independent witnesses, and the entry is written only as far as they agree and not one claim further — and beyond that pair, the state's own historical and agricultural record standing on one side and the documented memory of its cooks, fairs, and fisheries on the other, two different kinds of keeping tested against each other before either one is believed on its own Where the record thins into lore, this entry says so; where the evidence is strong, it rules accordingly; and since the contested dishes of New England require someone finally to rule, the ruling here is made on evidence and not on affection

It bakes dark and soft, eaten warm with cream; in the North Country it closed the bean supper as often as pie did. The defining traits are the marks by which the genuine article is told from the souvenir, in a state where the genuine article is still common enough to have impostors working its name, and they hold because inside the tolerances the dish is itself and outside them it is a story about the dish, and the entry keeps to the tolerances exactly as the cooks of New Hampshire keep to them nightly the description ends where the dish's tolerances end, deliberately, because beyond them lies the pleasant country of variation, which belongs to the cooks of New Hampshire and not to a frozen record, and the record keeps the center, which is enough for anyone to find the dish again

It lives in the farm record and the bean suppers of the state. For the reader who left New Hampshire and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a list of kitchens rather than addresses, because the reference does not print addresses — home stoves, church basements, shore stands, and fair booths, each holding the dish to the same standard — and beyond that point, there is a kind of map this entry draws without paper — the towns and tables of New Hampshire where the dish belongs, connected by nothing but the dish itself — and repeated across enough towns, the dish becomes a region on the map of the mind, and this entry is one point on that map, fixed so it can be drawn again

Read alongside the other entries of New Hampshire, Indian Pudding (New Hampshire) shows the method of this whole book: a promise about completeness, kept one kept dish at a time, because a region's food is not its dozen famous dishes but its hundreds of kept ones, however quiet they are — and beyond the method, the entry is the region's memory of itself, written by itself and outsourced to print so that forgetting has to work harder to erase it, while the dish, unbothered by its own documentation, goes on being made at home every canon ends up teaching its readers how to read it, and this one teaches a simple lesson through every entry: read for the record, not the rhetoric — a dish, a state, a set of marks, a home ground, with the rhetoric kept on a short leash throughout — so that a reader who finishes knowing the record has read it correctly A dish like Indian Pudding (New Hampshire) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as New Hampshire keeps it.
