Boston Baked Beans

Small white beans baked long and slow with salt pork, molasses, and mustard until dark and glossy. The claim of Boston Baked Beans on Massachusetts is not a marketing claim; it is a census claim, taken at church suppers, diners, and family tables where the dish appears because it has always appeared.

Colonial Boston baked beans on Saturday for the Sabbath, and molasses — the port's great trade good — sweetened the pot; the dish gave the city its Beantown name. This entry's record rests on two independent sources for every claim that carries weight — and beyond that pair, Massachusetts's good records and better cooks, and on those two assets this entry rests, and on nothing else at all The sources are named at the foot of the entry, chosen by the standing rule of print where print exists, testimony where print runs out, and silence where neither can honestly be had

The beans are pea beans or navy beans, the sauce a deep mahogany, the pork melting into the liquor. Boston brown bread is the obligatory partner. The defining traits are the form described here is the one the home kitchens of Massachusetts recognize, allow no substitutes for, and pass down by correction at the stove from one generation's hands to the next, and they hold because variations exist, as they do for every living dish, but the standard is the standard — the version a Massachusetts cook would set in front of family and call by this name without a word of explanation, and the version against which every variation is quietly measured the traits have been argued at more Massachusetts tables than any entry can count, and the argument itself is evidence — nobody argues the marks of a dish nobody cares about — and the description here is the argument's settled residue, what survived the arguing

It lives in the bean pot suppers of churches and granges across the state, and in the memory of every Boston kitchen. For the reader who left Massachusetts and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the dish as it is kept at home, described while home still keeps it, in the kitchens and seasons named above — and beyond that point, picture it where Massachusetts keeps it — in use, on tables, in season, among people who would find the idea of preserving it faintly comic, because from where they sit it is in no danger whatsoever — and then remember that the reference has seen regions forget, and so it writes, and the writing is this entry's whole apology and excuse

Read alongside the other entries of Massachusetts, Boston Baked Beans shows the method of this whole book: a dish is admitted on living identity, documented on two sources, and described at the length its record earns, and not one line further — and beyond the method, the canon's motto holds for this entry as for all of them: nothing kept, nothing lost — for the dish was kept by Massachusetts long before it was written here, and the writing is only the insurance on the keeping, taken out in the region's own name against the one loss a region cannot afford after the filing, the dish returns to being what it was: a working part of Massachusetts, eaten under its own name by people who may never read its entry, and the entry's satisfaction is precisely that it changed nothing about the dish except its chances of being remembered accurately Filed here, Boston Baked Beans is finished being a rumor and becomes a record — the Massachusetts dish as it stands, neither inflated nor apologized for, ready for the reader who goes looking for it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Massachusetts.
