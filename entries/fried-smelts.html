Fried Smelts

Small silvery fish, dredged and fried whole, eaten bones and all. It would be possible to write the food of Maine without Fried Smelts, but the writing would be a lie of omission, and this reference does not omit.

Smelts run the Maine rivers in late winter, and the smelt camps on the ice — whole villages of shacks with rakes and dips — are a Maine institution as much as a fishery. This entry's record rests on the house rule that two sources support every load-bearing claim, or the claim does not appear in the entry at all — and beyond that pair, Maine's good records and better cooks, and on those two assets this entry rests, and on nothing else at all The sources are named at the foot of the entry, chosen by the standing rule of print where print exists, testimony where print runs out, and silence where neither can honestly be had

Fried crisp in cornmeal, a smelt is eaten like a french fry of the river: head off in polite company, everything else down. Lemon and tartar divide the camps. The defining traits are deliberately narrow, because the dish is narrow: one settled way of making it, and a hundred small permissions around the edges of that way, and they hold because variations exist, as they do for every living dish, but the standard is the standard — the version a Maine cook would set in front of family and call by this name without a word of explanation, and the version against which every variation is quietly measured the traits have been argued at more Maine tables than any entry can count, and the argument itself is evidence — nobody argues the marks of a dish nobody cares about — and the description here is the argument's settled residue, what survived the arguing

They live on the tidal rivers — the Kennebec, the Penobscot, and the little rivers of the midcoast. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: Maine's calendar rather than this book's directions, for the dish keeps its season faithfully, and in its season it is not hidden at all — it is simply there, where it has always been, waiting to be recognized rather than discovered — and beyond that point, some entries in this canon describe dishes that live in one town and guard it jealously, but this one lives the length of Maine, and the length of the state is richer for it, for a dish at home everywhere in its state is a dish that has won its argument with geography

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Fried Smelts shows the method of this whole book: returned to its people, because the reference keeps only the description, carefully, and gives the dish back to Maine exactly as it found it, no better and no worse than it was — and beyond the method, nothing in it can rot on the page — no prices to expire within the year, no hours to change with the season, no crowns to defend when the next season comes — because what remains is the durable part, what the dish is and where it was born and what defines it and where in Maine it lives, which is the part the diaspora writes home about the entry is closed the way a fair book is closed — the exhibits entered, the ruling made, the record straight — and Maine goes on cooking its food, and the reference goes on keeping its record, and the reader goes on to the next entry, satisfied or arguing, and that orderly succession is as it should be That is where Fried Smelts leaves the reader — with the dish whole: what it is, where it was born, and the marks by which Maine knows it, kept in one place against the day it is wanted again.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Maine.
