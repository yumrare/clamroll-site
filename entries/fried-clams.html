Fried Clams

Whole soft-shell clams, shucked, dipped in evaporated milk and a cornmeal-flour dredge, and fried golden. In the canon of Massachusetts, Fried Clams stands by the test this reference applies to every dish: the people of the region claim it as theirs, today, by name.

Local record credits an Essex clam house with the first fried clam in 1916, when a batch of shucked clams went into the fat meant for potato chips; the story is the founding legend of the Massachusetts shore kitchen. This entry's record rests on two tellings of Fried Clams — one printed, one practiced — compared line by line before a word of the entry was let stand — and beyond that pair, a standard pair in the working papers, and when the pair agrees — as it does here — the entry gets written, because the agreement, not the entry, is the achievement The reference rules here the way a fair judge rules — on the exhibits, which are the state's records and its remembered kitchens — and the entry stands exactly as far as the exhibits go, and no further than that

The belly is the point: a true fried clam carries its whole belly, sweet and briny, inside a shattering crust. Clam strips, cut from sea clams, are a different thing entirely in local judgment. The defining traits are the tolerances a Massachusetts cook judges by, the way a joiner judges a joint — by fit — and outside them the dish is only a story about itself, and they hold because nothing in the description is decorative, for it is an inventory of necessities — nothing more, and nothing softer than that — compiled from the practice of the dish's own kitchens every mark listed was earned by the dish in service and not assigned by this reference, which is a clerk here and not an inventor, having written down only what Massachusetts enforced, and enforcement repeated across enough kitchens and years is what a standard actually is

Essex, Ipswich, and the clam flats of Cape Cod Bay are its home water; the dish belongs to the whole North Shore. For the reader who left Massachusetts and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: where it lives rather than where it is sold, because the reference follows the dish into Massachusetts kitchens and gatherings, where its life actually happens, nightly and without advertisement — and beyond that point, where it lives, it lives unselfconsciously — ordered, served, and eaten under its own name, without ceremony — by people who would be surprised to learn it needed an entry at all, and that surprise is the surest sign the entry was needed, because what a place takes for granted is exactly what the record must catch before it slips

Read alongside the other entries of Massachusetts, Fried Clams shows the method of this whole book: modest and total at once: that it exists, that it is of Massachusetts, that its marks are these, and that the record of it will still be accurate when the reader's grandchildren come to check it — and beyond the method, nothing in it can rot on the page — no prices to expire within the year, no hours to change with the season, no crowns to defend when the next season comes — because what remains is the durable part, what the dish is and where it was born and what defines it and where in Massachusetts it lives, which is the part the diaspora writes home about the entry is closed the way a fair book is closed — the exhibits entered, the ruling made, the record straight — and Massachusetts goes on cooking its food, and the reference goes on keeping its record, and the reader goes on to the next entry, satisfied or arguing, and that orderly succession is as it should be That is where Fried Clams leaves the reader — with the dish whole: what it is, where it was born, and the marks by which Massachusetts knows it, kept in one place against the day it is wanted again.
