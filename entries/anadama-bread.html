Anadama Bread

A yeast bread of cornmeal and molasses, dense and slightly sweet. In the working vocabulary of Massachusetts food, Anadama Bread is a common noun: everybody knows it, nobody footnotes it, and this entry exists because the rest of the world needs the footnote.

Rockport tradition says a fisherman, cursing a wife named Anna who fed him cornmeal mush and molasses, damned her name into a bread — 'Anna, damn her' — and the name stuck to the loaf. This entry's record rests on the judgment of two independent witnesses, and the entry is written only as far as they agree and not one claim further — and beyond that pair, Massachusetts's good records and better cooks, and on those two assets this entry rests, and on nothing else at all The sources are named at the foot of the entry, chosen by the standing rule of print where print exists, testimony where print runs out, and silence where neither can honestly be had

It rises on a cooked cornmeal porridge, which keeps it moist; the crumb is golden, the crust firm, and it toasts better than almost any bread in the canon. The defining traits are the marks by which the genuine article is told from the souvenir, in a state where the genuine article is still common enough to have impostors working its name, and they hold because a portrait rather than a eulogy, because the dish is alive in Massachusetts today and still made to this standard by living hands, and the standard is checkable at any table where the dish is served under its own name — a check the entry not only allows but invites the marks are the dish's tolerances and its testimony at once: the tolerances say how far it may bend, the testimony says it has not broken, and both are matters of record in Massachusetts rather than matters of opinion anywhere

It lives on Cape Ann first — Rockport and Gloucester — and in bakeries across the North Shore. For the reader who left Massachusetts and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a list of kitchens rather than addresses, because the reference does not print addresses — home stoves, church basements, shore stands, and fair booths, each holding the dish to the same standard — and beyond that point, geography, for such a dish, is destiny with better scenery — it lives in Massachusetts because Massachusetts is the kind of place that produces it, its waters and its winters and its stubbornness about doing things the kept way — and the entry therefore leaves both the dish and its meaning exactly where it found them

Read alongside the other entries of Massachusetts, Anadama Bread shows the method of this whole book: a promise about completeness, kept one kept dish at a time, because a region's food is not its dozen famous dishes but its hundreds of kept ones, however quiet they are — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold Massachusetts at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that Massachusetts food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation A dish like Anadama Bread is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Massachusetts keeps it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Massachusetts.
