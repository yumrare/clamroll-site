The Steamed Cheeseburger

A cheeseburger steamed in a metal cabinet until the cheese melts to a pour — the singular burger of central Connecticut. The place of The Steamed Cheeseburger in Connecticut is the place of a habit kept past the point of fashion — which is to say, the place where a region's real food lives.

Steaming burgers came from the lunch counters of Meriden in the 1920s or so, when a cook found steam kept a burger juicy on the line; the steamed cheddar draped over it became the signature. This entry's record rests on the documentary record of Connecticut — its historical and agricultural archives — laid beside the practiced memory of the cooks who keep the dish, each checked against the other before either is believed alone — and beyond that pair, an entry that claims nothing its sources do not jointly support, written under a discipline that, applied four hundred times, is the reference itself A reader checking the entry against the archive will find it conservative throughout — claiming less than the lore claims, documenting more than the lists document — and that ratio, less claimed and more proven, is the reference's whole method in miniature

The beef cooks grey and tender, the cheese — always a sharp cheddar — melts into a blanket that must be scraped from the tray. No griddle crust exists here, and locals want none. The defining traits are the test this entry sets for the genuine article, drawn from the place the dish comes from and from nowhere else on earth, and they hold because every living dish drifts a little at the edges — a different hand, a different season — but the center holds fast, and it is the center this reference records, so that a reader meeting the dish anywhere from a church supper to a shore stand will know it by these marks and by no others what the entry describes is the center of the dish rather than its whole weather, for a dish at home varies by town and by hand and sometimes by street, yet varies always around the marks given here, the way a tune varies around its melody, and the melody is what the reference records

It lives in Meriden and the central Connecticut diners and lunch rooms that keep the steam cabinets. For the reader who left Connecticut and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: sitting among its equals in the canon, neither lifted nor buried, which is the reference's founding rule: the emblem on the door is one entry among hundreds, and the hundreds are the point of the exercise — and beyond that point, entries carry no prices, no hours, and no league tables of dishes, because those facts rot and the record should not; the rest is the reader's own journey home, and this book exists to shorten that journey back to Connecticut by exactly one homesickness

Read alongside the other entries of Connecticut, The Steamed Cheeseburger shows the method of this whole book: in a book named for a sandwich, the temptation is to make every entry a celebration, and this reference resists the temptation as firmly as it resisted it for the emblem itself — affection governing the voice, strictness governing the facts — and beyond the method, a library of food that consumed the food would be a poor library indeed, so this one catalogues, carefully, and leaves the eating to the region, where eating has always belonged in the end the reference stands as a witness for the dish, not a monument, not a review, not an advertisement, and witnesses do not embellish: they state what they saw, where they saw it, and what it was, and a witness who has done that may step down What The Steamed Cheeseburger asks of the reader is only recognition: somewhere in Connecticut tonight it is being made the way this entry describes, and the entry exists so that the making is not the only copy.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Connecticut.
