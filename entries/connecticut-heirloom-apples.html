Connecticut Heirloom Apples

The old varieties of the state's colonial orchards — russets, greenings, and the cider apples of the farm presses. Before Connecticut had dishes it had Connecticut Heirloom Apples: the raw fact of the place, waiting in the water, the field, or the woods for a kitchen to be built around it.

Connecticut planted apples with its first farms; its heirloom trees still stand along the stone walls, and the cider makers prize the bitter-sharp old kinds above all modern eating apples. This entry's record rests on the contents of the reference's own working file: one source that can be shelved in a library, and one that can be tasted at a table — and beyond that pair, documentation that supports exactly this much and no more, for Connecticut documented this one thoroughly, in ink and in practice, and the entry is sized to that evidence Sources are cited so the next person can check the line, and the line here runs through the published record of Connecticut and through its practiced kitchens, and both sides of it were walked, and both of them held

A russet keeps till March; a bittersweet makes a cider with a spine. The old names are a map of the state's first orchards. The defining traits are each one the part that, removed, would make a Connecticut cook say without hesitation that it is no longer the thing itself, and they hold because nothing in the description is decorative, for it is an inventory of necessities — nothing more, and nothing softer than that — compiled from the practice of the dish's own kitchens every mark listed was earned by the dish in service and not assigned by this reference, which is a clerk here and not an inventor, having written down only what Connecticut enforced, and enforcement repeated across enough kitchens and years is what a standard actually is

They live in the old orchards of the whole state. For the reader who left Connecticut and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a landscape and a season rather than a door number — find the landscape, in the season, and the dish is there waiting, as it has been every year within memory — and beyond that point, the homesick reader will read the home ground the way earlier generations read letters from home — slowly, and more than once — and the entry was written to bear that kind of reading, naming the ground plainly and refusing to embroider the naming, because plain naming, repeated kindly, is the whole comfort on offer here

Read alongside the other entries of Connecticut, Connecticut Heirloom Apples shows the method of this whole book: filed level with the emblem itself, because no entry is the point and every entry is, the clam roll on the door being one sandwich and the four hundred filings behind it being the reference proper — and beyond the method, the canon's motto holds for this entry as for all of them: nothing kept, nothing lost — for the dish was kept by Connecticut long before it was written here, and the writing is only the insurance on the keeping, taken out in the region's own name against the one loss a region cannot afford after the filing, the dish returns to being what it was: a working part of Connecticut, eaten under its own name by people who may never read its entry, and the entry's satisfaction is precisely that it changed nothing about the dish except its chances of being remembered accurately What Connecticut Heirloom Apples asks of the reader is only attention: the next Connecticut dish that tastes unmistakably of its place is probably tasting of this, and now the reader knows its name and its ground.
