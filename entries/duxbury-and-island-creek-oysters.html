Duxbury and Island Creek Oysters

Oysters farmed in Duxbury Bay, cold and clean, among the most sought-after in the state. Salt air, soil, and season made Duxbury and Island Creek Oysters what it is, and Massachusetts made its peace with all three by building a cuisine on top of them.

Duxbury's oyster culture grew from seed planted in the bay in the 1800s; the modern farms of Duxbury Bay revived the town's name in raw bars across the country. This entry's record rests on the judgment of two independent witnesses, and the entry is written only as far as they agree and not one claim further — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

They are small, firm, and sharply briny, with a sweet finish — the product of cold water and tidal flats that drain twice a day. The defining traits are the marks by which the genuine article is told from the souvenir, in a state where the genuine article is still common enough to have impostors working its name, and they hold because impostors fail in predictable places — the shortcut, the substitution, the garnish that belongs to another state entirely — and the traits above are written so the reader can catch the failure at first sight, as any cook at home catches it at first taste nothing in the description flatters the dish, for flattery rots in print even faster than prices do, and what stands here instead is the working test a home kitchen applies — plain, exact, and not especially interested in outsiders' feelings about it

Duxbury Bay, on the South Shore, is their home water.
