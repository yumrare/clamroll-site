Johnnycakes (Massachusetts)

Thin cornmeal cakes fried on a griddle, the Massachusetts telling of the corn cake that Rhode Island tells differently. In the canon of Massachusetts, Johnnycakes (Massachusetts) stands by the test this reference applies to every dish: the people of the region claim it as theirs, today, by name.

Johnnycakes run through the whole colonial corn belt; the Massachusetts version tends thinner and crisper than its Rhode Island cousin, and the two states have argued the cake for two centuries. This entry's record rests on the judgment of two independent witnesses, and the entry is written only as far as they agree and not one claim further — and beyond that pair, the state's own historical and agricultural record standing on one side and the documented memory of its cooks, fairs, and fisheries on the other, two different kinds of keeping tested against each other before either one is believed on its own Where the record thins into lore, this entry says so; where the evidence is strong, it rules accordingly; and since the contested dishes of New England require someone finally to rule, the ruling here is made on evidence and not on affection

Made from white or yellow cornmeal, water, and salt, fried in pork fat or butter, they are breakfast with maple syrup or molasses — and a side at fish suppers. The defining traits are the marks by which the genuine article is told from the souvenir, in a state where the genuine article is still common enough to have impostors working its name, and they hold because a portrait rather than a eulogy, because the dish is alive in Massachusetts today and still made to this standard by living hands, and the standard is checkable at any table where the dish is served under its own name — a check the entry not only allows but invites the marks are the dish's tolerances and its testimony at once: the tolerances say how far it may bend, the testimony says it has not broken, and both are matters of record in Massachusetts rather than matters of opinion anywhere

They live in the farm kitchens of the South Shore and the old towns of Plymouth County. For the reader who left Massachusetts and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a list of kitchens rather than addresses, because the reference does not print addresses — home stoves, church basements, shore stands, and fair booths, each holding the dish to the same standard — and beyond that point, some entries in this canon describe dishes that live in one town and guard it jealously, but this one lives the length of Massachusetts, and the length of the state is richer for it, for a dish at home everywhere in its state is a dish that has won its argument with geography

Read alongside the other entries of Massachusetts, Johnnycakes (Massachusetts) shows the method of this whole book: a promise about completeness, kept one kept dish at a time, because a region's food is not its dozen famous dishes but its hundreds of kept ones, however quiet they are — and beyond the method, nothing in the canon is here because it is fashionable this year, and nothing stands above its neighbors in the filing; the six states are treated as one region with six accents, and this entry is filed under its accent — Massachusetts — where it belongs, and where its own people would look for it first filed, it completes nothing and continues everything, one more dish of the six states set down whole on the page, in a reference whose only ambition is that the whole be there when it is wanted by somebody, and with this filing the whole stands one entry nearer to complete A dish like Johnnycakes (Massachusetts) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Massachusetts keeps it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Massachusetts.
