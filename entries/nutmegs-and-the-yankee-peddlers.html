Nutmegs and the Yankee Peddlers

The spice trade and tall tale that named Connecticut the Nutmeg State. The food at Nutmegs and the Yankee Peddlers matters, but the gathering is the dish: Connecticut cooks it communally, serves it communally, and remembers it communally.

Connecticut's peddlers sold the young nation's spices door to door; the legend that they carved wooden nutmegs to cheat the frontier is likely slander, but the state's honest nutmeg trade was real enough to earn the name. This entry's record rests on the archive of Connecticut on one side and the living kitchens of Connecticut on the other, with the entry written in the narrow ground where the two agree — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

The nutmeg itself flavored the region's custards, puddings, and eggnogs — grated fresh, it is the colonial pantry's perfume. The defining traits are stated flatly, because at home they are not in dispute: ask in Connecticut and the answer returns with the confidence of long practice, and flat statement, in a reference, is a form of respect, and they hold because every living dish drifts a little at the edges — a different hand, a different season — but the center holds fast, and it is the center this reference records, so that a reader meeting the dish anywhere from a church supper to a shore stand will know it by these marks and by no others what the entry describes is the center of the dish rather than its whole weather, for a dish at home varies by town and by hand and sometimes by street, yet varies always around the marks given here, the way a tune varies around its melody, and the melody is what the reference records

It lives in the state's nickname and every custard in this canon. For the reader who left Connecticut and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: home ground where the record is thickest, though the dish respects no fence and no town line, living wherever in Connecticut its conditions are met — and its conditions are the state's own weather, water, and habit — and beyond that point, the homesick reader will read the home ground the way earlier generations read letters from home — slowly, and more than once — and the entry was written to bear that kind of reading, naming the ground plainly and refusing to embroider the naming, because plain naming, repeated kindly, is the whole comfort on offer here

Read alongside the other entries of Connecticut, Nutmegs and the Yankee Peddlers shows the method of this whole book: written for the distance — across an ocean, across a generation — by a reference that believes distance is where trust in a record is actually tested, and wrote accordingly — and beyond the method, there is a discipline to stopping, and this entry stops here — definition, origin, traits, and home ground, documented and done — and what a reader does with the entry afterward, the trip, the table, the argument settled at last between cousins, is the reader's own affair the writing is insurance and nothing grander — a record taken out against forgetting, in the region's own name, by a reference that has watched other regions lose dishes they swore they would remember forever What Nutmegs and the Yankee Peddlers asks of the reader is only respect for the repetition: the same table, the same season, the same food, again — which in Connecticut is not a failure of imagination but the definition of home.

Sources: state historical society records; community, church, and fair records of Connecticut.
