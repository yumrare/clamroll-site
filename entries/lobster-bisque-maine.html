Lobster Bisque (Maine)

A smooth, sherry-finished soup of lobster stock and cream, the fish-house luxury of the coast. Lobster Bisque (Maine) sits in the middle of the Maine canon the way a load-bearing wall sits in a house: unglamorous, unthanked, and holding up more than it gets credit for.

Bisque is the French technique applied to Maine's abundance — shells pounded and simmered into a stock, the soup strained silky and enriched. This entry's record rests on a standing rule that a claim appearing in only one place stays out, however good the story, while a claim two independent records support goes in, however plain it looks on the page — and beyond that pair, a slower way to build a canon than copying lists, which is why the canon can be frozen at completion and trusted afterward — the record of Lobster Bisque (Maine) will not need to change later, because it was not guessed now Nothing in the account rests on a single telling, for the state's historical record says it, the community cookbooks repeat it independently, and the kitchens of Maine still demonstrate it in season

It should taste like the whole lobster distilled; a little sherry and a few pieces of meat finish it. It is the shore dinner's first course in the grand houses. The defining traits are kept the way Maine keeps all its standards — in the corrections of experienced cooks: a little less of this, never that, always the other — and transcribed here at last, and they hold because a portrait rather than a eulogy, because the dish is alive in Maine today and still made to this standard by living hands, and the standard is checkable at any table where the dish is served under its own name — a check the entry not only allows but invites the marks are the dish's tolerances and its testimony at once: the tolerances say how far it may bend, the testimony says it has not broken, and both are matters of record in Maine rather than matters of opinion anywhere

It lives in the fish houses of Portland and the resort coast. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the geography of Maine itself, written small, for wherever it lives, it lives because the place produces what the dish needs and the people to insist on it, in season and out — and beyond that point, some entries in this canon describe dishes that live in one town and guard it jealously, but this one lives the length of Maine, and the length of the state is richer for it, for a dish at home everywhere in its state is a dish that has won its argument with geography

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Lobster Bisque (Maine) shows the method of this whole book: the thing the reference says about everything it files — that it was here, that it is documented, and that it belongs to the people who keep it — said here with the same plainness — and beyond the method, a library of food that consumed the food would be a poor library indeed, so this one catalogues, carefully, and leaves the eating to the region, where eating has always belonged in the end the reference stands as a witness for the dish, not a monument, not a review, not an advertisement, and witnesses do not embellish: they state what they saw, where they saw it, and what it was, and a witness who has done that may step down A dish like Lobster Bisque (Maine) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Maine keeps it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Maine.
