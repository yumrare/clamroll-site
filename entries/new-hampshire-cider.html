New Hampshire Cider

Fresh and fermented cider from the state's orchard country, pressed each fall. In the pantry of New Hampshire, New Hampshire Cider is not an option but a premise, the thing the cooking assumes the way a sentence assumes its grammar.

New Hampshire's farms pressed cider from the first plantings; the mill and the farm-stand jug are both fixtures of its autumn, and hard cider was the farm's bankable crop for two centuries. This entry's record rests on the judgment of two independent witnesses, and the entry is written only as far as they agree and not one claim further — and beyond that pair, a slower way to build a canon than copying lists, which is why the canon can be frozen at completion and trusted afterward — the record of New Hampshire Cider will not need to change later, because it was not guessed now Nothing in the account rests on a single telling, for the state's historical record says it, the community cookbooks repeat it independently, and the kitchens of New Hampshire still demonstrate it in season

Sweet cider is cloudy, sharp, and brief — it begins to ferment almost at once — while the aged farmhouse cider ran dry and strong. The defining traits are the marks by which the genuine article is told from the souvenir, in a state where the genuine article is still common enough to have impostors working its name, and they hold because nothing in the description is decorative, for it is an inventory of necessities — nothing more, and nothing softer than that — compiled from the practice of the dish's own kitchens every mark listed was earned by the dish in service and not assigned by this reference, which is a clerk here and not an inventor, having written down only what New Hampshire enforced, and enforcement repeated across enough kitchens and years is what a standard actually is

It lives in the orchard towns of the Merrimack and Connecticut valleys. For the reader who left New Hampshire and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a list of kitchens rather than addresses, because the reference does not print addresses — home stoves, church basements, shore stands, and fair booths, each holding the dish to the same standard — and beyond that point, there is a kind of map this entry draws without paper — the towns and tables of New Hampshire where the dish belongs, connected by nothing but the dish itself — and repeated across enough towns, the dish becomes a region on the map of the mind, and this entry is one point on that map, fixed so it can be drawn again

Read alongside the other entries of New Hampshire, New Hampshire Cider shows the method of this whole book: a promise about completeness, kept one kept dish at a time, because a region's food is not its dozen famous dishes but its hundreds of kept ones, however quiet they are — and beyond the method, the canon's motto holds for this entry as for all of them: nothing kept, nothing lost — for the dish was kept by New Hampshire long before it was written here, and the writing is only the insurance on the keeping, taken out in the region's own name against the one loss a region cannot afford after the filing, the dish returns to being what it was: a working part of New Hampshire, eaten under its own name by people who may never read its entry, and the entry's satisfaction is precisely that it changed nothing about the dish except its chances of being remembered accurately What New Hampshire Cider asks of the reader is only attention: the next New Hampshire dish that tastes unmistakably of its place is probably tasting of this, and now the reader knows its name and its ground.
