Salmon and Peas on the Fourth

The Independence Day dinner of the season's first salmon and first garden peas. Dishes can travel; traditions cannot, and Salmon and Peas on the Fourth has never traveled — it exists only where Maine gathers, which is exactly why the reference records it.

For generations the Fourth in Maine was not a cookout but a sit-down dinner: the first Penobscot salmon, the first peas from the garden, new potatoes, and egg sauce. This entry's record rests on the house rule that two sources support every load-bearing claim, or the claim does not appear in the entry at all — and beyond that pair, a slower way to build a canon than copying lists, which is why the canon can be frozen at completion and trusted afterward — the record of Salmon and Peas on the Fourth will not need to change later, because it was not guessed now Nothing in the account rests on a single telling, for the state's historical record says it, the community cookbooks repeat it independently, and the kitchens of Maine still demonstrate it in season

The pairing is a calendar made edible — river and garden arriving together at midsummer — and old Maine families still set the plate when they can. The defining traits are deliberately narrow, because the dish is narrow: one settled way of making it, and a hundred small permissions around the edges of that way, and they hold because every living dish drifts a little at the edges — a different hand, a different season — but the center holds fast, and it is the center this reference records, so that a reader meeting the dish anywhere from a church supper to a shore stand will know it by these marks and by no others what the entry describes is the center of the dish rather than its whole weather, for a dish at home varies by town and by hand and sometimes by street, yet varies always around the marks given here, the way a tune varies around its melody, and the melody is what the reference records

It lives in the memory and the keeping of river towns along the Penobscot and the coast. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: Maine's calendar rather than this book's directions, for the dish keeps its season faithfully, and in its season it is not hidden at all — it is simply there, where it has always been, waiting to be recognized rather than discovered — and beyond that point, the homesick reader will read the home ground the way earlier generations read letters from home — slowly, and more than once — and the entry was written to bear that kind of reading, naming the ground plainly and refusing to embroider the naming, because plain naming, repeated kindly, is the whole comfort on offer here

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Salmon and Peas on the Fourth shows the method of this whole book: returned to its people, because the reference keeps only the description, carefully, and gives the dish back to Maine exactly as it found it, no better and no worse than it was — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold Maine at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that Maine food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation The entry cannot hold the noise of Salmon and Peas on the Fourth — the hall, the kitchen steam, the arguments about last year — but it holds the pattern, and the pattern, kept, is what lets the noise happen again next year in Maine.

Sources: state historical society records; community, church, and fair records of Maine.
