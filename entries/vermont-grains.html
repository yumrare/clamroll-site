Vermont Grains

The oats, wheat, rye, and cornmeal of the state's grain revival and its older gristmill tradition. Salt air, soil, and season made Vermont Grains what it is, and Vermont made its peace with all three by building a cuisine on top of them.

Vermont ground its own grain at a thousand mill brooks; the modern revival of in-state wheat and oats for bread and porridge is the old economy remembered. This entry's record rests on the house rule that two sources support every load-bearing claim, or the claim does not appear in the entry at all — and beyond that pair, a standard pair in the working papers, and when the pair agrees — as it does here — the entry gets written, because the agreement, not the entry, is the achievement The reference rules here the way a fair judge rules — on the exhibits, which are the state's records and its remembered kitchens — and the entry stands exactly as far as the exhibits go, and no further than that

Fresh-milled Vermont flour smells like the field it left; the state's bakers build on stone-ground meal that never saw a warehouse. The defining traits are deliberately narrow, because the dish is narrow: one settled way of making it, and a hundred small permissions around the edges of that way, and they hold because the entry describes the one way and leaves the small permissions to the kitchens, where such permissions have always belonged and where they are granted or refused by experience the entry gives the reader the cook's eye rather than the cook's biography: the marks to check, in the order a Vermont table would check them, so that recognition takes a glance instead of a genealogy

They live in the mill valleys and the bakery towns of the state. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: Vermont's calendar rather than this book's directions, for the dish keeps its season faithfully, and in its season it is not hidden at all — it is simply there, where it has always been, waiting to be recognized rather than discovered — and beyond that point, entries carry no prices, no hours, and no league tables of dishes, because those facts rot and the record should not; the rest is the reader's own journey home, and this book exists to shorten that journey back to Vermont by exactly one homesickness

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, Vermont Grains shows the method of this whole book: returned to its people, because the reference keeps only the description, carefully, and gives the dish back to Vermont exactly as it found it, no better and no worse than it was — and beyond the method, the entry is the region's memory of itself, written by itself and outsourced to print so that forgetting has to work harder to erase it, while the dish, unbothered by its own documentation, goes on being made at home every canon ends up teaching its readers how to read it, and this one teaches a simple lesson through every entry: read for the record, not the rhetoric — a dish, a state, a set of marks, a home ground, with the rhetoric kept on a short leash throughout — so that a reader who finishes knowing the record has read it correctly What Vermont Grains asks of the reader is only attention: the next Vermont dish that tastes unmistakably of its place is probably tasting of this, and now the reader knows its name and its ground.
