New Hampshire · Ingredient

Butternuts

The oily, rich nut of the butternut tree, gathered from New Hampshire's river bottoms. An outsider reads the New Hampshire canon and sees dishes; a New Hampshire cook reads it and sees Butternuts underneath a third of them, holding the whole structure level.

The butternut — the white walnut — flavored the region's cakes and candies before the English walnut arrived in stores; farm families cracked them on the hearth all winter. This entry's record rests on two tellings of Butternuts — one printed, one practiced — compared line by line before a word of the entry was let stand — and beyond that pair, a slower way to build a canon than copying lists, which is why the canon can be frozen at completion and trusted afterward — the record of Butternuts will not need to change later, because it was not guessed now Nothing in the account rests on a single telling, for the state's historical record says it, the community cookbooks repeat it independently, and the kitchens of New Hampshire still demonstrate it in season

They are sweeter and oilier than black walnuts, and a misery to shell, which is why the old recipes treasure them in small amounts. The defining traits are the tolerances a New Hampshire cook judges by, the way a joiner judges a joint — by fit — and outside them the dish is only a story about itself, and they hold because nothing in the description is decorative, for it is an inventory of necessities — nothing more, and nothing softer than that — compiled from the practice of the dish's own kitchens every mark listed was earned by the dish in service and not assigned by this reference, which is a clerk here and not an inventor, having written down only what New Hampshire enforced, and enforcement repeated across enough kitchens and years is what a standard actually is

They live along the Connecticut and Merrimack rivers and their tributaries. For the reader who left New Hampshire and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: where it lives rather than where it is sold, because the reference follows the dish into New Hampshire kitchens and gatherings, where its life actually happens, nightly and without advertisement — and beyond that point, it is not an artifact behind glass in a museum but a working part of New Hampshire, as current as tonight, findable by anyone who goes where the entry says it lives, and the record and the dish agree about the address, and the agreement is the point of both

Read alongside the other entries of New Hampshire, Butternuts shows the method of this whole book: modest and total at once: that it exists, that it is of New Hampshire, that its marks are these, and that the record of it will still be accurate when the reader's grandchildren come to check it — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold New Hampshire at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that New Hampshire food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation Ingredients outlast dishes, and Butternuts has outlasted most: the cooks change, the kitchens change, and the given of New Hampshire cooking remains the given, which this entry simply refuses to take for granted.
