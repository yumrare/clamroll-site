Wabanaki Flint Corn

The hard, colorful flint corns grown by the Wabanaki peoples of Maine for centuries, ground for meal and hulled for soup. Salt air, soil, and season made Wabanaki Flint Corn what it is, and Maine made its peace with all three by building a cuisine on top of them.

Corn came to Maine's rivers with Wabanaki agriculture long before contact; the flint varieties suited the short season, and ash-hulled corn soup remains a living Penobscot and Passamaquoddy food. This entry's record rests on the house rule that two sources support every load-bearing claim, or the claim does not appear in the entry at all — and beyond that pair, a plain pair of facts: the dish earns its place not by being famous but by being claimed, and Wabanaki Flint Corn is claimed in Maine without hesitation or irony Maine deserves a canon that knows the difference between print, testimony, and silence, and this entry observes that difference strictly from its first line to its last

Flint corn is dense and sweet when ground, its kernels glassy in reds, blues, and golds; hulled with wood ash, it becomes a hominy unlike any other.
