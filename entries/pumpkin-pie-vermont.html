Pumpkin Pie (Vermont)

The spiced pumpkin custard pie of the Vermont harvest table. In the working vocabulary of Vermont food, Pumpkin Pie (Vermont) is a common noun: everybody knows it, nobody footnotes it, and this entry exists because the rest of the world needs the footnote.

Vermont keeps pumpkin pie beside its squash pie without confusion: the pumpkin is the festival fruit, the pie its ceremony, and the cheddar rule applies to it as to apple. This entry's record rests on load-bearing claims standing on two sources, drawn from state records, fair and fishery reports, church and community cookbooks, and the standard regional food histories — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

It is smooth and warm-spiced; the state's telling leans darker on the molasses than its neighbors'. The defining traits are the ones Vermont's own cooks police, at the stove and at the table, by correction and by example, whenever a hand drifts from the standard, and they hold because every living dish drifts a little at the edges — a different hand, a different season — but the center holds fast, and it is the center this reference records, so that a reader meeting the dish anywhere from a church supper to a shore stand will know it by these marks and by no others what the entry describes is the center of the dish rather than its whole weather, for a dish at home varies by town and by hand and sometimes by street, yet varies always around the marks given here, the way a tune varies around its melody, and the melody is what the reference records

It lives at Thanksgiving and harvest tables statewide. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the things that hold across decades — what the dish is, where it comes from, and what defines it — rather than the things that rot by spring — and beyond that point, entries carry no prices, no hours, and no league tables of dishes, because those facts rot and the record should not; the rest is the reader's own journey home, and this book exists to shorten that journey back to Vermont by exactly one homesickness

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, Pumpkin Pie (Vermont) shows the method of this whole book: the record is frozen when it is finished, corrected only for outright error, and this entry is written to that standard from its first line to its last — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold Vermont at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that Vermont food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation A dish like Pumpkin Pie (Vermont) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Vermont keeps it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Vermont.
