Aroostook Potatoes

The potatoes of Aroostook County, the great northern county that made Maine a potato state. Foundations are not famous, and Aroostook Potatoes is not famous; it is something better in the economy of a cuisine — indispensable, the way flour is indispensable, present in the background of a hundred Maine triumphs.

Aroostook's soil and short summer grow a potato with dense, dry flesh; the harvest once closed the schools, and the county's barrels and later its trucks fed the East. This entry's record rests on load-bearing claims standing on two sources, drawn from state records, fair and fishery reports, church and community cookbooks, and the standard regional food histories — and beyond that pair, documentation that supports exactly this much and no more, for Maine documented this one thoroughly, in ink and in practice, and the entry is sized to that evidence Sources are cited so the next person can check the line, and the line here runs through the published record of Maine and through its practiced kitchens, and both sides of it were walked, and both of them held

Russets for baking, rounds for the pot — the Aroostook potato is floury, earthy, and keeping; it goes into needhams, donuts, and every County supper. The defining traits are the ones Maine's own cooks police, at the stove and at the table, by correction and by example, whenever a hand drifts from the standard, and they hold because variations exist, as they do for every living dish, but the standard is the standard — the version a Maine cook would set in front of family and call by this name without a word of explanation, and the version against which every variation is quietly measured the traits have been argued at more Maine tables than any entry can count, and the argument itself is evidence — nobody argues the marks of a dish nobody cares about — and the description here is the argument's settled residue, what survived the arguing

The County — Aroostook — is their home ground. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the things that hold across decades — what the dish is, where it comes from, and what defines it — rather than the things that rot by spring — and beyond that point, picture it where Maine keeps it — in use, on tables, in season, among people who would find the idea of preserving it faintly comic, because from where they sit it is in no danger whatsoever — and then remember that the reference has seen regions forget, and so it writes, and the writing is this entry's whole apology and excuse

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Aroostook Potatoes shows the method of this whole book: the record is frozen when it is finished, corrected only for outright error, and this entry is written to that standard from its first line to its last — and beyond the method, a library of food that consumed the food would be a poor library indeed, so this one catalogues, carefully, and leaves the eating to the region, where eating has always belonged in the end the reference stands as a witness for the dish, not a monument, not a review, not an advertisement, and witnesses do not embellish: they state what they saw, where they saw it, and what it was, and a witness who has done that may step down If the dishes of Maine are the region's sentences, Aroostook Potatoes is one of its oldest words — and the reference, which exists to keep the language, keeps the word with its meaning attached.

Sources: state agricultural and fishery records; regional food histories of Maine.
