New Hampshire · Dish

Switchel

A cold drink of water, molasses or maple, ginger, and vinegar — the haymaker's refreshment. Ask in New Hampshire what belongs on the table and Switchel will be named — not as a novelty, but as a habit of the place, kept by the people who live there.

Switchel was the fuel of the hayfield: sweet, sharp, and sustaining, carried to the mowing in a jug and drunk by the gallon through the hottest work of the year. This entry's record rests on two independent sources for every claim that carries weight — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

It tastes like ginger beer crossed with lemonade's stern ancestor; the vinegar is not a flaw but the point — it cuts the dust. The defining traits are the form described here is the one the home kitchens of New Hampshire recognize, allow no substitutes for, and pass down by correction at the stove from one generation's hands to the next, and they hold because imitations elsewhere borrow the name and miss the substance described here, and the homesick look for this entry first because it describes the thing itself and never the souvenir of it a description can fail a dish by praising it, and this one refuses the risk, giving Switchel its marks plainly, the way a lighthouse is given its coordinates — because readers need the dish located, not celebrated, and located exactly

It lives in the record of every hay farm in the state, and in revival kitchens each summer. For the reader who left New Hampshire and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the dish as it is kept at home, described while home still keeps it, in the kitchens and seasons named above — and beyond that point, that is the purpose of the whole reference — the canon set down whole, cited, and frozen, so that the food of the six states can be checked against the record rather than against nostalgia alone
