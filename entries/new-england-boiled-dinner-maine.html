New England Boiled Dinner (Maine)

Corned beef with cabbage, potatoes, carrots, turnip, and beets from one pot. In the canon of Maine, New England Boiled Dinner (Maine) stands by the test this reference applies to every dish: the people of the region claim it as theirs, today, by name.

The boiled dinner is Maine's Sunday architecture — the beef brisket or 'grey' corned beef of the old markets, the roots from the cellar, the whole thing timed to church. This entry's record rests on the house rule that two sources support every load-bearing claim, or the claim does not appear in the entry at all — and beyond that pair, a standard pair in the working papers, and when the pair agrees — as it does here — the entry gets written, because the agreement, not the entry, is the achievement The reference rules here the way a fair judge rules — on the exhibits, which are the state's records and its remembered kitchens — and the entry stands exactly as far as the exhibits go, and no further than that

Everything is served in its slices with mustard and vinegar on the table; the pot liquor is saved for tomorrow's pea soup or hash. The defining traits are deliberately narrow, because the dish is narrow: one settled way of making it, and a hundred small permissions around the edges of that way, and they hold because inside the tolerances the dish is itself and outside them it is a story about the dish, and the entry keeps to the tolerances exactly as the cooks of Maine keep to them nightly the description ends where the dish's tolerances end, deliberately, because beyond them lies the pleasant country of variation, which belongs to the cooks of Maine and not to a frozen record, and the record keeps the center, which is enough for anyone to find the dish again

It lives in farm and town kitchens the length of Maine. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: Maine's calendar rather than this book's directions, for the dish keeps its season faithfully, and in its season it is not hidden at all — it is simply there, where it has always been, waiting to be recognized rather than discovered — and beyond that point, that is the purpose of the whole reference — the canon set down whole, cited, and frozen, so that the food of the six states can be checked against the record rather than against nostalgia alone

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, New England Boiled Dinner (Maine) shows the method of this whole book: returned to its people, because the reference keeps only the description, carefully, and gives the dish back to Maine exactly as it found it, no better and no worse than it was — and beyond the method, the reference set out to write down the whole working food of a region, in full, and this entry is one of the filings the promise required — not a landmark, necessarily, but a kept thing, and kept things are the canon's true population nothing here pretends that writing the dish down was the important part, for the important part happened in Maine kitchens over centuries, without any help from print, and the entry is the minutes of that meeting — and minutes matter, because they are how institutions remember, but the meeting is the dish itself A dish like New England Boiled Dinner (Maine) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Maine keeps it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Maine.
