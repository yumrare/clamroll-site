Fish and Chips (Connecticut)

Batter-fried Sound fish with potatoes, the Friday plate of the shore towns. A visitor could eat through Connecticut for a month and miss Fish and Chips (Connecticut) entirely, which says nothing about the dish and everything about the visitor; the locals missed nothing.

The shore's fish houses fry the day's haddock, cod, or flounder; Friday in a Connecticut port still smells of the fryer by long custom. This entry's record rests on the archive of Connecticut on one side and the living kitchens of Connecticut on the other, with the entry written in the narrow ground where the two agree — and beyond that pair, the state's own historical and agricultural record standing on one side and the documented memory of its cooks, fairs, and fisheries on the other, two different kinds of keeping tested against each other before either one is believed on its own Where the record thins into lore, this entry says so; where the evidence is strong, it rules accordingly; and since the contested dishes of New England require someone finally to rule, the ruling here is made on evidence and not on affection

The batter crisps thin; the fish is the Sound's; vinegar and tartar divide the counter. The defining traits are stated flatly, because at home they are not in dispute: ask in Connecticut and the answer returns with the confidence of long practice, and flat statement, in a reference, is a form of respect, and they hold because the standard is written in no cookbook, and the traits above are the kitchen corrections themselves, transcribed at last into print for readers who were not raised within earshot of them to describe the dish loosely would be to lose it, because the dish is its details, and Connecticut cooks hold those details the way singers hold pitch — not by thinking about them, but by wincing when they are wrong — and the traits above are the wince, transcribed

It lives in the shore towns from Bridgeport to Stonington. For the reader who left Connecticut and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: home ground where the record is thickest, though the dish respects no fence and no town line, living wherever in Connecticut its conditions are met — and its conditions are the state's own weather, water, and habit — and beyond that point, geography, for such a dish, is destiny with better scenery — it lives in Connecticut because Connecticut is the kind of place that produces it, its waters and its winters and its stubbornness about doing things the kept way — and the entry therefore leaves both the dish and its meaning exactly where it found them

Read alongside the other entries of Connecticut, Fish and Chips (Connecticut) shows the method of this whole book: written for the distance — across an ocean, across a generation — by a reference that believes distance is where trust in a record is actually tested, and wrote accordingly — and beyond the method, the canon's motto holds for this entry as for all of them: nothing kept, nothing lost — for the dish was kept by Connecticut long before it was written here, and the writing is only the insurance on the keeping, taken out in the region's own name against the one loss a region cannot afford after the filing, the dish returns to being what it was: a working part of Connecticut, eaten under its own name by people who may never read its entry, and the entry's satisfaction is precisely that it changed nothing about the dish except its chances of being remembered accurately A dish like Fish and Chips (Connecticut) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Connecticut keeps it.
