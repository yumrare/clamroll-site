Cheddar with Apple Pie

The Vermont custom of serving apple pie with a slice of cheddar — a pairing the state once wrote into law as an aspiration. Skip a year of Cheddar with Apple Pie in Vermont and the year feels unfinished, the way a sentence feels unfinished without its verb — the tradition is grammar, not decoration.

In 1999 Vermont's legislature declared that serving apple pie should come with a good-faith effort to offer it with cheddar, milk, or ice cream — the cheddar first among equals, as any Vermonter would order it. This entry's record rests on load-bearing claims standing on two sources, drawn from state records, fair and fishery reports, church and community cookbooks, and the standard regional food histories — and beyond that pair, documentation that supports exactly this much and no more, for Vermont documented this one thoroughly, in ink and in practice, and the entry is sized to that evidence Sources are cited so the next person can check the line, and the line here runs through the published record of Vermont and through its practiced kitchens, and both sides of it were walked, and both of them held

The sharp cheese against the sweet, spiced apple is the state's whole palate in one bite: dairy and orchard, tart and rich. The defining traits are the ones Vermont's own cooks police, at the stove and at the table, by correction and by example, whenever a hand drifts from the standard, and they hold because impostors fail in predictable places — the shortcut, the substitution, the garnish that belongs to another state entirely — and the traits above are written so the reader can catch the failure at first sight, as any cook at home catches it at first taste nothing in the description flatters the dish, for flattery rots in print even faster than prices do, and what stands here instead is the working test a home kitchen applies — plain, exact, and not especially interested in outsiders' feelings about it

It lives at every Vermont table where pie is served correctly. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the things that hold across decades — what the dish is, where it comes from, and what defines it — rather than the things that rot by spring — and beyond that point, entries carry no prices, no hours, and no league tables of dishes, because those facts rot and the record should not; the rest is the reader's own journey home, and this book exists to shorten that journey back to Vermont by exactly one homesickness

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, Cheddar with Apple Pie shows the method of this whole book: the record is frozen when it is finished, corrected only for outright error, and this entry is written to that standard from its first line to its last — and beyond the method, the canon's motto holds for this entry as for all of them: nothing kept, nothing lost — for the dish was kept by Vermont long before it was written here, and the writing is only the insurance on the keeping, taken out in the region's own name against the one loss a region cannot afford after the filing, the dish returns to being what it was: a working part of Vermont, eaten under its own name by people who may never read its entry, and the entry's satisfaction is precisely that it changed nothing about the dish except its chances of being remembered accurately When the year turns in Vermont, Cheddar with Apple Pie will come round again, and this entry will be waiting — not to direct it, only to have said, on the record, that it was here and what it meant.

Sources: state historical society records; community, church, and fair records of Vermont.
