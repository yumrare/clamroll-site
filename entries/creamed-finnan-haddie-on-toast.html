Creamed Finnan Haddie on Toast

Poached smoked haddock in a cream sauce, ladled over toast. No dish enters the Massachusetts canon on charm alone, and Creamed Finnan Haddie on Toast did not: it is here because kitchens across the state still make it, name it, and correct anyone who makes it wrong.

Creamed finnan haddie was the hotel breakfast and the Lenten supper of old Boston — the smoked fish stretched with sauce into a dish of quiet luxury. This entry's record rests on two independent sources for every claim that carries weight — and beyond that pair, a standard pair in the working papers, and when the pair agrees — as it does here — the entry gets written, because the agreement, not the entry, is the achievement The reference rules here the way a fair judge rules — on the exhibits, which are the state's records and its remembered kitchens — and the entry stands exactly as far as the exhibits go, and no further than that

The sauce is a plain white sauce enriched with the poaching milk; the toast must be crisp enough to stand under it. The defining traits are the form described here is the one the home kitchens of Massachusetts recognize, allow no substitutes for, and pass down by correction at the stove from one generation's hands to the next, and they hold because impostors fail in predictable places — the shortcut, the substitution, the garnish that belongs to another state entirely — and the traits above are written so the reader can catch the failure at first sight, as any cook at home catches it at first taste nothing in the description flatters the dish, for flattery rots in print even faster than prices do, and what stands here instead is the working test a home kitchen applies — plain, exact, and not especially interested in outsiders' feelings about it

It lives in Boston's breakfast record and the fish-market towns of the North Shore. For the reader who left Massachusetts and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the dish as it is kept at home, described while home still keeps it, in the kitchens and seasons named above — and beyond that point, that is the purpose of the whole reference — the canon set down whole, cited, and frozen, so that the food of the six states can be checked against the record rather than against nostalgia alone

Read alongside the other entries of Massachusetts, Creamed Finnan Haddie on Toast shows the method of this whole book: a dish is admitted on living identity, documented on two sources, and described at the length its record earns, and not one line further — and beyond the method, the canon's motto holds for this entry as for all of them: nothing kept, nothing lost — for the dish was kept by Massachusetts long before it was written here, and the writing is only the insurance on the keeping, taken out in the region's own name against the one loss a region cannot afford after the filing, the dish returns to being what it was: a working part of Massachusetts, eaten under its own name by people who may never read its entry, and the entry's satisfaction is precisely that it changed nothing about the dish except its chances of being remembered accurately A dish like Creamed Finnan Haddie on Toast is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Massachusetts keeps it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Massachusetts.
