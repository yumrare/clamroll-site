The Wild Blueberry Harvest

The August raking of the barrens, when hand rakes comb the low bushes and the barrens turn blue-black with fruit. The young people of Maine are handed The Wild Blueberry Harvest the way they are handed tools — not explained, simply included, until the hands know.

The harvest still runs on the hand rake, a tool unchanged in a century, and whole communities — including Mi'kmaq and migrant crews — have worked the barrens season for generations. This entry's record rests on a standing rule that a claim appearing in only one place stays out, however good the story, while a claim two independent records support goes in, however plain it looks on the page — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

For a few weeks the barrens are a working sea of people and crates; the harvest ends in pie, and the year turns on it. The defining traits are kept the way Maine keeps all its standards — in the corrections of experienced cooks: a little less of this, never that, always the other — and transcribed here at last, and they hold because variations exist, as they do for every living dish, but the standard is the standard — the version a Maine cook would set in front of family and call by this name without a word of explanation, and the version against which every variation is quietly measured the traits have been argued at more Maine tables than any entry can count, and the argument itself is evidence — nobody argues the marks of a dish nobody cares about — and the description here is the argument's settled residue, what survived the arguing

It lives on the barrens of Washington County, around Cherryfield, the blueberry capital of the state's claim. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the geography of Maine itself, written small, for wherever it lives, it lives because the place produces what the dish needs and the people to insist on it, in season and out — and beyond that point, the homesick reader will read the home ground the way earlier generations read letters from home — slowly, and more than once — and the entry was written to bear that kind of reading, naming the ground plainly and refusing to embroider the naming, because plain naming, repeated kindly, is the whole comfort on offer here

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, The Wild Blueberry Harvest shows the method of this whole book: the thing the reference says about everything it files — that it was here, that it is documented, and that it belongs to the people who keep it — said here with the same plainness — and beyond the method, the book files by state and treats all states alike, refusing fame, fashion, and the editor's appetite as arrangements, and this entry benefits from the refusal exactly as the emblem does, for in this book, unusually, the footnote and the headline weigh the same the record rests there, not because more could not be said, but because a reference earns its trust by stopping where its evidence stops, and the evidence for this entry stops exactly here, complete and checked A tradition like The Wild Blueberry Harvest is the region remembering itself out loud, and the reference keeps it for the same reason Maine keeps it: because a table that gathers is a place that continues.

Sources: state historical society records; community, church, and fair records of Maine.
