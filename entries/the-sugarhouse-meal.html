Vermont · Tradition

The Sugarhouse Meal

The working meal of the sugar camp — beans, pickles, eggs, and everything maple — eaten between firings. The dishes of Vermont do not float free; they are moored to gatherings, and The Sugarhouse Meal is one of the moorings — a tradition that keeps a whole table in its place.

The sugarhouse runs day and night in season, and its meals are built for the crew: maple beans, fried eggs, pickles against the sweetness in the air, and syrup on all of it. This entry's record rests on the house rule that two sources support every load-bearing claim, or the claim does not appear in the entry at all — and beyond that pair, the state's own historical and agricultural record standing on one side and the documented memory of its cooks, fairs, and fisheries on the other, two different kinds of keeping tested against each other before either one is believed on its own Where the record thins into lore, this entry says so; where the evidence is strong, it rules accordingly; and since the contested dishes of New England require someone finally to rule, the ruling here is made on evidence and not on affection

Sugar on snow closes the meal when visitors come; the crew itself eats standing, watching the evaporator, in the steam. The defining traits are deliberately narrow, because the dish is narrow: one settled way of making it, and a hundred small permissions around the edges of that way, and they hold because impostors fail in predictable places — the shortcut, the substitution, the garnish that belongs to another state entirely — and the traits above are written so the reader can catch the failure at first sight, as any cook at home catches it at first taste nothing in the description flatters the dish, for flattery rots in print even faster than prices do, and what stands here instead is the working test a home kitchen applies — plain, exact, and not especially interested in outsiders' feelings about it

It lives in the sugar houses of the whole state during the run. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: Vermont's calendar rather than this book's directions, for the dish keeps its season faithfully, and in its season it is not hidden at all — it is simply there, where it has always been, waiting to be recognized rather than discovered — and beyond that point, that is the purpose of the whole reference — the canon set down whole, cited, and frozen, so that the food of the six states can be checked against the record rather than against nostalgia alone

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, The Sugarhouse Meal shows the method of this whole book: returned to its people, because the reference keeps only the description, carefully, and gives the dish back to Vermont exactly as it found it, no better and no worse than it was — and beyond the method, there is a discipline to stopping, and this entry stops here — definition, origin, traits, and home ground, documented and done — and what a reader does with the entry afterward, the trip, the table, the argument settled at last between cousins, is the reader's own affair the writing is insurance and nothing grander — a record taken out against forgetting, in the region's own name, by a reference that has watched other regions lose dishes they swore they would remember forever What The Sugarhouse Meal asks of the reader is only respect for the repetition: the same table, the same season, the same food, again — which in Vermont is not a failure of imagination but the definition of home.

Sources: state historical society records; community, church, and fair records of Vermont.
