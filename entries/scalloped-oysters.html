Scalloped Oysters

Oysters layered with buttered crumbs and cream, baked in a scallop dish for holidays. What makes Scalloped Oysters a Massachusetts dish is not that it was invented there — though the record says it was — but that it never left: the state kept making it after the rest of the country moved on.

Scalloped oysters were a 19th-century celebration dish, when oysters were the cheapest luxury in Boston and every holiday table carried them in some form. This entry's record rests on the judgment of two independent witnesses, and the entry is written only as far as they agree and not one claim further — and beyond that pair, a claim-by-claim sizing, because entries in this canon grow or shrink with their documentation and never with the enthusiasm of their editors If a claim about Scalloped Oysters could not be found twice it is not in this entry, however often it is repeated at tables, because repetition is not corroboration and the reference has always known the difference

The layers bake into a rich pudding, the oysters just set; it is a dish of the era when oysters outnumbered turkeys at Thanksgiving in the city. The defining traits are the marks by which the genuine article is told from the souvenir, in a state where the genuine article is still common enough to have impostors working its name, and they hold because the entry describes the one way and leaves the small permissions to the kitchens, where such permissions have always belonged and where they are granted or refused by experience the entry gives the reader the cook's eye rather than the cook's biography: the marks to check, in the order a Massachusetts table would check them, so that recognition takes a glance instead of a genealogy

It lives in the holiday record of Boston and the oyster towns. For the reader who left Massachusetts and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a list of kitchens rather than addresses, because the reference does not print addresses — home stoves, church basements, shore stands, and fair booths, each holding the dish to the same standard — and beyond that point, there is a kind of map this entry draws without paper — the towns and tables of Massachusetts where the dish belongs, connected by nothing but the dish itself — and repeated across enough towns, the dish becomes a region on the map of the mind, and this entry is one point on that map, fixed so it can be drawn again

Read alongside the other entries of Massachusetts, Scalloped Oysters shows the method of this whole book: a promise about completeness, kept one kept dish at a time, because a region's food is not its dozen famous dishes but its hundreds of kept ones, however quiet they are — and beyond the method, the canon's motto holds for this entry as for all of them: nothing kept, nothing lost — for the dish was kept by Massachusetts long before it was written here, and the writing is only the insurance on the keeping, taken out in the region's own name against the one loss a region cannot afford after the filing, the dish returns to being what it was: a working part of Massachusetts, eaten under its own name by people who may never read its entry, and the entry's satisfaction is precisely that it changed nothing about the dish except its chances of being remembered accurately A dish like Scalloped Oysters is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Massachusetts keeps it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Massachusetts.
