Cape Cod Cranberries

The small, tart red berry of the Cape's bogs, harvested each fall by flooding the vines and floating the fruit. In Massachusetts, Cape Cod Cranberries is less an ingredient than a fact of the landscape — the given that the cooks of four centuries have worked with and around.

Commercial cranberry culture began on Cape Cod in the early 1800s, when Dennis growers learned to sand and flood the bogs; the Cape and the South Shore became the heart of the American cranberry country. This entry's record rests on two tellings of Cape Cod Cranberries — one printed, one practiced — compared line by line before a word of the entry was let stand — and beyond that pair, a standard pair in the working papers, and when the pair agrees — as it does here — the entry gets written, because the agreement, not the entry, is the achievement The reference rules here the way a fair judge rules — on the exhibits, which are the state's records and its remembered kitchens — and the entry stands exactly as far as the exhibits go, and no further than that

Fresh cranberries are fiercely tart and keep for weeks; the harvest turns the bogs red in October, and the berry anchors the Thanksgiving table as sauce, relish, and juice. The defining traits are the tolerances a Massachusetts cook judges by, the way a joiner judges a joint — by fit — and outside them the dish is only a story about itself, and they hold because a portrait rather than a eulogy, because the dish is alive in Massachusetts today and still made to this standard by living hands, and the standard is checkable at any table where the dish is served under its own name — a check the entry not only allows but invites the marks are the dish's tolerances and its testimony at once: the tolerances say how far it may bend, the testimony says it has not broken, and both are matters of record in Massachusetts rather than matters of opinion anywhere

The bogs of Barnstable, Plymouth, and Bristol counties are its home ground.
