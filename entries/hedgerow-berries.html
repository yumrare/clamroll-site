Hedgerow Berries

The wild blackberries and raspberries of the field edges, gathered in July and August. In the canon of New Hampshire, Hedgerow Berries stands by the test this reference applies to every dish: the people of the region claim it as theirs, today, by name.

Every New Hampshire farm has its berry edge — the stone wall and the fence line where the brambles fruit free for the picking, at the price of scratches. This entry's record rests on two tellings of Hedgerow Berries — one printed, one practiced — compared line by line before a word of the entry was let stand — and beyond that pair, the state's own historical and agricultural record standing on one side and the documented memory of its cooks, fairs, and fisheries on the other, two different kinds of keeping tested against each other before either one is believed on its own Where the record thins into lore, this entry says so; where the evidence is strong, it rules accordingly; and since the contested dishes of New England require someone finally to rule, the ruling here is made on evidence and not on affection

They go to pie, jam, and the mouth in that order of honesty; a hedgerow pie is the taste of the farm's wild margin. The defining traits are the tolerances a New Hampshire cook judges by, the way a joiner judges a joint — by fit — and outside them the dish is only a story about itself, and they hold because a portrait rather than a eulogy, because the dish is alive in New Hampshire today and still made to this standard by living hands, and the standard is checkable at any table where the dish is served under its own name — a check the entry not only allows but invites the marks are the dish's tolerances and its testimony at once: the tolerances say how far it may bend, the testimony says it has not broken, and both are matters of record in New Hampshire rather than matters of opinion anywhere

They live along every stone wall in the state. For the reader who left New Hampshire and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: where it lives rather than where it is sold, because the reference follows the dish into New Hampshire kitchens and gatherings, where its life actually happens, nightly and without advertisement — and beyond that point, that is the purpose of the whole reference — the canon set down whole, cited, and frozen, so that the food of the six states can be checked against the record rather than against nostalgia alone

Read alongside the other entries of New Hampshire, Hedgerow Berries shows the method of this whole book: modest and total at once: that it exists, that it is of New Hampshire, that its marks are these, and that the record of it will still be accurate when the reader's grandchildren come to check it — and beyond the method, nothing in the canon is here because it is fashionable this year, and nothing stands above its neighbors in the filing; the six states are treated as one region with six accents, and this entry is filed under its accent — New Hampshire — where it belongs, and where its own people would look for it first filed, it completes nothing and continues everything, one more dish of the six states set down whole on the page, in a reference whose only ambition is that the whole be there when it is wanted by somebody, and with this filing the whole stands one entry nearer to complete A dish like Hedgerow Berries is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as New Hampshire keeps it.
