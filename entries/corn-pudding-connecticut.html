Corn Pudding (Connecticut)

A baked pudding of fresh corn, milk, and eggs — the valley harvest's soft dish. The claim of Corn Pudding (Connecticut) on Connecticut is not a marketing claim; it is a census claim, taken at church suppers, diners, and family tables where the dish appears because it has always appeared.

Corn pudding is the fresh corn's supper form: kernels cut from the cob, set in a custard, and baked for the harvest table while the corn is at its peak. This entry's record rests on the contents of the reference's own working file: one source that can be shelved in a library, and one that can be tasted at a table — and beyond that pair, documentation that supports exactly this much and no more, for Connecticut documented this one thoroughly, in ink and in practice, and the entry is sized to that evidence Sources are cited so the next person can check the line, and the line here runs through the published record of Connecticut and through its practiced kitchens, and both sides of it were walked, and both of them held

It is soft, sweet, and golden; the valley serves it beside the ham and the beans in August's brief glut. The defining traits are each one the part that, removed, would make a Connecticut cook say without hesitation that it is no longer the thing itself, and they hold because the entry describes the one way and leaves the small permissions to the kitchens, where such permissions have always belonged and where they are granted or refused by experience the entry gives the reader the cook's eye rather than the cook's biography: the marks to check, in the order a Connecticut table would check them, so that recognition takes a glance instead of a genealogy

It lives in the Connecticut River valley's farm kitchens, at harvest suppers from the Massachusetts line to the Sound. For the reader who left Connecticut and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a landscape and a season rather than a door number — find the landscape, in the season, and the dish is there waiting, as it has been every year within memory — and beyond that point, the homesick reader will read the home ground the way earlier generations read letters from home — slowly, and more than once — and the entry was written to bear that kind of reading, naming the ground plainly and refusing to embroider the naming, because plain naming, repeated kindly, is the whole comfort on offer here
