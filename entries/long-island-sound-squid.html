Long Island Sound Squid

The longfin squid of the Sound and the offshore grounds, landed at Stonington. Whole stretches of the Connecticut table — soups, suppers, shore dinners — are unthinkable without Long Island Sound Squid, which is the plainest definition of a foundation ingredient.

Squid is the Sound's silver harvest — jigged and trawled in season, landed at Stonington for the calamari of three states' shore tables. This entry's record rests on the documentary record of Connecticut — its historical and agricultural archives — laid beside the practiced memory of the cooks who keep the dish, each checked against the other before either is believed alone — and beyond that pair, a slower way to build a canon than copying lists, which is why the canon can be frozen at completion and trusted afterward — the record of Long Island Sound Squid will not need to change later, because it was not guessed now Nothing in the account rests on a single telling, for the state's historical record says it, the community cookbooks repeat it independently, and the kitchens of Connecticut still demonstrate it in season

Fresh Sound squid is sweet and tender beyond the frozen article's dreams; fried Rhode Island style or grilled at the shore, it is the fleet's summer pride. The defining traits are the test this entry sets for the genuine article, drawn from the place the dish comes from and from nowhere else on earth, and they hold because variations exist, as they do for every living dish, but the standard is the standard — the version a Connecticut cook would set in front of family and call by this name without a word of explanation, and the version against which every variation is quietly measured the traits have been argued at more Connecticut tables than any entry can count, and the argument itself is evidence — nobody argues the marks of a dish nobody cares about — and the description here is the argument's settled residue, what survived the arguing

Its home is the Sound and the grounds south of it.
