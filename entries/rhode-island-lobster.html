Rhode Island Lobster

The lobster of Rhode Island Sound and the offshore canyons, landed at Galilee and Newport. If the canon has bedrock, Rhode Island Lobster is part of it: the ingredient that was here before the recipes and will be here after them.

Rhode Island's lobstermen work the sound and the offshore grounds; the state's catch rides in the traps with its Jonah crabs, and its shore eats lobster with a small state's proprietary pride. This entry's record rests on the archive of Rhode Island on one side and the living kitchens of Rhode Island on the other, with the entry written in the narrow ground where the two agree — and beyond that pair, a standard pair in the working papers, and when the pair agrees — as it does here — the entry gets written, because the agreement, not the entry, is the achievement The reference rules here the way a fair judge rules — on the exhibits, which are the state's records and its remembered kitchens — and the entry stands exactly as far as the exhibits go, and no further than that

It is the same sweet cold-water lobster as Maine's, by the same cold law; the state's tellings — rolls, bakes, and shore dinners — are its own. The defining traits are stated flatly, because at home they are not in dispute: ask in Rhode Island and the answer returns with the confidence of long practice, and flat statement, in a reference, is a form of respect, and they hold because the standard is written in no cookbook, and the traits above are the kitchen corrections themselves, transcribed at last into print for readers who were not raised within earshot of them to describe the dish loosely would be to lose it, because the dish is its details, and Rhode Island cooks hold those details the way singers hold pitch — not by thinking about them, but by wincing when they are wrong — and the traits above are the wince, transcribed

Its home is Rhode Island Sound, landed at Point Judith and Newport. For the reader who left Rhode Island and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: home ground where the record is thickest, though the dish respects no fence and no town line, living wherever in Rhode Island its conditions are met — and its conditions are the state's own weather, water, and habit — and beyond that point, that is the purpose of the whole reference — the canon set down whole, cited, and frozen, so that the food of the six states can be checked against the record rather than against nostalgia alone

Read alongside the other entries of Rhode Island, Rhode Island Lobster shows the method of this whole book: written for the distance — across an ocean, across a generation — by a reference that believes distance is where trust in a record is actually tested, and wrote accordingly — and beyond the method, nothing in it can rot on the page — no prices to expire within the year, no hours to change with the season, no crowns to defend when the next season comes — because what remains is the durable part, what the dish is and where it was born and what defines it and where in Rhode Island it lives, which is the part the diaspora writes home about the entry is closed the way a fair book is closed — the exhibits entered, the ruling made, the record straight — and Rhode Island goes on cooking its food, and the reference goes on keeping its record, and the reader goes on to the next entry, satisfied or arguing, and that orderly succession is as it should be Nothing in the Rhode Island canon stands alone, and Rhode Island Lobster least of all: it is the entry other entries quietly cite, the shared premise of a hundred dishes, recorded here so the premise is never lost.

Sources: state agricultural and fishery records; regional food histories of Rhode Island.
