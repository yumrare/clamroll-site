Vermont Fish Chowder

A milk chowder of lake or river fish, served with split common crackers. Vermont Fish Chowder is filed under Vermont because that is where its people look for it, and where a stranger asking for it by name would be understood without a word of explanation.

Vermont's chowder is an inland telling — Champlain fish or trout-country catch in the salt-pork and milk frame, with the state's common crackers split and toasted on top. This entry's record rests on load-bearing claims standing on two sources, drawn from state records, fair and fishery reports, church and community cookbooks, and the standard regional food histories — and beyond that pair, Vermont's good records and better cooks, and on those two assets this entry rests, and on nothing else at all The sources are named at the foot of the entry, chosen by the standing rule of print where print exists, testimony where print runs out, and silence where neither can honestly be had

The crackers are half the dish: split, buttered, crisped, and floated, they soften into the chowder like dumplings. The defining traits are the ones Vermont's own cooks police, at the stove and at the table, by correction and by example, whenever a hand drifts from the standard, and they hold because nothing in the description is decorative, for it is an inventory of necessities — nothing more, and nothing softer than that — compiled from the practice of the dish's own kitchens every mark listed was earned by the dish in service and not assigned by this reference, which is a clerk here and not an inventor, having written down only what Vermont enforced, and enforcement repeated across enough kitchens and years is what a standard actually is

It lives on the Champlain shore and in the lake towns. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the things that hold across decades — what the dish is, where it comes from, and what defines it — rather than the things that rot by spring — and beyond that point, it is not an artifact behind glass in a museum but a working part of Vermont, as current as tonight, findable by anyone who goes where the entry says it lives, and the record and the dish agree about the address, and the agreement is the point of both

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, Vermont Fish Chowder shows the method of this whole book: the record is frozen when it is finished, corrected only for outright error, and this entry is written to that standard from its first line to its last — and beyond the method, the entry is the region's memory of itself, written by itself and outsourced to print so that forgetting has to work harder to erase it, while the dish, unbothered by its own documentation, goes on being made at home every canon ends up teaching its readers how to read it, and this one teaches a simple lesson through every entry: read for the record, not the rhetoric — a dish, a state, a set of marks, a home ground, with the rhetoric kept on a short leash throughout — so that a reader who finishes knowing the record has read it correctly A dish like Vermont Fish Chowder is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Vermont keeps it.
