Rhode Island White Flint Corn

The white cap flint corn of the state — the johnnycake corn, stone-ground by Rhode Island mills for three centuries. It is not an exaggeration to call Rhode Island White Flint Corn infrastructure: roads move goods, but this is what Rhode Island actually runs on.

This is the canon's most argued-over grain: Rhode Island's johnnycake makers insist on the state's white flint, grown in its fields and ground on its stones, and the mills' survival is a state project. This entry's record rests on the contents of the reference's own working file: one source that can be shelved in a library, and one that can be tasted at a table — and beyond that pair, the state's own historical and agricultural record standing on one side and the documented memory of its cooks, fairs, and fisheries on the other, two different kinds of keeping tested against each other before either one is believed on its own Where the record thins into lore, this entry says so; where the evidence is strong, it rules accordingly; and since the contested dishes of New England require someone finally to rule, the ruling here is made on evidence and not on affection

The meal is fine, white, and sweet; a cornmeal of any other origin makes, by the state's doctrine, a different and lesser cake. The defining traits are each one the part that, removed, would make a Rhode Island cook say without hesitation that it is no longer the thing itself, and they hold because every living dish drifts a little at the edges — a different hand, a different season — but the center holds fast, and it is the center this reference records, so that a reader meeting the dish anywhere from a church supper to a shore stand will know it by these marks and by no others what the entry describes is the center of the dish rather than its whole weather, for a dish at home varies by town and by hand and sometimes by street, yet varies always around the marks given here, the way a tune varies around its melody, and the melody is what the reference records

It lives in South County and wherever the state's mills still turn. For the reader who left Rhode Island and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a landscape and a season rather than a door number — find the landscape, in the season, and the dish is there waiting, as it has been every year within memory — and beyond that point, the homesick reader will read the home ground the way earlier generations read letters from home — slowly, and more than once — and the entry was written to bear that kind of reading, naming the ground plainly and refusing to embroider the naming, because plain naming, repeated kindly, is the whole comfort on offer here

Read alongside the other entries of Rhode Island, Rhode Island White Flint Corn shows the method of this whole book: filed level with the emblem itself, because no entry is the point and every entry is, the clam roll on the door being one sandwich and the four hundred filings behind it being the reference proper — and beyond the method, nothing in the canon is here because it is fashionable this year, and nothing stands above its neighbors in the filing; the six states are treated as one region with six accents, and this entry is filed under its accent — Rhode Island — where it belongs, and where its own people would look for it first filed, it completes nothing and continues everything, one more dish of the six states set down whole on the page, in a reference whose only ambition is that the whole be there when it is wanted by somebody, and with this filing the whole stands one entry nearer to complete What Rhode Island White Flint Corn asks of the reader is only attention: the next Rhode Island dish that tastes unmistakably of its place is probably tasting of this, and now the reader knows its name and its ground.

Sources: state agricultural and fishery records; regional food histories of Rhode Island.
