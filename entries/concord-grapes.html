Concord Grapes

The blue-black slip-skin grape developed in Concord, the parent of American grape juice and a thousand backyard arbors. The record of Concord Grapes in Massachusetts is older than the state itself: Indigenous hands worked it first, and every kitchen since has been an annotation.

Ephraim Bull bred the Concord in the 1840s and 1850s from native fox grapes, seeking a grape that would ripen in New England; it won prizes and spread across the country. This entry's record rests on the judgment of two independent witnesses, and the entry is written only as far as they agree and not one claim further — and beyond that pair, a standard pair in the working papers, and when the pair agrees — as it does here — the entry gets written, because the agreement, not the entry, is the achievement The reference rules here the way a fair judge rules — on the exhibits, which are the state's records and its remembered kitchens — and the entry stands exactly as far as the exhibits go, and no further than that

The skin slips from the pulp; the flavor is musky, sweet, and unmistakable — the taste of grape jelly itself. The whole harvest perfume of a September arbor is in it. The defining traits are the marks by which the genuine article is told from the souvenir, in a state where the genuine article is still common enough to have impostors working its name, and they hold because variations exist, as they do for every living dish, but the standard is the standard — the version a Massachusetts cook would set in front of family and call by this name without a word of explanation, and the version against which every variation is quietly measured the traits have been argued at more Massachusetts tables than any entry can count, and the argument itself is evidence — nobody argues the marks of a dish nobody cares about — and the description here is the argument's settled residue, what survived the arguing

Concord is its birthplace; it grows on arbors and farm fences across the commonwealth. For the reader who left Massachusetts and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a list of kitchens rather than addresses, because the reference does not print addresses — home stoves, church basements, shore stands, and fair booths, each holding the dish to the same standard — and beyond that point, some entries in this canon describe dishes that live in one town and guard it jealously, but this one lives the length of Massachusetts, and the length of the state is richer for it, for a dish at home everywhere in its state is a dish that has won its argument with geography

Read alongside the other entries of Massachusetts, Concord Grapes shows the method of this whole book: a promise about completeness, kept one kept dish at a time, because a region's food is not its dozen famous dishes but its hundreds of kept ones, however quiet they are — and beyond the method, nothing in it can rot on the page — no prices to expire within the year, no hours to change with the season, no crowns to defend when the next season comes — because what remains is the durable part, what the dish is and where it was born and what defines it and where in Massachusetts it lives, which is the part the diaspora writes home about the entry is closed the way a fair book is closed — the exhibits entered, the ruling made, the record straight — and Massachusetts goes on cooking its food, and the reference goes on keeping its record, and the reader goes on to the next entry, satisfied or arguing, and that orderly succession is as it should be An ingredient's fame is measured in other dishes' names, and by that measure Concord Grapes is among the most famous things in Massachusetts — famous everywhere except on the label, which this entry finally supplies.
