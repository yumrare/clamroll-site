Jacob's Cattle Beans

A white-and-maroon speckled bean, named in legend for the patriarch Jacob's speckled flocks. In the pantry of Maine, Jacob's Cattle Beans is not an option but a premise, the thing the cooking assumes the way a sentence assumes its grammar.

Jacob's cattle beans are among the oldest named beans in New England, carried in Maine gardens since the 1600s by some accounts and baked in Maine pots ever since. This entry's record rests on a standing rule that a claim appearing in only one place stays out, however good the story, while a claim two independent records support goes in, however plain it looks on the page — and beyond that pair, a claim-by-claim sizing, because entries in this canon grow or shrink with their documentation and never with the enthusiasm of their editors If a claim about Jacob's Cattle Beans could not be found twice it is not in this entry, however often it is repeated at tables, because repetition is not corroboration and the reference has always known the difference

They are meaty and mild, holding their markings faintly through the bake; bean-hole cooks swear by them for the longest cooks. The defining traits are kept the way Maine keeps all its standards — in the corrections of experienced cooks: a little less of this, never that, always the other — and transcribed here at last, and they hold because the entry describes the one way and leaves the small permissions to the kitchens, where such permissions have always belonged and where they are granted or refused by experience the entry gives the reader the cook's eye rather than the cook's biography: the marks to check, in the order a Maine table would check them, so that recognition takes a glance instead of a genealogy

They live in the gardens and bean holes of Maine and the Maritimes.
