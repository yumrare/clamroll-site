Narragansett Bay Oysters

Oysters of the bay — in the 1800s, among the most famous in America, shipped as far as San Francisco. Salt air, soil, and season made Narragansett Bay Oysters what it is, and Rhode Island made its peace with all three by building a cuisine on top of them.

The bay's oyster grounds were leased and planted through the 1800s until the bay was a garden of beds; pollution closed the old fishery, and the modern farms work the same waters anew. This entry's record rests on the documentary record of Rhode Island — its historical and agricultural archives — laid beside the practiced memory of the cooks who keep the dish, each checked against the other before either is believed alone — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

The bay's farmers now grow them again on the old grounds; a bay oyster is briny, clean, and quick — a small, cold-water oyster of real distinction. The defining traits are the test this entry sets for the genuine article, drawn from the place the dish comes from and from nowhere else on earth, and they hold because nothing in the description is decorative, for it is an inventory of necessities — nothing more, and nothing softer than that — compiled from the practice of the dish's own kitchens every mark listed was earned by the dish in service and not assigned by this reference, which is a clerk here and not an inventor, having written down only what Rhode Island enforced, and enforcement repeated across enough kitchens and years is what a standard actually is

They live in Narragansett Bay, from Providence to the Sakonnet. For the reader who left Rhode Island and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: sitting among its equals in the canon, neither lifted nor buried, which is the reference's founding rule: the emblem on the door is one entry among hundreds, and the hundreds are the point of the exercise — and beyond that point, geography, for such a dish, is destiny with better scenery — it lives in Rhode Island because Rhode Island is the kind of place that produces it, its waters and its winters and its stubbornness about doing things the kept way — and the entry therefore leaves both the dish and its meaning exactly where it found them

Read alongside the other entries of Rhode Island, Narragansett Bay Oysters shows the method of this whole book: in a book named for a sandwich, the temptation is to make every entry a celebration, and this reference resists the temptation as firmly as it resisted it for the emblem itself — affection governing the voice, strictness governing the facts — and beyond the method, nothing in it can rot on the page — no prices to expire within the year, no hours to change with the season, no crowns to defend when the next season comes — because what remains is the durable part, what the dish is and where it was born and what defines it and where in Rhode Island it lives, which is the part the diaspora writes home about the entry is closed the way a fair book is closed — the exhibits entered, the ruling made, the record straight — and Rhode Island goes on cooking its food, and the reference goes on keeping its record, and the reader goes on to the next entry, satisfied or arguing, and that orderly succession is as it should be If the dishes of Rhode Island are the region's sentences, Narragansett Bay Oysters is one of its oldest words — and the reference, which exists to keep the language, keeps the word with its meaning attached.
