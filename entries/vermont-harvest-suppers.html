Vermont Harvest Suppers

The fall church and town suppers of the harvest — turkey, squash, and pie in the meeting hall. Dishes can travel; traditions cannot, and Vermont Harvest Suppers has never traveled — it exists only where Vermont gathers, which is exactly why the reference records it.

Vermont's harvest supper is the twin of New Hampshire's turkey supper, with the state's own accents: maple in the squash, cheddar by the pie, and the whole town in the hall. This entry's record rests on load-bearing claims standing on two sources, drawn from state records, fair and fishery reports, church and community cookbooks, and the standard regional food histories — and beyond that pair, documentation that supports exactly this much and no more, for Vermont documented this one thoroughly, in ink and in practice, and the entry is sized to that evidence Sources are cited so the next person can check the line, and the line here runs through the published record of Vermont and through its practiced kitchens, and both sides of it were walked, and both of them held

The supper funds the church roof and closes the farm year; the pie table is judged silently and thoroughly by everyone present. The defining traits are the ones Vermont's own cooks police, at the stove and at the table, by correction and by example, whenever a hand drifts from the standard, and they hold because inside the tolerances the dish is itself and outside them it is a story about the dish, and the entry keeps to the tolerances exactly as the cooks of Vermont keep to them nightly the description ends where the dish's tolerances end, deliberately, because beyond them lies the pleasant country of variation, which belongs to the cooks of Vermont and not to a frozen record, and the record keeps the center, which is enough for anyone to find the dish again

They live in the small towns of the whole state each October. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the things that hold across decades — what the dish is, where it comes from, and what defines it — rather than the things that rot by spring — and beyond that point, entries carry no prices, no hours, and no league tables of dishes, because those facts rot and the record should not; the rest is the reader's own journey home, and this book exists to shorten that journey back to Vermont by exactly one homesickness

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, Vermont Harvest Suppers shows the method of this whole book: the record is frozen when it is finished, corrected only for outright error, and this entry is written to that standard from its first line to its last — and beyond the method, the book files by state and treats all states alike, refusing fame, fashion, and the editor's appetite as arrangements, and this entry benefits from the refusal exactly as the emblem does, for in this book, unusually, the footnote and the headline weigh the same the record rests there, not because more could not be said, but because a reference earns its trust by stopping where its evidence stops, and the evidence for this entry stops exactly here, complete and checked A tradition is the only entry in this canon that its subjects could write themselves, and in a sense the people of Vermont did: the reference only transcribed what their attendance has been saying for generations.

Sources: state historical society records; community, church, and fair records of Vermont.
