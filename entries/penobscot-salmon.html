Penobscot Salmon

The Atlantic salmon of the Penobscot River, once so abundant it was Maine's spring currency. Whole stretches of the Maine table — soups, suppers, shore dinners — are unthinkable without Penobscot Salmon, which is the plainest definition of a foundation ingredient.

The Penobscot ran with salmon for ten thousand years of Wabanaki life; the commercial fishery made the first salmon of spring a ceremonial gift, sent to governors and presidents. This entry's record rests on load-bearing claims standing on two sources, drawn from state records, fair and fishery reports, church and community cookbooks, and the standard regional food histories — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

A fresh Penobscot fish with the season's first peas was the state's Fourth of July plate — salmon and peas — a tradition this canon keeps as the river's restoration continues. The defining traits are the ones Maine's own cooks police, at the stove and at the table, by correction and by example, whenever a hand drifts from the standard, and they hold because the entry describes the one way and leaves the small permissions to the kitchens, where such permissions have always belonged and where they are granted or refused by experience the entry gives the reader the cook's eye rather than the cook's biography: the marks to check, in the order a Maine table would check them, so that recognition takes a glance instead of a genealogy

Its home is the Penobscot, from the bay to the forks. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the things that hold across decades — what the dish is, where it comes from, and what defines it — rather than the things that rot by spring — and beyond that point, where it lives, it lives unselfconsciously — ordered, served, and eaten under its own name, without ceremony — by people who would be surprised to learn it needed an entry at all, and that surprise is the surest sign the entry was needed, because what a place takes for granted is exactly what the record must catch before it slips

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Penobscot Salmon shows the method of this whole book: the record is frozen when it is finished, corrected only for outright error, and this entry is written to that standard from its first line to its last — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold Maine at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that Maine food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation Ingredients outlast dishes, and Penobscot Salmon has outlasted most: the cooks change, the kitchens change, and the given of Maine cooking remains the given, which this entry simply refuses to take for granted.

Sources: state agricultural and fishery records; regional food histories of Maine.
