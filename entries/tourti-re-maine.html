Tourtière (Maine)

A double-crust meat pie of pork, potato, and warm spice, the Franco-American Christmas pie. In the working vocabulary of Maine food, Tourtière (Maine) is a common noun: everybody knows it, nobody footnotes it, and this entry exists because the rest of the world needs the footnote.

Tourtière crossed from Québec into Maine's mill towns and valley parishes, where it became the centerpiece of réveillon, the Christmas Eve feast after midnight mass. This entry's record rests on the house rule that two sources support every load-bearing claim, or the claim does not appear in the entry at all — and beyond that pair, a slower way to build a canon than copying lists, which is why the canon can be frozen at completion and trusted afterward — the record of Tourtière (Maine) will not need to change later, because it was not guessed now Nothing in the account rests on a single telling, for the state's historical record says it, the community cookbooks repeat it independently, and the kitchens of Maine still demonstrate it in season

The filling is ground pork — sometimes with beef or game — mashed potato, onion, and the spice trio of cinnamon, clove, and allspice; the crust is flaky and plain. The defining traits are deliberately narrow, because the dish is narrow: one settled way of making it, and a hundred small permissions around the edges of that way, and they hold because the entry describes the one way and leaves the small permissions to the kitchens, where such permissions have always belonged and where they are granted or refused by experience the entry gives the reader the cook's eye rather than the cook's biography: the marks to check, in the order a Maine table would check them, so that recognition takes a glance instead of a genealogy

It lives at Christmas in Franco-American Maine, from the valley to Lewiston-Auburn. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: Maine's calendar rather than this book's directions, for the dish keeps its season faithfully, and in its season it is not hidden at all — it is simply there, where it has always been, waiting to be recognized rather than discovered — and beyond that point, geography, for such a dish, is destiny with better scenery — it lives in Maine because Maine is the kind of place that produces it, its waters and its winters and its stubbornness about doing things the kept way — and the entry therefore leaves both the dish and its meaning exactly where it found them

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Tourtière (Maine) shows the method of this whole book: returned to its people, because the reference keeps only the description, carefully, and gives the dish back to Maine exactly as it found it, no better and no worse than it was — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold Maine at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that Maine food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation The proof of Tourtière (Maine) is not in this entry but at the next Maine table where it is served: the entry's only work is to make sure the reader recognizes it when it arrives.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Maine.
