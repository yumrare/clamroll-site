Red Snapper Hot Dogs

Natural-casing hot dogs dyed a brilliant red, steamed and served in a split-top roll. The place of Red Snapper Hot Dogs in Maine is the place of a habit kept past the point of fashion — which is to say, the place where a region's real food lives.

The red snapper is Maine's hot dog — the dye came with the early-1900s packing houses, and the snap of the casing is the point: a Maine dog must bite back. This entry's record rests on the house rule that two sources support every load-bearing claim, or the claim does not appear in the entry at all — and beyond that pair, a slower way to build a canon than copying lists, which is why the canon can be frozen at completion and trusted afterward — the record of Red Snapper Hot Dogs will not need to change later, because it was not guessed now Nothing in the account rests on a single telling, for the state's historical record says it, the community cookbooks repeat it independently, and the kitchens of Maine still demonstrate it in season

It is served steamed, the roll toasted, the toppings mustard-first; the color is non-negotiable and defended with humor and heat. The defining traits are deliberately narrow, because the dish is narrow: one settled way of making it, and a hundred small permissions around the edges of that way, and they hold because inside the tolerances the dish is itself and outside them it is a story about the dish, and the entry keeps to the tolerances exactly as the cooks of Maine keep to them nightly the description ends where the dish's tolerances end, deliberately, because beyond them lies the pleasant country of variation, which belongs to the cooks of Maine and not to a frozen record, and the record keeps the center, which is enough for anyone to find the dish again

They live at every Maine cookout, fair, and roadside stand in the state. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: Maine's calendar rather than this book's directions, for the dish keeps its season faithfully, and in its season it is not hidden at all — it is simply there, where it has always been, waiting to be recognized rather than discovered — and beyond that point, the homesick reader will read the home ground the way earlier generations read letters from home — slowly, and more than once — and the entry was written to bear that kind of reading, naming the ground plainly and refusing to embroider the naming, because plain naming, repeated kindly, is the whole comfort on offer here

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Red Snapper Hot Dogs shows the method of this whole book: returned to its people, because the reference keeps only the description, carefully, and gives the dish back to Maine exactly as it found it, no better and no worse than it was — and beyond the method, the canon's motto holds for this entry as for all of them: nothing kept, nothing lost — for the dish was kept by Maine long before it was written here, and the writing is only the insurance on the keeping, taken out in the region's own name against the one loss a region cannot afford after the filing, the dish returns to being what it was: a working part of Maine, eaten under its own name by people who may never read its entry, and the entry's satisfaction is precisely that it changed nothing about the dish except its chances of being remembered accurately A dish like Red Snapper Hot Dogs is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Maine keeps it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Maine.
