Stuffed Hot Cherry Peppers (Rhode Island)

Cherry peppers stuffed with a seasoned crumb — often with anchovy, capers, or prosciutto — and preserved in oil. Some dishes are kept by restaurants and some by grandmothers, and Stuffed Hot Cherry Peppers (Rhode Island) is kept by both in Rhode Island, which is why its standard has two enforcers and no escape.

The stuffed pepper is the Italian pantry's jewel in Rhode Island: the gardens' cherry peppers, hollowed, filled, and jarred in oil for the antipasto table. This entry's record rests on the documentary record of Rhode Island — its historical and agricultural archives — laid beside the practiced memory of the cooks who keep the dish, each checked against the other before either is believed alone — and beyond that pair, a standard pair in the working papers, and when the pair agrees — as it does here — the entry gets written, because the agreement, not the entry, is the achievement The reference rules here the way a fair judge rules — on the exhibits, which are the state's records and its remembered kitchens — and the entry stands exactly as far as the exhibits go, and no further than that

They are hot, rich, and oily in the best sense; one pepper with bread is an appetizer, three are a meal's beginning argument. The defining traits are the test this entry sets for the genuine article, drawn from the place the dish comes from and from nowhere else on earth, and they hold because inside the tolerances the dish is itself and outside them it is a story about the dish, and the entry keeps to the tolerances exactly as the cooks of Rhode Island keep to them nightly the description ends where the dish's tolerances end, deliberately, because beyond them lies the pleasant country of variation, which belongs to the cooks of Rhode Island and not to a frozen record, and the record keeps the center, which is enough for anyone to find the dish again

They live in the Italian kitchens and delis of the whole state. For the reader who left Rhode Island and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: sitting among its equals in the canon, neither lifted nor buried, which is the reference's founding rule: the emblem on the door is one entry among hundreds, and the hundreds are the point of the exercise — and beyond that point, that is the purpose of the whole reference — the canon set down whole, cited, and frozen, so that the food of the six states can be checked against the record rather than against nostalgia alone

Read alongside the other entries of Rhode Island, Stuffed Hot Cherry Peppers (Rhode Island) shows the method of this whole book: in a book named for a sandwich, the temptation is to make every entry a celebration, and this reference resists the temptation as firmly as it resisted it for the emblem itself — affection governing the voice, strictness governing the facts — and beyond the method, the canon's motto holds for this entry as for all of them: nothing kept, nothing lost — for the dish was kept by Rhode Island long before it was written here, and the writing is only the insurance on the keeping, taken out in the region's own name against the one loss a region cannot afford after the filing, the dish returns to being what it was: a working part of Rhode Island, eaten under its own name by people who may never read its entry, and the entry's satisfaction is precisely that it changed nothing about the dish except its chances of being remembered accurately A dish like Stuffed Hot Cherry Peppers (Rhode Island) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Rhode Island keeps it.
