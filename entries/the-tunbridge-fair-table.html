The Tunbridge Fair Table

The food of the Tunbridge World's Fair — maple judged, pies contested, and church suppers under the tents. In Vermont, The Tunbridge Fair Table is the calendar made visible: a tradition by which the year, the harvest, and the community are all measured at once.

Tunbridge has held its fair since 1867, 'a real country fair' by its own proud account, where the maple syrup classes are a state championship in all but name. This entry's record rests on the house rule that two sources support every load-bearing claim, or the claim does not appear in the entry at all — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

The fair table is the whole Vermont canon in one field: sugar exhibits, pie contests, bean suppers, and creemees at the stand. The defining traits are deliberately narrow, because the dish is narrow: one settled way of making it, and a hundred small permissions around the edges of that way, and they hold because inside the tolerances the dish is itself and outside them it is a story about the dish, and the entry keeps to the tolerances exactly as the cooks of Vermont keep to them nightly the description ends where the dish's tolerances end, deliberately, because beyond them lies the pleasant country of variation, which belongs to the cooks of Vermont and not to a frozen record, and the record keeps the center, which is enough for anyone to find the dish again

It lives in Tunbridge each September. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: Vermont's calendar rather than this book's directions, for the dish keeps its season faithfully, and in its season it is not hidden at all — it is simply there, where it has always been, waiting to be recognized rather than discovered — and beyond that point, it is not an artifact behind glass in a museum but a working part of Vermont, as current as tonight, findable by anyone who goes where the entry says it lives, and the record and the dish agree about the address, and the agreement is the point of both

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, The Tunbridge Fair Table shows the method of this whole book: returned to its people, because the reference keeps only the description, carefully, and gives the dish back to Vermont exactly as it found it, no better and no worse than it was — and beyond the method, nothing in the canon is here because it is fashionable this year, and nothing stands above its neighbors in the filing; the six states are treated as one region with six accents, and this entry is filed under its accent — Vermont — where it belongs, and where its own people would look for it first filed, it completes nothing and continues everything, one more dish of the six states set down whole on the page, in a reference whose only ambition is that the whole be there when it is wanted by somebody, and with this filing the whole stands one entry nearer to complete Traditions end quietly when they end, and The Tunbridge Fair Table has not ended: somewhere in Vermont the next one is already on a calendar, and this entry exists so the pattern is on record while it is still alive.

Sources: state historical society records; community, church, and fair records of Vermont.
