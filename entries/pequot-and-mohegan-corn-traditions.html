Pequot and Mohegan Corn Traditions

The corn agriculture of Connecticut's Indigenous peoples — the flint corns, the ash-hulled samp, and the succotash the colonists learned at their tables. The first cooks of Connecticut built on what the place gave them, and the place gave Pequot and Mohegan Corn Traditions in quantities that amounted to an instruction.

Corn came up the Connecticut valley with its first farmers, centuries before the English; the Pequot and Mohegan kept the seed, the ash-lye hulling, and the bean partnership that this whole canon descends from. This entry's record rests on the archive of Connecticut on one side and the living kitchens of Connecticut on the other, with the entry written in the narrow ground where the two agree — and beyond that pair, documentation that supports exactly this much and no more, for Connecticut documented this one thoroughly, in ink and in practice, and the entry is sized to that evidence Sources are cited so the next person can check the line, and the line here runs through the published record of Connecticut and through its practiced kitchens, and both sides of it were walked, and both of them held

The valley's flint corn is hard, sweet, and many-colored; hulled into samp or ground to meal, it is the foundation under half the dishes in this book. The defining traits are stated flatly, because at home they are not in dispute: ask in Connecticut and the answer returns with the confidence of long practice, and flat statement, in a reference, is a form of respect, and they hold because the standard is written in no cookbook, and the traits above are the kitchen corrections themselves, transcribed at last into print for readers who were not raised within earshot of them to describe the dish loosely would be to lose it, because the dish is its details, and Connecticut cooks hold those details the way singers hold pitch — not by thinking about them, but by wincing when they are wrong — and the traits above are the wince, transcribed

It lives in the tribal communities of Mashantucket and the Mohegan, and in the state's oldest fields. For the reader who left Connecticut and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: home ground where the record is thickest, though the dish respects no fence and no town line, living wherever in Connecticut its conditions are met — and its conditions are the state's own weather, water, and habit — and beyond that point, it is not an artifact behind glass in a museum but a working part of Connecticut, as current as tonight, findable by anyone who goes where the entry says it lives, and the record and the dish agree about the address, and the agreement is the point of both

Read alongside the other entries of Connecticut, Pequot and Mohegan Corn Traditions shows the method of this whole book: written for the distance — across an ocean, across a generation — by a reference that believes distance is where trust in a record is actually tested, and wrote accordingly — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold Connecticut at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that Connecticut food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation An ingredient like Pequot and Mohegan Corn Traditions does quiet work: it decides the taste of a hundred Connecticut dishes before any of them has a name, and the reference records it so the foundation is as documented as the dishes built on it.
