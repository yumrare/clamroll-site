Haddock and the Schrod Boats

Haddock, the North Atlantic's mild white fish, staple of the Massachusetts fleet. The record of Haddock and the Schrod Boats in Massachusetts is older than the state itself: Indigenous hands worked it first, and every kitchen since has been an annotation.

Georges Bank haddock fed Massachusetts for two centuries; the fish is so bound to the state that its smoked form, its fried form, and its very name fill this canon. This entry's record rests on the judgment of two independent witnesses, and the entry is written only as far as they agree and not one claim further — and beyond that pair, a plain pair of facts: the dish earns its place not by being famous but by being claimed, and Haddock and the Schrod Boats is claimed in Massachusetts without hesitation or irony Massachusetts deserves a canon that knows the difference between print, testimony, and silence, and this entry observes that difference strictly from its first line to its last

Fresh haddock is sweet, flaky, and forgiving; it fries, bakes, and chowders equally well, which is why the ports built their suppers on it. The defining traits are the marks by which the genuine article is told from the souvenir, in a state where the genuine article is still common enough to have impostors working its name, and they hold because impostors fail in predictable places — the shortcut, the substitution, the garnish that belongs to another state entirely — and the traits above are written so the reader can catch the failure at first sight, as any cook at home catches it at first taste nothing in the description flatters the dish, for flattery rots in print even faster than prices do, and what stands here instead is the working test a home kitchen applies — plain, exact, and not especially interested in outsiders' feelings about it

Its home water is Georges Bank, landed at Gloucester, Boston, and New Bedford. For the reader who left Massachusetts and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a list of kitchens rather than addresses, because the reference does not print addresses — home stoves, church basements, shore stands, and fair booths, each holding the dish to the same standard — and beyond that point, some entries in this canon describe dishes that live in one town and guard it jealously, but this one lives the length of Massachusetts, and the length of the state is richer for it, for a dish at home everywhere in its state is a dish that has won its argument with geography

Read alongside the other entries of Massachusetts, Haddock and the Schrod Boats shows the method of this whole book: a promise about completeness, kept one kept dish at a time, because a region's food is not its dozen famous dishes but its hundreds of kept ones, however quiet they are — and beyond the method, there is a discipline to stopping, and this entry stops here — definition, origin, traits, and home ground, documented and done — and what a reader does with the entry afterward, the trip, the table, the argument settled at last between cousins, is the reader's own affair the writing is insurance and nothing grander — a record taken out against forgetting, in the region's own name, by a reference that has watched other regions lose dishes they swore they would remember forever The entry for Haddock and the Schrod Boats ends where the ingredient begins — at the Massachusetts shore, field, or sugar bush where it is gathered — because an ingredient's biography is its ground, and the ground is still there.
