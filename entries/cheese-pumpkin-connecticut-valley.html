Cheese Pumpkin (Connecticut Valley)

The tan, cheese-shaped heirloom squash of the valley — the old pie pumpkin. Foundations are not famous, and Cheese Pumpkin (Connecticut Valley) is not famous; it is something better in the economy of a cuisine — indispensable, the way flour is indispensable, present in the background of a hundred Connecticut triumphs.

Before the can, the pie was made from the cheese pumpkin — so named for its resemblance to a wheel of cheese — grown in the Connecticut valley since the early 1800s and kept for its dense, sweet flesh. This entry's record rests on the contents of the reference's own working file: one source that can be shelved in a library, and one that can be tasted at a table — and beyond that pair, a standard pair in the working papers, and when the pair agrees — as it does here — the entry gets written, because the agreement, not the entry, is the achievement The reference rules here the way a fair judge rules — on the exhibits, which are the state's records and its remembered kitchens — and the entry stands exactly as far as the exhibits go, and no further than that

It makes a pie darker and richer than the jack-o'-lantern kinds; the valley's Thanksgiving standard was the cheese pumpkin long before the tin. The defining traits are each one the part that, removed, would make a Connecticut cook say without hesitation that it is no longer the thing itself, and they hold because nothing in the description is decorative, for it is an inventory of necessities — nothing more, and nothing softer than that — compiled from the practice of the dish's own kitchens every mark listed was earned by the dish in service and not assigned by this reference, which is a clerk here and not an inventor, having written down only what Connecticut enforced, and enforcement repeated across enough kitchens and years is what a standard actually is

It lives in the valley gardens of Connecticut and its river neighbors. For the reader who left Connecticut and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a landscape and a season rather than a door number — find the landscape, in the season, and the dish is there waiting, as it has been every year within memory — and beyond that point, picture it where Connecticut keeps it — in use, on tables, in season, among people who would find the idea of preserving it faintly comic, because from where they sit it is in no danger whatsoever — and then remember that the reference has seen regions forget, and so it writes, and the writing is this entry's whole apology and excuse
