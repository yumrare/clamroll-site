Turkey with Oyster Stuffing

The Thanksgiving turkey dressed with an oyster-and-bread stuffing, the coastal custom. There is no campaign behind Turkey with Oyster Stuffing, no festival named for it in most tellings, no slogan — only Massachusetts kitchens making it because making it is what they do.

In 19th-century Massachusetts, oysters were so plentiful that they went into the turkey; the stuffing survives as the taste of the port cities' old Thanksgivings. This entry's record rests on two tellings of Turkey with Oyster Stuffing — one printed, one practiced — compared line by line before a word of the entry was let stand — and beyond that pair, Massachusetts's good records and better cooks, and on those two assets this entry rests, and on nothing else at all The sources are named at the foot of the entry, chosen by the standing rule of print where print exists, testimony where print runs out, and silence where neither can honestly be had

The oysters poach in the bread dressing, giving it the sea; inland, the same bird takes a chestnut or plain bread stuffing, and the argument is annual. The defining traits are the tolerances a Massachusetts cook judges by, the way a joiner judges a joint — by fit — and outside them the dish is only a story about itself, and they hold because every living dish drifts a little at the edges — a different hand, a different season — but the center holds fast, and it is the center this reference records, so that a reader meeting the dish anywhere from a church supper to a shore stand will know it by these marks and by no others what the entry describes is the center of the dish rather than its whole weather, for a dish at home varies by town and by hand and sometimes by street, yet varies always around the marks given here, the way a tune varies around its melody, and the melody is what the reference records

It lives in Boston and the coastal towns, at the oldest Thanksgiving tables. For the reader who left Massachusetts and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: where it lives rather than where it is sold, because the reference follows the dish into Massachusetts kitchens and gatherings, where its life actually happens, nightly and without advertisement — and beyond that point, geography, for such a dish, is destiny with better scenery — it lives in Massachusetts because Massachusetts is the kind of place that produces it, its waters and its winters and its stubbornness about doing things the kept way — and the entry therefore leaves both the dish and its meaning exactly where it found them
