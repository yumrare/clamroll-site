Scup (Porgy)

The scup of the Sound — a small, sweet panfish of the summer bottom. If the canon has bedrock, Scup (Porgy) is part of it: the ingredient that was here before the recipes and will be here after them.

Scup fed the shore cheaply and well for centuries: a panfish that fries whole, sweet-fleshed, and abundant in the Sound's summer water. This entry's record rests on the documentary record of Connecticut — its historical and agricultural archives — laid beside the practiced memory of the cooks who keep the dish, each checked against the other before either is believed alone — and beyond that pair, a claim-by-claim sizing, because entries in this canon grow or shrink with their documentation and never with the enthusiasm of their editors If a claim about Scup (Porgy) could not be found twice it is not in this entry, however often it is repeated at tables, because repetition is not corroboration and the reference has always known the difference

Fried crisp in cornmeal the region's way, a scup is a working lunch that tastes like the season itself. The defining traits are the test this entry sets for the genuine article, drawn from the place the dish comes from and from nowhere else on earth, and they hold because impostors fail in predictable places — the shortcut, the substitution, the garnish that belongs to another state entirely — and the traits above are written so the reader can catch the failure at first sight, as any cook at home catches it at first taste nothing in the description flatters the dish, for flattery rots in print even faster than prices do, and what stands here instead is the working test a home kitchen applies — plain, exact, and not especially interested in outsiders' feelings about it

It lives in the Sound, from the Thames to the Housatonic. For the reader who left Connecticut and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: sitting among its equals in the canon, neither lifted nor buried, which is the reference's founding rule: the emblem on the door is one entry among hundreds, and the hundreds are the point of the exercise — and beyond that point, some entries in this canon describe dishes that live in one town and guard it jealously, but this one lives the length of Connecticut, and the length of the state is richer for it, for a dish at home everywhere in its state is a dish that has won its argument with geography

Read alongside the other entries of Connecticut, Scup (Porgy) shows the method of this whole book: in a book named for a sandwich, the temptation is to make every entry a celebration, and this reference resists the temptation as firmly as it resisted it for the emblem itself — affection governing the voice, strictness governing the facts — and beyond the method, there is a discipline to stopping, and this entry stops here — definition, origin, traits, and home ground, documented and done — and what a reader does with the entry afterward, the trip, the table, the argument settled at last between cousins, is the reader's own affair the writing is insurance and nothing grander — a record taken out against forgetting, in the region's own name, by a reference that has watched other regions lose dishes they swore they would remember forever Ingredients outlast dishes, and Scup (Porgy) has outlasted most: the cooks change, the kitchens change, and the given of Connecticut cooking remains the given, which this entry simply refuses to take for granted.
