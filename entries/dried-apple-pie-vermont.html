Dried Apple Pie (Vermont)

A pie of dried apples, soaked back to life and spiced — the winter pie of the farm. In the working vocabulary of Vermont food, Dried Apple Pie (Vermont) is a common noun: everybody knows it, nobody footnotes it, and this entry exists because the rest of the world needs the footnote.

Vermont's farms dried apples on strings and racks above the stove; the dried-apple pie carried the orchard through to sugaring, when fresh food was still a rumor. This entry's record rests on the house rule that two sources support every load-bearing claim, or the claim does not appear in the entry at all — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

The filling is dense and dark, almost candied; with cream, it is a pie that tastes like patience rewarded. The defining traits are deliberately narrow, because the dish is narrow: one settled way of making it, and a hundred small permissions around the edges of that way, and they hold because inside the tolerances the dish is itself and outside them it is a story about the dish, and the entry keeps to the tolerances exactly as the cooks of Vermont keep to them nightly the description ends where the dish's tolerances end, deliberately, because beyond them lies the pleasant country of variation, which belongs to the cooks of Vermont and not to a frozen record, and the record keeps the center, which is enough for anyone to find the dish again

It lives in the farm record and the winter kitchens of the whole state. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: Vermont's calendar rather than this book's directions, for the dish keeps its season faithfully, and in its season it is not hidden at all — it is simply there, where it has always been, waiting to be recognized rather than discovered — and beyond that point, geography, for such a dish, is destiny with better scenery — it lives in Vermont because Vermont is the kind of place that produces it, its waters and its winters and its stubbornness about doing things the kept way — and the entry therefore leaves both the dish and its meaning exactly where it found them

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, Dried Apple Pie (Vermont) shows the method of this whole book: returned to its people, because the reference keeps only the description, carefully, and gives the dish back to Vermont exactly as it found it, no better and no worse than it was — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold Vermont at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that Vermont food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation A dish like Dried Apple Pie (Vermont) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Vermont keeps it.
