Maine Clam Chowder

A milk chowder of clams that pours thinner than Boston's — clam liquor forward, lightly thickened if at all. Maine Clam Chowder belongs to Maine the way weather belongs to a coast: not chosen, not imported, simply what the place produces when it feeds itself.

Maine has always regarded the thick white Boston chowder with suspicion; Down East cooks hold that a chowder should taste of clams and milk, not flour, and some use no thickener but the potato. This entry's record rests on a standing rule that a claim appearing in only one place stays out, however good the story, while a claim two independent records support goes in, however plain it looks on the page — and beyond that pair, documentation that supports exactly this much and no more, for Maine documented this one thoroughly, in ink and in practice, and the entry is sized to that evidence Sources are cited so the next person can check the line, and the line here runs through the published record of Maine and through its practiced kitchens, and both sides of it were walked, and both of them held

It is a shore chowder — clams, salt pork, onion, potato, milk — made in the morning and better at noon; the crackers go in at the table. The defining traits are kept the way Maine keeps all its standards — in the corrections of experienced cooks: a little less of this, never that, always the other — and transcribed here at last, and they hold because every living dish drifts a little at the edges — a different hand, a different season — but the center holds fast, and it is the center this reference records, so that a reader meeting the dish anywhere from a church supper to a shore stand will know it by these marks and by no others what the entry describes is the center of the dish rather than its whole weather, for a dish at home varies by town and by hand and sometimes by street, yet varies always around the marks given here, the way a tune varies around its melody, and the melody is what the reference records

It lives in the home kitchens and shore restaurants of the whole Maine coast. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the geography of Maine itself, written small, for wherever it lives, it lives because the place produces what the dish needs and the people to insist on it, in season and out — and beyond that point, entries carry no prices, no hours, and no league tables of dishes, because those facts rot and the record should not; the rest is the reader's own journey home, and this book exists to shorten that journey back to Maine by exactly one homesickness

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Maine Clam Chowder shows the method of this whole book: the thing the reference says about everything it files — that it was here, that it is documented, and that it belongs to the people who keep it — said here with the same plainness — and beyond the method, the canon's motto holds for this entry as for all of them: nothing kept, nothing lost — for the dish was kept by Maine long before it was written here, and the writing is only the insurance on the keeping, taken out in the region's own name against the one loss a region cannot afford after the filing, the dish returns to being what it was: a working part of Maine, eaten under its own name by people who may never read its entry, and the entry's satisfaction is precisely that it changed nothing about the dish except its chances of being remembered accurately Filed here, Maine Clam Chowder is finished being a rumor and becomes a record — the Maine dish as it stands, neither inflated nor apologized for, ready for the reader who goes looking for it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Maine.
