Smoked Alewives

Alewives split, brined, and smoked over hardwoods — the spring run preserved. In the working vocabulary of Maine food, Smoked Alewives is a common noun: everybody knows it, nobody footnotes it, and this entry exists because the rest of the world needs the footnote.

Smoking was how the glut of the spring run became the year's food; alewives took smoke well, and a smoked alewife with brown bread was a working lunch for a century. This entry's record rests on load-bearing claims standing on two sources, drawn from state records, fair and fishery reports, church and community cookbooks, and the standard regional food histories — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

They are strong, oily, and deeply smoky — an acquired taste that Down East and midcoast families acquire early and keep for life. The defining traits are the ones Maine's own cooks police, at the stove and at the table, by correction and by example, whenever a hand drifts from the standard, and they hold because inside the tolerances the dish is itself and outside them it is a story about the dish, and the entry keeps to the tolerances exactly as the cooks of Maine keep to them nightly the description ends where the dish's tolerances end, deliberately, because beyond them lies the pleasant country of variation, which belongs to the cooks of Maine and not to a frozen record, and the record keeps the center, which is enough for anyone to find the dish again

They live on the midcoast and Down East rivers where the runs return. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the things that hold across decades — what the dish is, where it comes from, and what defines it — rather than the things that rot by spring — and beyond that point, the homesick reader will read the home ground the way earlier generations read letters from home — slowly, and more than once — and the entry was written to bear that kind of reading, naming the ground plainly and refusing to embroider the naming, because plain naming, repeated kindly, is the whole comfort on offer here

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Smoked Alewives shows the method of this whole book: the record is frozen when it is finished, corrected only for outright error, and this entry is written to that standard from its first line to its last — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold Maine at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that Maine food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation A dish like Smoked Alewives is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Maine keeps it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Maine.
