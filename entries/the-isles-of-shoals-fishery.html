The Isles of Shoals Fishery

The fishing islands nine miles off Portsmouth, once the coast's richest cod and haddock station. The food at The Isles of Shoals Fishery matters, but the gathering is the dish: New Hampshire cooks it communally, serves it communally, and remembers it communally.

The Shoals were a fishing nation unto themselves in the 1600s and 1700s — their dried fish went to Europe, their harbor was a city on the water, and Portsmouth grew rich in their lee. This entry's record rests on two tellings of The Isles of Shoals Fishery — one printed, one practiced — compared line by line before a word of the entry was let stand — and beyond that pair, a standard pair in the working papers, and when the pair agrees — as it does here — the entry gets written, because the agreement, not the entry, is the achievement The reference rules here the way a fair judge rules — on the exhibits, which are the state's records and its remembered kitchens — and the entry stands exactly as far as the exhibits go, and no further than that

The islands' table was fish in every form — fresh, salted, smoked — and their name for a fine haddock, 'Shoals fish,' was a market grade. The defining traits are the tolerances a New Hampshire cook judges by, the way a joiner judges a joint — by fit — and outside them the dish is only a story about itself, and they hold because the standard is written in no cookbook, and the traits above are the kitchen corrections themselves, transcribed at last into print for readers who were not raised within earshot of them to describe the dish loosely would be to lose it, because the dish is its details, and New Hampshire cooks hold those details the way singers hold pitch — not by thinking about them, but by wincing when they are wrong — and the traits above are the wince, transcribed

It lives in the record of the Seacoast, nine miles out. For the reader who left New Hampshire and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: where it lives rather than where it is sold, because the reference follows the dish into New Hampshire kitchens and gatherings, where its life actually happens, nightly and without advertisement — and beyond that point, the homesick reader will read the home ground the way earlier generations read letters from home — slowly, and more than once — and the entry was written to bear that kind of reading, naming the ground plainly and refusing to embroider the naming, because plain naming, repeated kindly, is the whole comfort on offer here

Read alongside the other entries of New Hampshire, The Isles of Shoals Fishery shows the method of this whole book: modest and total at once: that it exists, that it is of New Hampshire, that its marks are these, and that the record of it will still be accurate when the reader's grandchildren come to check it — and beyond the method, nothing in it can rot on the page — no prices to expire within the year, no hours to change with the season, no crowns to defend when the next season comes — because what remains is the durable part, what the dish is and where it was born and what defines it and where in New Hampshire it lives, which is the part the diaspora writes home about the entry is closed the way a fair book is closed — the exhibits entered, the ruling made, the record straight — and New Hampshire goes on cooking its food, and the reference goes on keeping its record, and the reader goes on to the next entry, satisfied or arguing, and that orderly succession is as it should be When the year turns in New Hampshire, The Isles of Shoals Fishery will come round again, and this entry will be waiting — not to direct it, only to have said, on the record, that it was here and what it meant.
