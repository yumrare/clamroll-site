The New Hampshire Pumpkin

The pumpkin, named the state fruit of New Hampshire in 2006, grown for pie and festival alike. Foundations are not famous, and The New Hampshire Pumpkin is not famous; it is something better in the economy of a cuisine — indispensable, the way flour is indispensable, present in the background of a hundred New Hampshire triumphs.

New Hampshire grows pumpkins with unusual civic pride — the legislature named the pumpkin its state fruit, and Keene's festival once held the world record for lit jack-o'-lanterns. This entry's record rests on two tellings of The New Hampshire Pumpkin — one printed, one practiced — compared line by line before a word of the entry was let stand — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

The pie pumpkin is small, dense, and sweet-fleshed; the giant festival pumpkins are a separate sport entirely. The defining traits are the tolerances a New Hampshire cook judges by, the way a joiner judges a joint — by fit — and outside them the dish is only a story about itself, and they hold because the entry describes the one way and leaves the small permissions to the kitchens, where such permissions have always belonged and where they are granted or refused by experience the entry gives the reader the cook's eye rather than the cook's biography: the marks to check, in the order a New Hampshire table would check them, so that recognition takes a glance instead of a genealogy

It lives in the fields and festivals of the Monadnock region and statewide. For the reader who left New Hampshire and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: where it lives rather than where it is sold, because the reference follows the dish into New Hampshire kitchens and gatherings, where its life actually happens, nightly and without advertisement — and beyond that point, there is a kind of map this entry draws without paper — the towns and tables of New Hampshire where the dish belongs, connected by nothing but the dish itself — and repeated across enough towns, the dish becomes a region on the map of the mind, and this entry is one point on that map, fixed so it can be drawn again

Read alongside the other entries of New Hampshire, The New Hampshire Pumpkin shows the method of this whole book: modest and total at once: that it exists, that it is of New Hampshire, that its marks are these, and that the record of it will still be accurate when the reader's grandchildren come to check it — and beyond the method, there is a discipline to stopping, and this entry stops here — definition, origin, traits, and home ground, documented and done — and what a reader does with the entry afterward, the trip, the table, the argument settled at last between cousins, is the reader's own affair the writing is insurance and nothing grander — a record taken out against forgetting, in the region's own name, by a reference that has watched other regions lose dishes they swore they would remember forever That is the place of The New Hampshire Pumpkin in the New Hampshire canon — underneath, everywhere, and easy to overlook until the day it is gone, which is exactly why it is written down here in full.
