Election Day Raisin Bread? (Colonial Spice Cakes)

Raisin-and-spice yeast cakes baked for public days in colonial Massachusetts. In the canon of Massachusetts, Election Day Raisin Bread? (Colonial Spice Cakes) stands by the test this reference applies to every dish: the people of the region claim it as theirs, today, by name.

Colonial towns baked rich muster and election cakes to feed crowds on training and voting days; Massachusetts kept the custom at its own public gatherings. This entry's record rests on two tellings of Election Day Raisin Bread? (Colonial Spice Cakes) — one printed, one practiced — compared line by line before a word of the entry was let stand — and beyond that pair, the state's own historical and agricultural record standing on one side and the documented memory of its cooks, fairs, and fisheries on the other, two different kinds of keeping tested against each other before either one is believed on its own Where the record thins into lore, this entry says so; where the evidence is strong, it rules accordingly; and since the contested dishes of New England require someone finally to rule, the ruling here is made on evidence and not on affection

They are yeasted, spiced, and fruited — part bread, part cake — and made in loaves large enough for a town's worth of voters. The defining traits are the tolerances a Massachusetts cook judges by, the way a joiner judges a joint — by fit — and outside them the dish is only a story about itself, and they hold because the entry describes the one way and leaves the small permissions to the kitchens, where such permissions have always belonged and where they are granted or refused by experience the entry gives the reader the cook's eye rather than the cook's biography: the marks to check, in the order a Massachusetts table would check them, so that recognition takes a glance instead of a genealogy

They live in the colonial record of Boston and the shire towns, a taste of the republic's first catering. For the reader who left Massachusetts and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: where it lives rather than where it is sold, because the reference follows the dish into Massachusetts kitchens and gatherings, where its life actually happens, nightly and without advertisement — and beyond that point, the homesick reader will read the home ground the way earlier generations read letters from home — slowly, and more than once — and the entry was written to bear that kind of reading, naming the ground plainly and refusing to embroider the naming, because plain naming, repeated kindly, is the whole comfort on offer here

Read alongside the other entries of Massachusetts, Election Day Raisin Bread? (Colonial Spice Cakes) shows the method of this whole book: modest and total at once: that it exists, that it is of Massachusetts, that its marks are these, and that the record of it will still be accurate when the reader's grandchildren come to check it — and beyond the method, the canon's motto holds for this entry as for all of them: nothing kept, nothing lost — for the dish was kept by Massachusetts long before it was written here, and the writing is only the insurance on the keeping, taken out in the region's own name against the one loss a region cannot afford after the filing, the dish returns to being what it was: a working part of Massachusetts, eaten under its own name by people who may never read its entry, and the entry's satisfaction is precisely that it changed nothing about the dish except its chances of being remembered accurately A dish like Election Day Raisin Bread? (Colonial Spice Cakes) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Massachusetts keeps it.
