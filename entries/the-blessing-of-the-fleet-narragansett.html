The Blessing of the Fleet (Narragansett)

The summer ceremony at Galilee, when the fishing fleet is blessed for the season in the Portuguese and working-waterfront tradition. Dishes can travel; traditions cannot, and The Blessing of the Fleet (Narragansett) has never traveled — it exists only where Rhode Island gathers, which is exactly why the reference records it.

Narragansett's Blessing of the Fleet has opened the season since the 1960s — the boats dressed, the crews' families ashore, and a weekend of the fleet's own food around it. This entry's record rests on the contents of the reference's own working file: one source that can be shelved in a library, and one that can be tasted at a table — and beyond that pair, Rhode Island's good records and better cooks, and on those two assets this entry rests, and on nothing else at all The sources are named at the foot of the entry, chosen by the standing rule of print where print exists, testimony where print runs out, and silence where neither can honestly be had

The festival table runs on the fleet's catch — calamari first among Rhode Island's dishes — and the blessing is the point of the party. The defining traits are each one the part that, removed, would make a Rhode Island cook say without hesitation that it is no longer the thing itself, and they hold because impostors fail in predictable places — the shortcut, the substitution, the garnish that belongs to another state entirely — and the traits above are written so the reader can catch the failure at first sight, as any cook at home catches it at first taste nothing in the description flatters the dish, for flattery rots in print even faster than prices do, and what stands here instead is the working test a home kitchen applies — plain, exact, and not especially interested in outsiders' feelings about it

It lives at Galilee, in Narragansett, each summer. For the reader who left Rhode Island and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a landscape and a season rather than a door number — find the landscape, in the season, and the dish is there waiting, as it has been every year within memory — and beyond that point, that is the purpose of the whole reference — the canon set down whole, cited, and frozen, so that the food of the six states can be checked against the record rather than against nostalgia alone

Read alongside the other entries of Rhode Island, The Blessing of the Fleet (Narragansett) shows the method of this whole book: filed level with the emblem itself, because no entry is the point and every entry is, the clam roll on the door being one sandwich and the four hundred filings behind it being the reference proper — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold Rhode Island at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that Rhode Island food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation Nothing about The Blessing of the Fleet (Narragansett) is finished, which is its distinction in a frozen reference: the record is fixed, the tradition is not, and the gap between the two is where Rhode Island lives.

Sources: state historical society records; community, church, and fair records of Rhode Island.
