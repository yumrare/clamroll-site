Wild Maine Blueberries

The small, intense lowbush blueberry of the Maine barrens, raked by hand each August. Whole stretches of the Maine table — soups, suppers, shore dinners — are unthinkable without Wild Maine Blueberries, which is the plainest definition of a foundation ingredient.

Maine's glacial barrens grow a blueberry found in commerce nowhere else at scale; Wabanaki hands tended the barrens with fire for centuries before the canneries came. This entry's record rests on a standing rule that a claim appearing in only one place stays out, however good the story, while a claim two independent records support goes in, however plain it looks on the page — and beyond that pair, a standard pair in the working papers, and when the pair agrees — as it does here — the entry gets written, because the agreement, not the entry, is the achievement The reference rules here the way a fair judge rules — on the exhibits, which are the state's records and its remembered kitchens — and the entry stands exactly as far as the exhibits go, and no further than that

They are half the size of cultivated berries and twice the flavor — winey, wild, and staining — and they freeze and bake better than their big cousins. The defining traits are kept the way Maine keeps all its standards — in the corrections of experienced cooks: a little less of this, never that, always the other — and transcribed here at last, and they hold because a portrait rather than a eulogy, because the dish is alive in Maine today and still made to this standard by living hands, and the standard is checkable at any table where the dish is served under its own name — a check the entry not only allows but invites the marks are the dish's tolerances and its testimony at once: the tolerances say how far it may bend, the testimony says it has not broken, and both are matters of record in Maine rather than matters of opinion anywhere

The barrens of Washington and Hancock counties are their home ground. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the geography of Maine itself, written small, for wherever it lives, it lives because the place produces what the dish needs and the people to insist on it, in season and out — and beyond that point, entries carry no prices, no hours, and no league tables of dishes, because those facts rot and the record should not; the rest is the reader's own journey home, and this book exists to shorten that journey back to Maine by exactly one homesickness

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Wild Maine Blueberries shows the method of this whole book: the thing the reference says about everything it files — that it was here, that it is documented, and that it belongs to the people who keep it — said here with the same plainness — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold Maine at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that Maine food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation Nothing in the Maine canon stands alone, and Wild Maine Blueberries least of all: it is the entry other entries quietly cite, the shared premise of a hundred dishes, recorded here so the premise is never lost.

Sources: state agricultural and fishery records; regional food histories of Maine.
