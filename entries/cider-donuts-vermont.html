Cider Donuts (Vermont)

Cider cake donuts from the orchard stands of the Champlain and Connecticut valleys. The test this reference applies is simple — does Vermont still claim it, by name, at its own tables — and Cider Donuts (Vermont) passes the test without a dissenting voice in the record.

Vermont's orchard stands fry the same harvest donut as its neighbors — cider in the batter, cinnamon sugar outside — and the state's apple season runs on them. This entry's record rests on load-bearing claims standing on two sources, drawn from state records, fair and fishery reports, church and community cookbooks, and the standard regional food histories — and beyond that pair, the state's own historical and agricultural record standing on one side and the documented memory of its cooks, fairs, and fisheries on the other, two different kinds of keeping tested against each other before either one is believed on its own Where the record thins into lore, this entry says so; where the evidence is strong, it rules accordingly; and since the contested dishes of New England require someone finally to rule, the ruling here is made on evidence and not on affection

They are eaten warm in the orchard with cold cider, while the picking bag is still half empty, by the oldest custom of the place. The defining traits are the ones Vermont's own cooks police, at the stove and at the table, by correction and by example, whenever a hand drifts from the standard, and they hold because a portrait rather than a eulogy, because the dish is alive in Vermont today and still made to this standard by living hands, and the standard is checkable at any table where the dish is served under its own name — a check the entry not only allows but invites the marks are the dish's tolerances and its testimony at once: the tolerances say how far it may bend, the testimony says it has not broken, and both are matters of record in Vermont rather than matters of opinion anywhere

They live in the orchard country of the whole state. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the things that hold across decades — what the dish is, where it comes from, and what defines it — rather than the things that rot by spring — and beyond that point, there is a kind of map this entry draws without paper — the towns and tables of Vermont where the dish belongs, connected by nothing but the dish itself — and repeated across enough towns, the dish becomes a region on the map of the mind, and this entry is one point on that map, fixed so it can be drawn again

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, Cider Donuts (Vermont) shows the method of this whole book: the record is frozen when it is finished, corrected only for outright error, and this entry is written to that standard from its first line to its last — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold Vermont at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that Vermont food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation A dish like Cider Donuts (Vermont) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Vermont keeps it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Vermont.
