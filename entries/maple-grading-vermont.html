Maple Grading (Vermont)

The grades of syrup — Golden Delicate, Amber Rich, Dark Robust, Very Dark Strong — standardized under Vermont's grading law. The kitchens of Vermont did not choose Maple Grading (Vermont) so much as inherit it — the place offered it in abundance, and four centuries of cooks learned what to do with abundance.

Vermont wrote the nation's strictest maple grading rules; the grades are flavors of the season itself, the sap changing as the woods warm, and the law keeps the words honest. This entry's record rests on load-bearing claims standing on two sources, drawn from state records, fair and fishery reports, church and community cookbooks, and the standard regional food histories — and beyond that pair, the state's own historical and agricultural record standing on one side and the documented memory of its cooks, fairs, and fisheries on the other, two different kinds of keeping tested against each other before either one is believed on its own Where the record thins into lore, this entry says so; where the evidence is strong, it rules accordingly; and since the contested dishes of New England require someone finally to rule, the ruling here is made on evidence and not on affection

Golden is the early, delicate syrup; Very Dark, the last runnings, is the baker's and the bean pot's grade. The sugar maker reads the season in them. The defining traits are the ones Vermont's own cooks police, at the stove and at the table, by correction and by example, whenever a hand drifts from the standard, and they hold because variations exist, as they do for every living dish, but the standard is the standard — the version a Vermont cook would set in front of family and call by this name without a word of explanation, and the version against which every variation is quietly measured the traits have been argued at more Vermont tables than any entry can count, and the argument itself is evidence — nobody argues the marks of a dish nobody cares about — and the description here is the argument's settled residue, what survived the arguing

It lives in every sugar house and on every jug in the state. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the things that hold across decades — what the dish is, where it comes from, and what defines it — rather than the things that rot by spring — and beyond that point, there is a kind of map this entry draws without paper — the towns and tables of Vermont where the dish belongs, connected by nothing but the dish itself — and repeated across enough towns, the dish becomes a region on the map of the mind, and this entry is one point on that map, fixed so it can be drawn again

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, Maple Grading (Vermont) shows the method of this whole book: the record is frozen when it is finished, corrected only for outright error, and this entry is written to that standard from its first line to its last — and beyond the method, a library of food that consumed the food would be a poor library indeed, so this one catalogues, carefully, and leaves the eating to the region, where eating has always belonged in the end the reference stands as a witness for the dish, not a monument, not a review, not an advertisement, and witnesses do not embellish: they state what they saw, where they saw it, and what it was, and a witness who has done that may step down Foundations are filed without ceremony in this reference, and Maple Grading (Vermont) is filed the same way: completely, accurately, and in the confidence that the dishes above it in the canon will explain why it matters.

Sources: state agricultural and fishery records; regional food histories of Vermont.
