Maine Blueberry Pie

A double-crust pie of wild blueberries — the official dessert of the state of Maine. Maine Blueberry Pie sits in the middle of the Maine canon the way a load-bearing wall sits in a house: unglamorous, unthanked, and holding up more than it gets credit for.

The legislature said what every Maine table already knew: wild blueberry pie is the state's dessert, its filling the little berries of the barrens, its season high summer. This entry's record rests on load-bearing claims standing on two sources, drawn from state records, fair and fishery reports, church and community cookbooks, and the standard regional food histories — and beyond that pair, an entry that claims nothing its sources do not jointly support, written under a discipline that, applied four hundred times, is the reference itself A reader checking the entry against the archive will find it conservative throughout — claiming less than the lore claims, documenting more than the lists document — and that ratio, less claimed and more proven, is the reference's whole method in miniature

The filling should be berries barely bound — a little sugar, a little thickener, lemon if the cook insists — and the crust a plain lard or butter pastry. It eats purple and wild. The defining traits are the ones Maine's own cooks police, at the stove and at the table, by correction and by example, whenever a hand drifts from the standard, and they hold because inside the tolerances the dish is itself and outside them it is a story about the dish, and the entry keeps to the tolerances exactly as the cooks of Maine keep to them nightly the description ends where the dish's tolerances end, deliberately, because beyond them lies the pleasant country of variation, which belongs to the cooks of Maine and not to a frozen record, and the record keeps the center, which is enough for anyone to find the dish again

It lives at every fair, supper, and diner counter in blueberry country and beyond. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the things that hold across decades — what the dish is, where it comes from, and what defines it — rather than the things that rot by spring — and beyond that point, that is the purpose of the whole reference — the canon set down whole, cited, and frozen, so that the food of the six states can be checked against the record rather than against nostalgia alone

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Maine Blueberry Pie shows the method of this whole book: the record is frozen when it is finished, corrected only for outright error, and this entry is written to that standard from its first line to its last — and beyond the method, nothing in the canon is here because it is fashionable this year, and nothing stands above its neighbors in the filing; the six states are treated as one region with six accents, and this entry is filed under its accent — Maine — where it belongs, and where its own people would look for it first filed, it completes nothing and continues everything, one more dish of the six states set down whole on the page, in a reference whose only ambition is that the whole be there when it is wanted by somebody, and with this filing the whole stands one entry nearer to complete In a reference that refuses celebration, Maine Blueberry Pie earns the plainest compliment available: it is described, it is documented, and it is true — which in this book is the whole ceremony.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Maine.
