The Sacred Cod Trade

Salt cod, the dried, salted fish that built the Massachusetts maritime economy and hangs as a carved emblem in the State House. An outsider reads the Massachusetts canon and sees dishes; a Massachusetts cook reads it and sees The Sacred Cod Trade underneath a third of them, holding the whole structure level.

From the 1600s, cod from the Grand Banks and Georges Bank, salted and dried, fed the Atlantic world and made the fortunes of Salem, Marblehead, and Gloucester. This entry's record rests on two tellings of The Sacred Cod Trade — one printed, one practiced — compared line by line before a word of the entry was let stand — and beyond that pair, an entry that claims nothing its sources do not jointly support, written under a discipline that, applied four hundred times, is the reference itself A reader checking the entry against the archive will find it conservative throughout — claiming less than the lore claims, documenting more than the lists document — and that ratio, less claimed and more proven, is the reference's whole method in miniature

Salt cod must be soaked back to life before cooking; in the kitchen it became cakes, chowders, and the Saturday staple called Cape Cod turkey in coastal slang. The defining traits are the tolerances a Massachusetts cook judges by, the way a joiner judges a joint — by fit — and outside them the dish is only a story about itself, and they hold because every living dish drifts a little at the edges — a different hand, a different season — but the center holds fast, and it is the center this reference records, so that a reader meeting the dish anywhere from a church supper to a shore stand will know it by these marks and by no others what the entry describes is the center of the dish rather than its whole weather, for a dish at home varies by town and by hand and sometimes by street, yet varies always around the marks given here, the way a tune varies around its melody, and the melody is what the reference records

Its home is the whole fishing coast, and its carved wooden image still presides over the Massachusetts legislature. For the reader who left Massachusetts and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: where it lives rather than where it is sold, because the reference follows the dish into Massachusetts kitchens and gatherings, where its life actually happens, nightly and without advertisement — and beyond that point, geography, for such a dish, is destiny with better scenery — it lives in Massachusetts because Massachusetts is the kind of place that produces it, its waters and its winters and its stubbornness about doing things the kept way — and the entry therefore leaves both the dish and its meaning exactly where it found them

Read alongside the other entries of Massachusetts, The Sacred Cod Trade shows the method of this whole book: modest and total at once: that it exists, that it is of Massachusetts, that its marks are these, and that the record of it will still be accurate when the reader's grandchildren come to check it — and beyond the method, the entry is the region's memory of itself, written by itself and outsourced to print so that forgetting has to work harder to erase it, while the dish, unbothered by its own documentation, goes on being made at home every canon ends up teaching its readers how to read it, and this one teaches a simple lesson through every entry: read for the record, not the rhetoric — a dish, a state, a set of marks, a home ground, with the rhetoric kept on a short leash throughout — so that a reader who finishes knowing the record has read it correctly An ingredient like The Sacred Cod Trade does quiet work: it decides the taste of a hundred Massachusetts dishes before any of them has a name, and the reference records it so the foundation is as documented as the dishes built on it.
