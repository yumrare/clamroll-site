White Mountain Trout

Brook trout from the White Mountain streams, pan-fried at camp and lodge tables. White Mountain Trout sits in the middle of the New Hampshire canon the way a load-bearing wall sits in a house: unglamorous, unthanked, and holding up more than it gets credit for.

The White Mountains' sporting hotels and camps built their breakfasts on the morning's trout; the fish of the Saco, the Pemigewasset, and the Androscoggin headwaters remain the region's table pride. This entry's record rests on two tellings of White Mountain Trout — one printed, one practiced — compared line by line before a word of the entry was let stand — and beyond that pair, documentation that supports exactly this much and no more, for New Hampshire documented this one thoroughly, in ink and in practice, and the entry is sized to that evidence Sources are cited so the next person can check the line, and the line here runs through the published record of New Hampshire and through its practiced kitchens, and both sides of it were walked, and both of them held

A mountain trout is small and sweet, fried in pork fat or butter with nothing to hide it; the shore lunch is its ceremony. The defining traits are the tolerances a New Hampshire cook judges by, the way a joiner judges a joint — by fit — and outside them the dish is only a story about itself, and they hold because the entry describes the one way and leaves the small permissions to the kitchens, where such permissions have always belonged and where they are granted or refused by experience the entry gives the reader the cook's eye rather than the cook's biography: the marks to check, in the order a New Hampshire table would check them, so that recognition takes a glance instead of a genealogy

They live in the White Mountain streams and the camps along them. For the reader who left New Hampshire and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: where it lives rather than where it is sold, because the reference follows the dish into New Hampshire kitchens and gatherings, where its life actually happens, nightly and without advertisement — and beyond that point, there is a kind of map this entry draws without paper — the towns and tables of New Hampshire where the dish belongs, connected by nothing but the dish itself — and repeated across enough towns, the dish becomes a region on the map of the mind, and this entry is one point on that map, fixed so it can be drawn again

Read alongside the other entries of New Hampshire, White Mountain Trout shows the method of this whole book: modest and total at once: that it exists, that it is of New Hampshire, that its marks are these, and that the record of it will still be accurate when the reader's grandchildren come to check it — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold New Hampshire at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that New Hampshire food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation A dish like White Mountain Trout is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as New Hampshire keeps it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of New Hampshire.
