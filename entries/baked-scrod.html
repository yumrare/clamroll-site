Baked Scrod

Young cod or haddock, baked with buttered crumbs — 'scrod' being Boston's own word for the day's young white fish. In the working vocabulary of Massachusetts food, Baked Scrod is a common noun: everybody knows it, nobody footnotes it, and this entry exists because the rest of the world needs the footnote.

Scrod is a Boston coinage of the fish houses: the catch's young cod or haddock, whichever was finest, prepared simply so the fish spoke for itself. This entry's record rests on two tellings of Baked Scrod — one printed, one practiced — compared line by line before a word of the entry was let stand — and beyond that pair, a plain pair of facts: the dish earns its place not by being famous but by being claimed, and Baked Scrod is claimed in Massachusetts without hesitation or irony Massachusetts deserves a canon that knows the difference between print, testimony, and silence, and this entry observes that difference strictly from its first line to its last

It is baked hot and fast under buttered crumbs until the flakes separate; lemon and nothing much else. The defining traits are the tolerances a Massachusetts cook judges by, the way a joiner judges a joint — by fit — and outside them the dish is only a story about itself, and they hold because the standard is written in no cookbook, and the traits above are the kitchen corrections themselves, transcribed at last into print for readers who were not raised within earshot of them to describe the dish loosely would be to lose it, because the dish is its details, and Massachusetts cooks hold those details the way singers hold pitch — not by thinking about them, but by wincing when they are wrong — and the traits above are the wince, transcribed

It lives in Boston's fish houses and across the coast that landed the fish. For the reader who left Massachusetts and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: where it lives rather than where it is sold, because the reference follows the dish into Massachusetts kitchens and gatherings, where its life actually happens, nightly and without advertisement — and beyond that point, picture it where Massachusetts keeps it — in use, on tables, in season, among people who would find the idea of preserving it faintly comic, because from where they sit it is in no danger whatsoever — and then remember that the reference has seen regions forget, and so it writes, and the writing is this entry's whole apology and excuse

Read alongside the other entries of Massachusetts, Baked Scrod shows the method of this whole book: modest and total at once: that it exists, that it is of Massachusetts, that its marks are these, and that the record of it will still be accurate when the reader's grandchildren come to check it — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold Massachusetts at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that Massachusetts food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation A dish like Baked Scrod is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Massachusetts keeps it.
