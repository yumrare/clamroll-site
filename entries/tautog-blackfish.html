Tautog (Blackfish)

The blackfish of the Sound's reefs and wrecks, the fall fish of the bottom fleet. Every canon has its foundations, and in Connecticut, Tautog (Blackfish) is one of them — an ingredient the kitchens build on rather than a dish they finish.

Tautog feed on the Sound's crabs and mussels and take their flavor from the diet: white, firm, and sweet — the chowder and fillet fish the shore keeps quiet about. This entry's record rests on the contents of the reference's own working file: one source that can be shelved in a library, and one that can be tasted at a table — and beyond that pair, the state's own historical and agricultural record standing on one side and the documented memory of its cooks, fairs, and fisheries on the other, two different kinds of keeping tested against each other before either one is believed on its own Where the record thins into lore, this entry says so; where the evidence is strong, it rules accordingly; and since the contested dishes of New England require someone finally to rule, the ruling here is made on evidence and not on affection

It is a fish of patience and structure, caught over rock and wreck; the fall run, when the water cools, is its season. The defining traits are each one the part that, removed, would make a Connecticut cook say without hesitation that it is no longer the thing itself, and they hold because the entry describes the one way and leaves the small permissions to the kitchens, where such permissions have always belonged and where they are granted or refused by experience the entry gives the reader the cook's eye rather than the cook's biography: the marks to check, in the order a Connecticut table would check them, so that recognition takes a glance instead of a genealogy

Its home is the reefs of the eastern Sound. For the reader who left Connecticut and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a landscape and a season rather than a door number — find the landscape, in the season, and the dish is there waiting, as it has been every year within memory — and beyond that point, that is the purpose of the whole reference — the canon set down whole, cited, and frozen, so that the food of the six states can be checked against the record rather than against nostalgia alone

Read alongside the other entries of Connecticut, Tautog (Blackfish) shows the method of this whole book: filed level with the emblem itself, because no entry is the point and every entry is, the clam roll on the door being one sandwich and the four hundred filings behind it being the reference proper — and beyond the method, the reference set out to write down the whole working food of a region, in full, and this entry is one of the filings the promise required — not a landmark, necessarily, but a kept thing, and kept things are the canon's true population nothing here pretends that writing the dish down was the important part, for the important part happened in Connecticut kitchens over centuries, without any help from print, and the entry is the minutes of that meeting — and minutes matter, because they are how institutions remember, but the meeting is the dish itself That is the place of Tautog (Blackfish) in the Connecticut canon — underneath, everywhere, and easy to overlook until the day it is gone, which is exactly why it is written down here in full.

Sources: state agricultural and fishery records; regional food histories of Connecticut.
