Potato Donuts

Cake donuts made with mashed potato in the dough, a Portland bakery tradition turned statewide habit. Potato Donuts is one of the dishes by which Maine knows itself: a food with a birthplace, a season, and a standard that the home cooks enforce without ever writing it down.

Potato donuts use the County's crop to make a donut of unusual tenderness; Portland's bakers made them famous in the 2010s, but farm kitchens had folded potato into donuts for a century. This entry's record rests on a standing rule that a claim appearing in only one place stays out, however good the story, while a claim two independent records support goes in, however plain it looks on the page — and beyond that pair, documentation that supports exactly this much and no more, for Maine documented this one thoroughly, in ink and in practice, and the entry is sized to that evidence Sources are cited so the next person can check the line, and the line here runs through the published record of Maine and through its practiced kitchens, and both sides of it were walked, and both of them held

They are soft, high, and barely sweet under their glazes; the potato is invisible except in the crumb, which stays fresh a day longer than it should. The defining traits are kept the way Maine keeps all its standards — in the corrections of experienced cooks: a little less of this, never that, always the other — and transcribed here at last, and they hold because imitations elsewhere borrow the name and miss the substance described here, and the homesick look for this entry first because it describes the thing itself and never the souvenir of it a description can fail a dish by praising it, and this one refuses the risk, giving Potato Donuts its marks plainly, the way a lighthouse is given its coordinates — because readers need the dish located, not celebrated, and located exactly

They live in Portland's bakeries and in farm kitchens across the state. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the geography of Maine itself, written small, for wherever it lives, it lives because the place produces what the dish needs and the people to insist on it, in season and out — and beyond that point, it is not an artifact behind glass in a museum but a working part of Maine, as current as tonight, findable by anyone who goes where the entry says it lives, and the record and the dish agree about the address, and the agreement is the point of both

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Potato Donuts shows the method of this whole book: the thing the reference says about everything it files — that it was here, that it is documented, and that it belongs to the people who keep it — said here with the same plainness — and beyond the method, nothing in the canon is here because it is fashionable this year, and nothing stands above its neighbors in the filing; the six states are treated as one region with six accents, and this entry is filed under its accent — Maine — where it belongs, and where its own people would look for it first filed, it completes nothing and continues everything, one more dish of the six states set down whole on the page, in a reference whose only ambition is that the whole be there when it is wanted by somebody, and with this filing the whole stands one entry nearer to complete A dish like Potato Donuts is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Maine keeps it.
