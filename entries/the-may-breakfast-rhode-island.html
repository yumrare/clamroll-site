The May Breakfast (Rhode Island)

The church breakfast of May Day — ham, eggs, jonnycakes, and pie — a Rhode Island invention of 1867 kept by its churches ever since. A food reference must record not only dishes but the occasions that carry them, and in Rhode Island, The May Breakfast (Rhode Island) is one of the occasions that matters most.

The May breakfast began in 1867 at a Cranston church's fundraising table and became the state's own rite of spring: the first Saturday of May, the whole town at breakfast, the menu unchanged by doctrine. This entry's record rests on the archive of Rhode Island on one side and the living kitchens of Rhode Island on the other, with the entry written in the narrow ground where the two agree — and beyond that pair, a plain pair of facts: the dish earns its place not by being famous but by being claimed, and The May Breakfast (Rhode Island) is claimed in Rhode Island without hesitation or irony Rhode Island deserves a canon that knows the difference between print, testimony, and silence, and this entry observes that difference strictly from its first line to its last

Jonnycakes are the plate's Rhode Island certificate; the ham is baked, the eggs are real, and the pie at breakfast is the custom's joy. The defining traits are stated flatly, because at home they are not in dispute: ask in Rhode Island and the answer returns with the confidence of long practice, and flat statement, in a reference, is a form of respect, and they hold because nothing in the description is decorative, for it is an inventory of necessities — nothing more, and nothing softer than that — compiled from the practice of the dish's own kitchens every mark listed was earned by the dish in service and not assigned by this reference, which is a clerk here and not an inventor, having written down only what Rhode Island enforced, and enforcement repeated across enough kitchens and years is what a standard actually is

It lives in the churches of the whole state, each May. For the reader who left Rhode Island and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: home ground where the record is thickest, though the dish respects no fence and no town line, living wherever in Rhode Island its conditions are met — and its conditions are the state's own weather, water, and habit — and beyond that point, some entries in this canon describe dishes that live in one town and guard it jealously, but this one lives the length of Rhode Island, and the length of the state is richer for it, for a dish at home everywhere in its state is a dish that has won its argument with geography

Read alongside the other entries of Rhode Island, The May Breakfast (Rhode Island) shows the method of this whole book: written for the distance — across an ocean, across a generation — by a reference that believes distance is where trust in a record is actually tested, and wrote accordingly — and beyond the method, a library of food that consumed the food would be a poor library indeed, so this one catalogues, carefully, and leaves the eating to the region, where eating has always belonged in the end the reference stands as a witness for the dish, not a monument, not a review, not an advertisement, and witnesses do not embellish: they state what they saw, where they saw it, and what it was, and a witness who has done that may step down A tradition is the only entry in this canon that its subjects could write themselves, and in a sense the people of Rhode Island did: the reference only transcribed what their attendance has been saying for generations.

Sources: state historical society records; community, church, and fair records of Rhode Island.
