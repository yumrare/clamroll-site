Vermont · Ingredient

Green Mountain Potato

The famous heirloom potato bred in Vermont in 1878, the baker's potato of the eastern states for a century. Whole stretches of the Vermont table — soups, suppers, shore dinners — are unthinkable without Green Mountain Potato, which is the plainest definition of a foundation ingredient.

O.H. Alexander of Charlotte bred the Green Mountain from a cross of two older kinds; it became the standard American baking and table potato, grown from Maine to the Midwest. This entry's record rests on load-bearing claims standing on two sources, drawn from state records, fair and fishery reports, church and community cookbooks, and the standard regional food histories — and beyond that pair, Vermont's good records and better cooks, and on those two assets this entry rests, and on nothing else at all The sources are named at the foot of the entry, chosen by the standing rule of print where print exists, testimony where print runs out, and silence where neither can honestly be had

It is floury, white-fleshed, and thin-skinned, baking to snow; disease thinned it, but growers keep it for the flavor no russet matches. The defining traits are the ones Vermont's own cooks police, at the stove and at the table, by correction and by example, whenever a hand drifts from the standard, and they hold because inside the tolerances the dish is itself and outside them it is a story about the dish, and the entry keeps to the tolerances exactly as the cooks of Vermont keep to them nightly the description ends where the dish's tolerances end, deliberately, because beyond them lies the pleasant country of variation, which belongs to the cooks of Vermont and not to a frozen record, and the record keeps the center, which is enough for anyone to find the dish again

Charlotte, in Chittenden County, is its birthplace. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the things that hold across decades — what the dish is, where it comes from, and what defines it — rather than the things that rot by spring — and beyond that point, picture it where Vermont keeps it — in use, on tables, in season, among people who would find the idea of preserving it faintly comic, because from where they sit it is in no danger whatsoever — and then remember that the reference has seen regions forget, and so it writes, and the writing is this entry's whole apology and excuse

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, Green Mountain Potato shows the method of this whole book: the record is frozen when it is finished, corrected only for outright error, and this entry is written to that standard from its first line to its last — and beyond the method, nothing in the canon is here because it is fashionable this year, and nothing stands above its neighbors in the filing; the six states are treated as one region with six accents, and this entry is filed under its accent — Vermont — where it belongs, and where its own people would look for it first filed, it completes nothing and continues everything, one more dish of the six states set down whole on the page, in a reference whose only ambition is that the whole be there when it is wanted by somebody, and with this filing the whole stands one entry nearer to complete Nothing in the Vermont canon stands alone, and Green Mountain Potato least of all: it is the entry other entries quietly cite, the shared premise of a hundred dishes, recorded here so the premise is never lost.

Sources: state agricultural and fishery records; regional food histories of Vermont.
