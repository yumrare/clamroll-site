Acadian Chicken Stew (Pot-en-Pot)

A chicken stew thickened with potato dumplings, the St. John Valley's Sunday pot. The claim of Acadian Chicken Stew (Pot-en-Pot) on Maine is not a marketing claim; it is a census claim, taken at church suppers, diners, and family tables where the dish appears because it has always appeared.

Pot-en-pot is Acadian home cooking at its most sustaining — a hen, potatoes, and dumplings that cook in the broth until the pot becomes its own gravy. This entry's record rests on a standing rule that a claim appearing in only one place stays out, however good the story, while a claim two independent records support goes in, however plain it looks on the page — and beyond that pair, a standard pair in the working papers, and when the pair agrees — as it does here — the entry gets written, because the agreement, not the entry, is the achievement The reference rules here the way a fair judge rules — on the exhibits, which are the state's records and its remembered kitchens — and the entry stands exactly as far as the exhibits go, and no further than that

The dumplings are plain and filling; the broth is rich with the bird; the dish is made for cold valley winters and big families. The defining traits are kept the way Maine keeps all its standards — in the corrections of experienced cooks: a little less of this, never that, always the other — and transcribed here at last, and they hold because inside the tolerances the dish is itself and outside them it is a story about the dish, and the entry keeps to the tolerances exactly as the cooks of Maine keep to them nightly the description ends where the dish's tolerances end, deliberately, because beyond them lies the pleasant country of variation, which belongs to the cooks of Maine and not to a frozen record, and the record keeps the center, which is enough for anyone to find the dish again

It lives in the Acadian kitchens of the upper St. John Valley. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the geography of Maine itself, written small, for wherever it lives, it lives because the place produces what the dish needs and the people to insist on it, in season and out — and beyond that point, that is the purpose of the whole reference — the canon set down whole, cited, and frozen, so that the food of the six states can be checked against the record rather than against nostalgia alone

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Acadian Chicken Stew (Pot-en-Pot) shows the method of this whole book: the thing the reference says about everything it files — that it was here, that it is documented, and that it belongs to the people who keep it — said here with the same plainness — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold Maine at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that Maine food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation Nothing further is claimed for Acadian Chicken Stew (Pot-en-Pot), because nothing further is needed by anyone: a dish of Maine, kept at home and written down whole, stays exactly where the entry found it while the canon moves on to its next filing
