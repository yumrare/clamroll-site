Maple Eggnog (Vermont)

Eggnog sweetened with maple, the sugar country's Christmas bowl. The place of Maple Eggnog (Vermont) in Vermont is the place of a habit kept past the point of fashion — which is to say, the place where a region's real food lives.

Where maple is the sweetener, the holiday nog takes maple: the syrup's woodsmoke sweetness suits the nutmeg and the cream better than cane sugar ever argued. This entry's record rests on a standing rule that a claim appearing in only one place stays out, however good the story, while a claim two independent records support goes in, however plain it looks on the page — and beyond that pair, Vermont's good records and better cooks, and on those two assets this entry rests, and on nothing else at all The sources are named at the foot of the entry, chosen by the standing rule of print where print exists, testimony where print runs out, and silence where neither can honestly be had

It is thick, pale, and dangerous in company; the makers serve it in small cups with respect. The defining traits are kept the way Vermont keeps all its standards — in the corrections of experienced cooks: a little less of this, never that, always the other — and transcribed here at last, and they hold because inside the tolerances the dish is itself and outside them it is a story about the dish, and the entry keeps to the tolerances exactly as the cooks of Vermont keep to them nightly the description ends where the dish's tolerances end, deliberately, because beyond them lies the pleasant country of variation, which belongs to the cooks of Vermont and not to a frozen record, and the record keeps the center, which is enough for anyone to find the dish again

It lives at Christmas in the maple country. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the geography of Vermont itself, written small, for wherever it lives, it lives because the place produces what the dish needs and the people to insist on it, in season and out — and beyond that point, some entries in this canon describe dishes that live in one town and guard it jealously, but this one lives the length of Vermont, and the length of the state is richer for it, for a dish at home everywhere in its state is a dish that has won its argument with geography

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, Maple Eggnog (Vermont) shows the method of this whole book: the thing the reference says about everything it files — that it was here, that it is documented, and that it belongs to the people who keep it — said here with the same plainness — and beyond the method, the entry is the region's memory of itself, written by itself and outsourced to print so that forgetting has to work harder to erase it, while the dish, unbothered by its own documentation, goes on being made at home every canon ends up teaching its readers how to read it, and this one teaches a simple lesson through every entry: read for the record, not the rhetoric — a dish, a state, a set of marks, a home ground, with the rhetoric kept on a short leash throughout — so that a reader who finishes knowing the record has read it correctly A dish like Maple Eggnog (Vermont) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Vermont keeps it.
