The Ice Harvest

The winter cutting of lake ice, stacked in ice houses to keep the region's food cold through summer. The food at The Ice Harvest matters, but the gathering is the dish: New Hampshire cooks it communally, serves it communally, and remembers it communally.

Before refrigeration, New Hampshire's lakes were its cold storage: crews cut Winnipesaukee and Squam ice in January, and the ice house stood behind every farm and hotel. This entry's record rests on two independent sources for every claim that carries weight — and beyond that pair, a plain pair of facts: the dish earns its place not by being famous but by being claimed, and The Ice Harvest is claimed in New Hampshire without hesitation or irony New Hampshire deserves a canon that knows the difference between print, testimony, and silence, and this entry observes that difference strictly from its first line to its last

The harvest fed the cold chain that let butter, fish, and cider keep; an icebox in July was a lake in January, remembered. The defining traits are the form described here is the one the home kitchens of New Hampshire recognize, allow no substitutes for, and pass down by correction at the stove from one generation's hands to the next, and they hold because variations exist, as they do for every living dish, but the standard is the standard — the version a New Hampshire cook would set in front of family and call by this name without a word of explanation, and the version against which every variation is quietly measured the traits have been argued at more New Hampshire tables than any entry can count, and the argument itself is evidence — nobody argues the marks of a dish nobody cares about — and the description here is the argument's settled residue, what survived the arguing

It lives in the record of the lake towns, where the ice houses' foundations still stand. For the reader who left New Hampshire and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the dish as it is kept at home, described while home still keeps it, in the kitchens and seasons named above — and beyond that point, there is a kind of map this entry draws without paper — the towns and tables of New Hampshire where the dish belongs, connected by nothing but the dish itself — and repeated across enough towns, the dish becomes a region on the map of the mind, and this entry is one point on that map, fixed so it can be drawn again

Read alongside the other entries of New Hampshire, The Ice Harvest shows the method of this whole book: a dish is admitted on living identity, documented on two sources, and described at the length its record earns, and not one line further — and beyond the method, a library of food that consumed the food would be a poor library indeed, so this one catalogues, carefully, and leaves the eating to the region, where eating has always belonged in the end the reference stands as a witness for the dish, not a monument, not a review, not an advertisement, and witnesses do not embellish: they state what they saw, where they saw it, and what it was, and a witness who has done that may step down A tradition is the only entry in this canon that its subjects could write themselves, and in a sense the people of New Hampshire did: the reference only transcribed what their attendance has been saying for generations.

Sources: state historical society records; community, church, and fair records of New Hampshire.
