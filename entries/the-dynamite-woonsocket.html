The Dynamite (Woonsocket)

A torpedo roll filled with a spicy, long-simmered sauce of ground beef, peppers, and onions — Woonsocket's fiery sandwich. The Dynamite (Woonsocket) sits in the middle of the Rhode Island canon the way a load-bearing wall sits in a house: unglamorous, unthanked, and holding up more than it gets credit for.

The dynamite is the mill city's invention: Franco-American and Italian Woonsocket cooked the sauce for church fundraisers and fairs, and the sandwich — named for its heat — became the city's edible flag. This entry's record rests on the documentary record of Rhode Island — its historical and agricultural archives — laid beside the practiced memory of the cooks who keep the dish, each checked against the other before either is believed alone — and beyond that pair, documentation that supports exactly this much and no more, for Rhode Island documented this one thoroughly, in ink and in practice, and the entry is sized to that evidence Sources are cited so the next person can check the line, and the line here runs through the published record of Rhode Island and through its practiced kitchens, and both sides of it were walked, and both of them held

The sauce cooks for hours into a thick, pepper-hot blanket; the roll must be sturdy, the eating a lean-forward affair. Church suppers still run dynamite nights. The defining traits are the test this entry sets for the genuine article, drawn from the place the dish comes from and from nowhere else on earth, and they hold because imitations elsewhere borrow the name and miss the substance described here, and the homesick look for this entry first because it describes the thing itself and never the souvenir of it a description can fail a dish by praising it, and this one refuses the risk, giving The Dynamite (Woonsocket) its marks plainly, the way a lighthouse is given its coordinates — because readers need the dish located, not celebrated, and located exactly

It lives in Woonsocket, at its fairs, suppers, and sandwich counters. For the reader who left Rhode Island and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: sitting among its equals in the canon, neither lifted nor buried, which is the reference's founding rule: the emblem on the door is one entry among hundreds, and the hundreds are the point of the exercise — and beyond that point, picture it where Rhode Island keeps it — in use, on tables, in season, among people who would find the idea of preserving it faintly comic, because from where they sit it is in no danger whatsoever — and then remember that the reference has seen regions forget, and so it writes, and the writing is this entry's whole apology and excuse

Read alongside the other entries of Rhode Island, The Dynamite (Woonsocket) shows the method of this whole book: in a book named for a sandwich, the temptation is to make every entry a celebration, and this reference resists the temptation as firmly as it resisted it for the emblem itself — affection governing the voice, strictness governing the facts — and beyond the method, the book files by state and treats all states alike, refusing fame, fashion, and the editor's appetite as arrangements, and this entry benefits from the refusal exactly as the emblem does, for in this book, unusually, the footnote and the headline weigh the same the record rests there, not because more could not be said, but because a reference earns its trust by stopping where its evidence stops, and the evidence for this entry stops exactly here, complete and checked If The Dynamite (Woonsocket) vanished from Rhode Island tomorrow, this entry would become its memorial; as long as it is served, the entry is its census. Either way, the record holds.
