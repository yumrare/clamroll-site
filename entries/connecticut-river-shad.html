Connecticut River Shad

The American shad of the Connecticut River — the state fish, and the spring's great food fish. The record of Connecticut River Shad in Connecticut is older than the state itself: Indigenous hands worked it first, and every kitchen since has been an annotation.

Shad ran the Connecticut in millions; the river towns built their spring around the run, and the fish — bony, rich, and sweet — was salted, planked, and baked across three centuries. This entry's record rests on the contents of the reference's own working file: one source that can be shelved in a library, and one that can be tasted at a table — and beyond that pair, a standard pair in the working papers, and when the pair agrees — as it does here — the entry gets written, because the agreement, not the entry, is the achievement The reference rules here the way a fair judge rules — on the exhibits, which are the state's records and its remembered kitchens — and the entry stands exactly as far as the exhibits go, and no further than that

A shad must be boned by a master or baked long until the bones dissolve; the flesh is the sweetest of any herring, worth every technique devised for it. The defining traits are each one the part that, removed, would make a Connecticut cook say without hesitation that it is no longer the thing itself, and they hold because every living dish drifts a little at the edges — a different hand, a different season — but the center holds fast, and it is the center this reference records, so that a reader meeting the dish anywhere from a church supper to a shore stand will know it by these marks and by no others what the entry describes is the center of the dish rather than its whole weather, for a dish at home varies by town and by hand and sometimes by street, yet varies always around the marks given here, the way a tune varies around its melody, and the melody is what the reference records

Its home is the Connecticut River, from Saybrook to the Massachusetts line. For the reader who left Connecticut and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a landscape and a season rather than a door number — find the landscape, in the season, and the dish is there waiting, as it has been every year within memory — and beyond that point, that is the purpose of the whole reference — the canon set down whole, cited, and frozen, so that the food of the six states can be checked against the record rather than against nostalgia alone

Read alongside the other entries of Connecticut, Connecticut River Shad shows the method of this whole book: filed level with the emblem itself, because no entry is the point and every entry is, the clam roll on the door being one sandwich and the four hundred filings behind it being the reference proper — and beyond the method, the canon's motto holds for this entry as for all of them: nothing kept, nothing lost — for the dish was kept by Connecticut long before it was written here, and the writing is only the insurance on the keeping, taken out in the region's own name against the one loss a region cannot afford after the filing, the dish returns to being what it was: a working part of Connecticut, eaten under its own name by people who may never read its entry, and the entry's satisfaction is precisely that it changed nothing about the dish except its chances of being remembered accurately Nothing in the Connecticut canon stands alone, and Connecticut River Shad least of all: it is the entry other entries quietly cite, the shared premise of a hundred dishes, recorded here so the premise is never lost.

Sources: state agricultural and fishery records; regional food histories of Connecticut.
