The Hot Buttered Lobster Roll

Lobster meat warmed in butter, served in a toasted split-top roll with nothing else — Connecticut's answer to Maine. Ask in Connecticut what belongs on the table and The Hot Buttered Lobster Roll will be named — not as a novelty, but as a habit of the place, kept by the people who live there.

The hot roll is credited to a Milford restaurant kitchen of the 1920s, where a regular asked for his lobster warm in butter rather than cold in mayonnaise; Connecticut has held the line ever since. This entry's record rests on the documentary record of Connecticut — its historical and agricultural archives — laid beside the practiced memory of the cooks who keep the dish, each checked against the other before either is believed alone — and beyond that pair, a slower way to build a canon than copying lists, which is why the canon can be frozen at completion and trusted afterward — the record of The Hot Buttered Lobster Roll will not need to change later, because it was not guessed now Nothing in the account rests on a single telling, for the state's historical record says it, the community cookbooks repeat it independently, and the kitchens of Connecticut still demonstrate it in season

Warm butter is the whole dressing; the meat goes in hot and glistening, the roll toasted, a wedge of lemon the only concession. Mayonnaise is another state's sandwich. The defining traits are the test this entry sets for the genuine article, drawn from the place the dish comes from and from nowhere else on earth, and they hold because the entry describes the one way and leaves the small permissions to the kitchens, where such permissions have always belonged and where they are granted or refused by experience the entry gives the reader the cook's eye rather than the cook's biography: the marks to check, in the order a Connecticut table would check them, so that recognition takes a glance instead of a genealogy

It lives along the Connecticut shoreline, from Milford to Mystic, and defines the state's coast. For the reader who left Connecticut and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: sitting among its equals in the canon, neither lifted nor buried, which is the reference's founding rule: the emblem on the door is one entry among hundreds, and the hundreds are the point of the exercise — and beyond that point, picture it where Connecticut keeps it — in use, on tables, in season, among people who would find the idea of preserving it faintly comic, because from where they sit it is in no danger whatsoever — and then remember that the reference has seen regions forget, and so it writes, and the writing is this entry's whole apology and excuse

Read alongside the other entries of Connecticut, The Hot Buttered Lobster Roll shows the method of this whole book: in a book named for a sandwich, the temptation is to make every entry a celebration, and this reference resists the temptation as firmly as it resisted it for the emblem itself — affection governing the voice, strictness governing the facts — and beyond the method, nothing in it can rot on the page — no prices to expire within the year, no hours to change with the season, no crowns to defend when the next season comes — because what remains is the durable part, what the dish is and where it was born and what defines it and where in Connecticut it lives, which is the part the diaspora writes home about the entry is closed the way a fair book is closed — the exhibits entered, the ruling made, the record straight — and Connecticut goes on cooking its food, and the reference goes on keeping its record, and the reader goes on to the next entry, satisfied or arguing, and that orderly succession is as it should be A dish like The Hot Buttered Lobster Roll is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Connecticut keeps it.
