Hasty Pudding (Connecticut)

Boiled cornmeal mush, eaten with milk and maple or fried in slices. Open any honest account of Connecticut eating and Hasty Pudding (Connecticut) is in it — not featured, not celebrated, just present, the way the furniture of a kitchen is present.

The valley's hasty pudding follows the colonial form; Connecticut's telling fries the leftovers for breakfast under maple syrup, the state's own finish. This entry's record rests on the documentary record of Connecticut — its historical and agricultural archives — laid beside the practiced memory of the cooks who keep the dish, each checked against the other before either is believed alone — and beyond that pair, the state's own historical and agricultural record standing on one side and the documented memory of its cooks, fairs, and fisheries on the other, two different kinds of keeping tested against each other before either one is believed on its own Where the record thins into lore, this entry says so; where the evidence is strong, it rules accordingly; and since the contested dishes of New England require someone finally to rule, the ruling here is made on evidence and not on affection

It is the quickest dish in this canon and among the oldest — meal, water, salt, and the stirrer's patience. The defining traits are the test this entry sets for the genuine article, drawn from the place the dish comes from and from nowhere else on earth, and they hold because inside the tolerances the dish is itself and outside them it is a story about the dish, and the entry keeps to the tolerances exactly as the cooks of Connecticut keep to them nightly the description ends where the dish's tolerances end, deliberately, because beyond them lies the pleasant country of variation, which belongs to the cooks of Connecticut and not to a frozen record, and the record keeps the center, which is enough for anyone to find the dish again

It lives in the colonial record of the valley farms. For the reader who left Connecticut and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: sitting among its equals in the canon, neither lifted nor buried, which is the reference's founding rule: the emblem on the door is one entry among hundreds, and the hundreds are the point of the exercise — and beyond that point, geography, for such a dish, is destiny with better scenery — it lives in Connecticut because Connecticut is the kind of place that produces it, its waters and its winters and its stubbornness about doing things the kept way — and the entry therefore leaves both the dish and its meaning exactly where it found them

Read alongside the other entries of Connecticut, Hasty Pudding (Connecticut) shows the method of this whole book: in a book named for a sandwich, the temptation is to make every entry a celebration, and this reference resists the temptation as firmly as it resisted it for the emblem itself — affection governing the voice, strictness governing the facts — and beyond the method, nothing in it can rot on the page — no prices to expire within the year, no hours to change with the season, no crowns to defend when the next season comes — because what remains is the durable part, what the dish is and where it was born and what defines it and where in Connecticut it lives, which is the part the diaspora writes home about the entry is closed the way a fair book is closed — the exhibits entered, the ruling made, the record straight — and Connecticut goes on cooking its food, and the reference goes on keeping its record, and the reader goes on to the next entry, satisfied or arguing, and that orderly succession is as it should be A dish like Hasty Pudding (Connecticut) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Connecticut keeps it.
