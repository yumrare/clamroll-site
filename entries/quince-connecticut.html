Quince (Connecticut)

The quince of the colonial orchard — inedible raw, transformed by cooking into the old preserves and pies. In Connecticut, Quince (Connecticut) is less an ingredient than a fact of the landscape — the given that the cooks of four centuries have worked with and around.

Every colonial Connecticut orchard kept a quince tree for the preserves, the jelly, and the quince pie; the fruit's perfume filled the fall kitchen for a week at the making. This entry's record rests on the documentary record of Connecticut — its historical and agricultural archives — laid beside the practiced memory of the cooks who keep the dish, each checked against the other before either is believed alone — and beyond that pair, the state's own historical and agricultural record standing on one side and the documented memory of its cooks, fairs, and fisheries on the other, two different kinds of keeping tested against each other before either one is believed on its own Where the record thins into lore, this entry says so; where the evidence is strong, it rules accordingly; and since the contested dishes of New England require someone finally to rule, the ruling here is made on evidence and not on affection

Cooked, the quince turns rose-colored and fragrant beyond any apple; its jelly set without help, a gift of its pectin. The defining traits are the test this entry sets for the genuine article, drawn from the place the dish comes from and from nowhere else on earth, and they hold because a portrait rather than a eulogy, because the dish is alive in Connecticut today and still made to this standard by living hands, and the standard is checkable at any table where the dish is served under its own name — a check the entry not only allows but invites the marks are the dish's tolerances and its testimony at once: the tolerances say how far it may bend, the testimony says it has not broken, and both are matters of record in Connecticut rather than matters of opinion anywhere

It lives in the old orchards and the preserving kitchens of the state. For the reader who left Connecticut and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: sitting among its equals in the canon, neither lifted nor buried, which is the reference's founding rule: the emblem on the door is one entry among hundreds, and the hundreds are the point of the exercise — and beyond that point, the homesick reader will read the home ground the way earlier generations read letters from home — slowly, and more than once — and the entry was written to bear that kind of reading, naming the ground plainly and refusing to embroider the naming, because plain naming, repeated kindly, is the whole comfort on offer here

Read alongside the other entries of Connecticut, Quince (Connecticut) shows the method of this whole book: in a book named for a sandwich, the temptation is to make every entry a celebration, and this reference resists the temptation as firmly as it resisted it for the emblem itself — affection governing the voice, strictness governing the facts — and beyond the method, a library of food that consumed the food would be a poor library indeed, so this one catalogues, carefully, and leaves the eating to the region, where eating has always belonged in the end the reference stands as a witness for the dish, not a monument, not a review, not an advertisement, and witnesses do not embellish: they state what they saw, where they saw it, and what it was, and a witness who has done that may step down An ingredient like Quince (Connecticut) does quiet work: it decides the taste of a hundred Connecticut dishes before any of them has a name, and the reference records it so the foundation is as documented as the dishes built on it.
