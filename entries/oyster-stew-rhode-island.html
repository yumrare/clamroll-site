Oyster Stew (Rhode Island)

Bay oysters in a buttered milk stew, the oyster houses' Christmas bowl. There are dishes a state is famous for and dishes a state actually eats, and Oyster Stew (Rhode Island) is the second kind — which, in Rhode Island, is the only kind this reference counts.

When the bay's oysters fed the nation, the stew was Providence's Christmas Eve custom; the revived beds have brought the bowl back to the holiday table. This entry's record rests on the documentary record of Rhode Island — its historical and agricultural archives — laid beside the practiced memory of the cooks who keep the dish, each checked against the other before either is believed alone — and beyond that pair, the state's own historical and agricultural record standing on one side and the documented memory of its cooks, fairs, and fisheries on the other, two different kinds of keeping tested against each other before either one is believed on its own Where the record thins into lore, this entry says so; where the evidence is strong, it rules accordingly; and since the contested dishes of New England require someone finally to rule, the ruling here is made on evidence and not on affection

The oysters poach a minute in their liquor and milk; the butter rises; the bowl is eaten in silence first. The defining traits are the test this entry sets for the genuine article, drawn from the place the dish comes from and from nowhere else on earth, and they hold because the entry describes the one way and leaves the small permissions to the kitchens, where such permissions have always belonged and where they are granted or refused by experience the entry gives the reader the cook's eye rather than the cook's biography: the marks to check, in the order a Rhode Island table would check them, so that recognition takes a glance instead of a genealogy

It lives in Providence and the bay's oyster towns. For the reader who left Rhode Island and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: sitting among its equals in the canon, neither lifted nor buried, which is the reference's founding rule: the emblem on the door is one entry among hundreds, and the hundreds are the point of the exercise — and beyond that point, there is a kind of map this entry draws without paper — the towns and tables of Rhode Island where the dish belongs, connected by nothing but the dish itself — and repeated across enough towns, the dish becomes a region on the map of the mind, and this entry is one point on that map, fixed so it can be drawn again

Read alongside the other entries of Rhode Island, Oyster Stew (Rhode Island) shows the method of this whole book: in a book named for a sandwich, the temptation is to make every entry a celebration, and this reference resists the temptation as firmly as it resisted it for the emblem itself — affection governing the voice, strictness governing the facts — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold Rhode Island at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that Rhode Island food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation A dish like Oyster Stew (Rhode Island) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Rhode Island keeps it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Rhode Island.
