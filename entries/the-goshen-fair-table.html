The Goshen Fair Table

The food of the Goshen Fair — the Litchfield hills' harvest judged, baked, and supped upon. Every region keeps a few occasions it would no more skip than winter, and in Connecticut, The Goshen Fair Table is one of them — kept by towns, churches, and families acting in unison.

The Goshen Fair has closed the Litchfield summer for a century; its baking and preserving classes are the hills' annual examination, and its supper tents feed the county. This entry's record rests on the documentary record of Connecticut — its historical and agricultural archives — laid beside the practiced memory of the cooks who keep the dish, each checked against the other before either is believed alone — and beyond that pair, an entry that claims nothing its sources do not jointly support, written under a discipline that, applied four hundred times, is the reference itself A reader checking the entry against the archive will find it conservative throughout — claiming less than the lore claims, documenting more than the lists document — and that ratio, less claimed and more proven, is the reference's whole method in miniature

A blue ribbon at Goshen for pie or preserves is a lifetime standing; the fair's tables carry the whole farm canon of the northwest hills. The defining traits are the test this entry sets for the genuine article, drawn from the place the dish comes from and from nowhere else on earth, and they hold because imitations elsewhere borrow the name and miss the substance described here, and the homesick look for this entry first because it describes the thing itself and never the souvenir of it a description can fail a dish by praising it, and this one refuses the risk, giving The Goshen Fair Table its marks plainly, the way a lighthouse is given its coordinates — because readers need the dish located, not celebrated, and located exactly

It lives in Goshen each Labor Day weekend. For the reader who left Connecticut and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: sitting among its equals in the canon, neither lifted nor buried, which is the reference's founding rule: the emblem on the door is one entry among hundreds, and the hundreds are the point of the exercise — and beyond that point, entries carry no prices, no hours, and no league tables of dishes, because those facts rot and the record should not; the rest is the reader's own journey home, and this book exists to shorten that journey back to Connecticut by exactly one homesickness

Read alongside the other entries of Connecticut, The Goshen Fair Table shows the method of this whole book: in a book named for a sandwich, the temptation is to make every entry a celebration, and this reference resists the temptation as firmly as it resisted it for the emblem itself — affection governing the voice, strictness governing the facts — and beyond the method, nothing in it can rot on the page — no prices to expire within the year, no hours to change with the season, no crowns to defend when the next season comes — because what remains is the durable part, what the dish is and where it was born and what defines it and where in Connecticut it lives, which is the part the diaspora writes home about the entry is closed the way a fair book is closed — the exhibits entered, the ruling made, the record straight — and Connecticut goes on cooking its food, and the reference goes on keeping its record, and the reader goes on to the next entry, satisfied or arguing, and that orderly succession is as it should be A tradition is the only entry in this canon that its subjects could write themselves, and in a sense the people of Connecticut did: the reference only transcribed what their attendance has been saying for generations.

Sources: state historical society records; community, church, and fair records of Connecticut.
