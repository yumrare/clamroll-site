Vermont Applesauce and Cider Sauce

Apples cooked down to sauce — and, in the oldest form, boiled with cider to a dark, keeping sauce. Vermont Applesauce and Cider Sauce is filed under Vermont because that is where its people look for it, and where a stranger asking for it by name would be understood without a word of explanation.

Sauce is the orchard's overflow made useful; cider sauce, reduced and spiced, was the farm's winter sauce for pork and puddings before the freezer changed everything. This entry's record rests on load-bearing claims standing on two sources, drawn from state records, fair and fishery reports, church and community cookbooks, and the standard regional food histories — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

A fresh sauce is pale and bright; the cider sauce is mahogany and concentrated, tasting of the press and the kettle at once. The defining traits are the ones Vermont's own cooks police, at the stove and at the table, by correction and by example, whenever a hand drifts from the standard, and they hold because inside the tolerances the dish is itself and outside them it is a story about the dish, and the entry keeps to the tolerances exactly as the cooks of Vermont keep to them nightly the description ends where the dish's tolerances end, deliberately, because beyond them lies the pleasant country of variation, which belongs to the cooks of Vermont and not to a frozen record, and the record keeps the center, which is enough for anyone to find the dish again

It lives in the orchard towns of the state. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the things that hold across decades — what the dish is, where it comes from, and what defines it — rather than the things that rot by spring — and beyond that point, that is the purpose of the whole reference — the canon set down whole, cited, and frozen, so that the food of the six states can be checked against the record rather than against nostalgia alone

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, Vermont Applesauce and Cider Sauce shows the method of this whole book: the record is frozen when it is finished, corrected only for outright error, and this entry is written to that standard from its first line to its last — and beyond the method, nothing in the canon is here because it is fashionable this year, and nothing stands above its neighbors in the filing; the six states are treated as one region with six accents, and this entry is filed under its accent — Vermont — where it belongs, and where its own people would look for it first filed, it completes nothing and continues everything, one more dish of the six states set down whole on the page, in a reference whose only ambition is that the whole be there when it is wanted by somebody, and with this filing the whole stands one entry nearer to complete A dish like Vermont Applesauce and Cider Sauce is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Vermont keeps it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Vermont.
