Black Oxford Apple

A deep purple-black heirloom apple from Oxford County, a keeper apple of the old orchards. An outsider reads the Maine canon and sees dishes; a Maine cook reads it and sees Black Oxford Apple underneath a third of them, holding the whole structure level.

The Black Oxford was found in Paris, Maine, in the early 1800s and became the region's winter apple — picked in fall, eaten at Christmas, still sound in spring. This entry's record rests on a standing rule that a claim appearing in only one place stays out, however good the story, while a claim two independent records support goes in, however plain it looks on the page — and beyond that pair, documentation that supports exactly this much and no more, for Maine documented this one thoroughly, in ink and in practice, and the entry is sized to that evidence Sources are cited so the next person can check the line, and the line here runs through the published record of Maine and through its practiced kitchens, and both sides of it were walked, and both of them held

It is dense, sweet-tart, and slow to spoil; its skin is so dark it looks polished, and its flesh keeps a snap months into storage. The defining traits are kept the way Maine keeps all its standards — in the corrections of experienced cooks: a little less of this, never that, always the other — and transcribed here at last, and they hold because a portrait rather than a eulogy, because the dish is alive in Maine today and still made to this standard by living hands, and the standard is checkable at any table where the dish is served under its own name — a check the entry not only allows but invites the marks are the dish's tolerances and its testimony at once: the tolerances say how far it may bend, the testimony says it has not broken, and both are matters of record in Maine rather than matters of opinion anywhere

Oxford County is its birthplace; old trees still stand in western Maine. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the geography of Maine itself, written small, for wherever it lives, it lives because the place produces what the dish needs and the people to insist on it, in season and out — and beyond that point, where it lives, it lives unselfconsciously — ordered, served, and eaten under its own name, without ceremony — by people who would be surprised to learn it needed an entry at all, and that surprise is the surest sign the entry was needed, because what a place takes for granted is exactly what the record must catch before it slips
