Oyster Stew (Connecticut)

Sound oysters poached in milk and butter, the oyster towns' Christmas Eve bowl. In the canon of Connecticut, Oyster Stew (Connecticut) stands by the test this reference applies to every dish: the people of the region claim it as theirs, today, by name.

The stew is the oyster trade's home dish — the shuckers' and captains' families ate the harvest's first quality in this simplest form. This entry's record rests on the documentary record of Connecticut — its historical and agricultural archives — laid beside the practiced memory of the cooks who keep the dish, each checked against the other before either is believed alone — and beyond that pair, a slower way to build a canon than copying lists, which is why the canon can be frozen at completion and trusted afterward — the record of Oyster Stew (Connecticut) will not need to change later, because it was not guessed now Nothing in the account rests on a single telling, for the state's historical record says it, the community cookbooks repeat it independently, and the kitchens of Connecticut still demonstrate it in season

It is made in minutes and eaten at once; the oysters curl, the butter rises, and the bowl is the whole celebration. The defining traits are the test this entry sets for the genuine article, drawn from the place the dish comes from and from nowhere else on earth, and they hold because every living dish drifts a little at the edges — a different hand, a different season — but the center holds fast, and it is the center this reference records, so that a reader meeting the dish anywhere from a church supper to a shore stand will know it by these marks and by no others what the entry describes is the center of the dish rather than its whole weather, for a dish at home varies by town and by hand and sometimes by street, yet varies always around the marks given here, the way a tune varies around its melody, and the melody is what the reference records

It lives in Norwalk and the oyster towns of the western Sound. For the reader who left Connecticut and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: sitting among its equals in the canon, neither lifted nor buried, which is the reference's founding rule: the emblem on the door is one entry among hundreds, and the hundreds are the point of the exercise — and beyond that point, picture it where Connecticut keeps it — in use, on tables, in season, among people who would find the idea of preserving it faintly comic, because from where they sit it is in no danger whatsoever — and then remember that the reference has seen regions forget, and so it writes, and the writing is this entry's whole apology and excuse

Read alongside the other entries of Connecticut, Oyster Stew (Connecticut) shows the method of this whole book: in a book named for a sandwich, the temptation is to make every entry a celebration, and this reference resists the temptation as firmly as it resisted it for the emblem itself — affection governing the voice, strictness governing the facts — and beyond the method, nothing in the canon is here because it is fashionable this year, and nothing stands above its neighbors in the filing; the six states are treated as one region with six accents, and this entry is filed under its accent — Connecticut — where it belongs, and where its own people would look for it first filed, it completes nothing and continues everything, one more dish of the six states set down whole on the page, in a reference whose only ambition is that the whole be there when it is wanted by somebody, and with this filing the whole stands one entry nearer to complete A dish like Oyster Stew (Connecticut) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Connecticut keeps it.
