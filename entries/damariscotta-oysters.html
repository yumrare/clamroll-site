Damariscotta Oysters

Oysters from the Damariscotta River, grown over shell middens left by Wabanaki feasts centuries deep. The record of Damariscotta Oysters in Maine is older than the state itself: Indigenous hands worked it first, and every kitchen since has been an annotation.

The great oyster shell heaps of the Damariscotta are among the oldest feast records in New England; modern growers farm the same river, and its oysters are prized for a clean, cold brine. This entry's record rests on load-bearing claims standing on two sources, drawn from state records, fair and fishery reports, church and community cookbooks, and the standard regional food histories — and beyond that pair, the state's own historical and agricultural record standing on one side and the documented memory of its cooks, fairs, and fisheries on the other, two different kinds of keeping tested against each other before either one is believed on its own Where the record thins into lore, this entry says so; where the evidence is strong, it rules accordingly; and since the contested dishes of New England require someone finally to rule, the ruling here is made on evidence and not on affection

They are firm, sweet, and mineral — a river oyster with a sea finish — eaten raw with nothing or nearly nothing. The defining traits are the ones Maine's own cooks police, at the stove and at the table, by correction and by example, whenever a hand drifts from the standard, and they hold because the standard is written in no cookbook, and the traits above are the kitchen corrections themselves, transcribed at last into print for readers who were not raised within earshot of them to describe the dish loosely would be to lose it, because the dish is its details, and Maine cooks hold those details the way singers hold pitch — not by thinking about them, but by wincing when they are wrong — and the traits above are the wince, transcribed

The Damariscotta River, between Newcastle and Edgecomb, is their home. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the things that hold across decades — what the dish is, where it comes from, and what defines it — rather than the things that rot by spring — and beyond that point, geography, for such a dish, is destiny with better scenery — it lives in Maine because Maine is the kind of place that produces it, its waters and its winters and its stubbornness about doing things the kept way — and the entry therefore leaves both the dish and its meaning exactly where it found them

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Damariscotta Oysters shows the method of this whole book: the record is frozen when it is finished, corrected only for outright error, and this entry is written to that standard from its first line to its last — and beyond the method, nothing in the canon is here because it is fashionable this year, and nothing stands above its neighbors in the filing; the six states are treated as one region with six accents, and this entry is filed under its accent — Maine — where it belongs, and where its own people would look for it first filed, it completes nothing and continues everything, one more dish of the six states set down whole on the page, in a reference whose only ambition is that the whole be there when it is wanted by somebody, and with this filing the whole stands one entry nearer to complete What Damariscotta Oysters asks of the reader is only attention: the next Maine dish that tastes unmistakably of its place is probably tasting of this, and now the reader knows its name and its ground.

Sources: state agricultural and fishery records; regional food histories of Maine.
