Fiddleheads (Vermont)

Ostrich fern fiddleheads from Vermont's riverbanks, the spring's first green. Fiddleheads (Vermont) is one of the dishes by which Vermont knows itself: a food with a birthplace, a season, and a standard that the home cooks enforce without ever writing it down.

Vermont's rivers — the Connecticut, the Winooski, the Lamoille — run fern banks as Maine's do, and the fiddlehead season opens the foraging year in the same three-week rush. They are boiled or steamed, buttered, and eaten hot; the state's cooks argue lemon against vinegar as the finish. The defining traits are deliberately narrow, because the dish is narrow: one settled way of making it, and a hundred small permissions around the edges of that way, and they hold because variations exist, as they do for every living dish, but the standard is the standard — the version a Vermont cook would set in front of family and call by this name without a word of explanation, and the version against which every variation is quietly measured the traits have been argued at more Vermont tables than any entry can count, and the argument itself is evidence — nobody argues the marks of a dish nobody cares about — and the description here is the argument's settled residue, what survived the arguing

They live along the rivers of the whole state. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: Vermont's calendar rather than this book's directions, for the dish keeps its season faithfully, and in its season it is not hidden at all — it is simply there, where it has always been, waiting to be recognized rather than discovered — and beyond that point, some entries in this canon describe dishes that live in one town and guard it jealously, but this one lives the length of Vermont, and the length of the state is richer for it, for a dish at home everywhere in its state is a dish that has won its argument with geography
