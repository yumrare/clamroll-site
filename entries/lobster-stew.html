Lobster Stew

Lobster meat simmered in milk and butter, rested overnight so the flavor marries, and reheated gently. A visitor could eat through Maine for a month and miss Lobster Stew entirely, which says nothing about the dish and everything about the visitor; the locals missed nothing.

Lobster stew is the shore family's way with a surplus — simpler than bisque, richer than chowder, and always better the second day, a rule every Maine cook recites. This entry's record rests on a standing rule that a claim appearing in only one place stays out, however good the story, while a claim two independent records support goes in, however plain it looks on the page — and beyond that pair, a plain pair of facts: the dish earns its place not by being famous but by being claimed, and Lobster Stew is claimed in Maine without hesitation or irony Maine deserves a canon that knows the difference between print, testimony, and silence, and this entry observes that difference strictly from its first line to its last

It is only lobster, milk, butter, and seasoning; the liquor from the shells colors it coral and the meat stays in generous pieces. It must never boil. The defining traits are kept the way Maine keeps all its standards — in the corrections of experienced cooks: a little less of this, never that, always the other — and transcribed here at last, and they hold because inside the tolerances the dish is itself and outside them it is a story about the dish, and the entry keeps to the tolerances exactly as the cooks of Maine keep to them nightly the description ends where the dish's tolerances end, deliberately, because beyond them lies the pleasant country of variation, which belongs to the cooks of Maine and not to a frozen record, and the record keeps the center, which is enough for anyone to find the dish again

It lives in the home kitchens of the coast, at church suppers and family tables from Portland to Lubec. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the geography of Maine itself, written small, for wherever it lives, it lives because the place produces what the dish needs and the people to insist on it, in season and out — and beyond that point, there is a kind of map this entry draws without paper — the towns and tables of Maine where the dish belongs, connected by nothing but the dish itself — and repeated across enough towns, the dish becomes a region on the map of the mind, and this entry is one point on that map, fixed so it can be drawn again

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Lobster Stew shows the method of this whole book: the thing the reference says about everything it files — that it was here, that it is documented, and that it belongs to the people who keep it — said here with the same plainness — and beyond the method, the canon's motto holds for this entry as for all of them: nothing kept, nothing lost — for the dish was kept by Maine long before it was written here, and the writing is only the insurance on the keeping, taken out in the region's own name against the one loss a region cannot afford after the filing, the dish returns to being what it was: a working part of Maine, eaten under its own name by people who may never read its entry, and the entry's satisfaction is precisely that it changed nothing about the dish except its chances of being remembered accurately The last word on Lobster Stew is the simplest: it endures. Dishes survive in Maine because people keep choosing them, and the choosing, repeated across enough tables and enough years, is the whole of the evidence.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Maine.
