The Submarine Sandwich (New London Claim)

The long sandwich itself, for which New London's naval shore holds a naming claim. There are dishes a state is famous for and dishes a state actually eats, and The Submarine Sandwich (New London Claim) is the second kind — which, in Connecticut, is the only kind this reference counts.

The submarine sandwich's name is contested along the whole East Coast; New London's claim rests on its submarine base — the sandwich, by the telling, fed the sailors and took the boat's name in the Second World War era. This entry's record rests on the contents of the reference's own working file: one source that can be shelved in a library, and one that can be tasted at a table — and beyond that pair, documentation that supports exactly this much and no more, for Connecticut documented this one thoroughly, in ink and in practice, and the entry is sized to that evidence Sources are cited so the next person can check the line, and the line here runs through the published record of Connecticut and through its practiced kitchens, and both sides of it were walked, and both of them held

Whatever the name's port of origin, the form is fixed: the long roll, the layered cold cuts, the oil. Connecticut files its claim and eats the sandwich either way. The defining traits are each one the part that, removed, would make a Connecticut cook say without hesitation that it is no longer the thing itself, and they hold because inside the tolerances the dish is itself and outside them it is a story about the dish, and the entry keeps to the tolerances exactly as the cooks of Connecticut keep to them nightly the description ends where the dish's tolerances end, deliberately, because beyond them lies the pleasant country of variation, which belongs to the cooks of Connecticut and not to a frozen record, and the record keeps the center, which is enough for anyone to find the dish again

It lives in New London and Groton, in the shadow of the base. For the reader who left Connecticut and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: a landscape and a season rather than a door number — find the landscape, in the season, and the dish is there waiting, as it has been every year within memory — and beyond that point, some entries in this canon describe dishes that live in one town and guard it jealously, but this one lives the length of Connecticut, and the length of the state is richer for it, for a dish at home everywhere in its state is a dish that has won its argument with geography

Read alongside the other entries of Connecticut, The Submarine Sandwich (New London Claim) shows the method of this whole book: filed level with the emblem itself, because no entry is the point and every entry is, the clam roll on the door being one sandwich and the four hundred filings behind it being the reference proper — and beyond the method, the homesick reader this book is written for does not need to be sold Connecticut at all; that reader needs the dish described exactly as it is kept at home, so that memory can be checked against something steadier than longing, and that need is the one this entry was built to answer the filing is one of four hundred small acts of faith in the same proposition — that Connecticut food is worth recording exactly, in a country that records almost nothing exactly — and the proposition does not argue itself in any single entry but accumulates, entry by entry, and this is one unit of the accumulation A dish like The Submarine Sandwich (New London Claim) is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Connecticut keeps it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Connecticut.
