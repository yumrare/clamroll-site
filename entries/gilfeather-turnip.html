Gilfeather Turnip

A sweet rutabaga-turnip cross from Wardsboro — the official vegetable of the state of Vermont. The kitchens of Vermont did not choose Gilfeather Turnip so much as inherit it — the place offered it in abundance, and four centuries of cooks learned what to do with abundance.

The Gilfeather was bred in Wardsboro in the late 1800s by a farmer who guarded his seed like a state secret; Vermont repaid the turnip by naming it the state vegetable in 2016. This entry's record rests on the house rule that two sources support every load-bearing claim, or the claim does not appear in the entry at all — and beyond that pair, a plain pair of facts: the dish earns its place not by being famous but by being claimed, and Gilfeather Turnip is claimed in Vermont without hesitation or irony Vermont deserves a canon that knows the difference between print, testimony, and silence, and this entry observes that difference strictly from its first line to its last

It is mild, sweet, and white-fleshed, without the turnip's bite — it eats like the rutabaga's kinder sibling and sweetens after frost. The defining traits are deliberately narrow, because the dish is narrow: one settled way of making it, and a hundred small permissions around the edges of that way, and they hold because the standard is written in no cookbook, and the traits above are the kitchen corrections themselves, transcribed at last into print for readers who were not raised within earshot of them to describe the dish loosely would be to lose it, because the dish is its details, and Vermont cooks hold those details the way singers hold pitch — not by thinking about them, but by wincing when they are wrong — and the traits above are the wince, transcribed

Wardsboro is its hometown; it lives in the gardens of the whole state. For the reader who left Vermont and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: Vermont's calendar rather than this book's directions, for the dish keeps its season faithfully, and in its season it is not hidden at all — it is simply there, where it has always been, waiting to be recognized rather than discovered — and beyond that point, there is a kind of map this entry draws without paper — the towns and tables of Vermont where the dish belongs, connected by nothing but the dish itself — and repeated across enough towns, the dish becomes a region on the map of the mind, and this entry is one point on that map, fixed so it can be drawn again

Read alongside the other entries of Vermont, Gilfeather Turnip shows the method of this whole book: returned to its people, because the reference keeps only the description, carefully, and gives the dish back to Vermont exactly as it found it, no better and no worse than it was — and beyond the method, the canon's motto holds for this entry as for all of them: nothing kept, nothing lost — for the dish was kept by Vermont long before it was written here, and the writing is only the insurance on the keeping, taken out in the region's own name against the one loss a region cannot afford after the filing, the dish returns to being what it was: a working part of Vermont, eaten under its own name by people who may never read its entry, and the entry's satisfaction is precisely that it changed nothing about the dish except its chances of being remembered accurately If the dishes of Vermont are the region's sentences, Gilfeather Turnip is one of its oldest words — and the reference, which exists to keep the language, keeps the word with its meaning attached.

Sources: state agricultural and fishery records; regional food histories of Vermont.
