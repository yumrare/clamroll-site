Sea Moss Pudding

A blancmange set with Irish moss seaweed, the old shore dessert of the Maine coast. Ask in Maine what belongs on the table and Sea Moss Pudding will be named — not as a novelty, but as a habit of the place, kept by the people who live there.

Irish moss, gathered at low tide and dried on sheets, was the coast's gelatin before the box kind; the pudding it set — milk, vanilla, a little sugar — was the shore's delicate dessert. This entry's record rests on a standing rule that a claim appearing in only one place stays out, however good the story, while a claim two independent records support goes in, however plain it looks on the page — and beyond that pair, the region's own evidence: the written record the state kept of itself, and the unwritten record its cooks keep nightly, and the entry stands where the two records overlap The evidence was weighed the way this reference weighs everything: twice-found claims in, single-source stories out, and the weighing done before the writing rather than after it

It is barely set, trembling, and white; served with berries or maple, it tastes of the tidy, thrifty shore kitchen that made it. The defining traits are kept the way Maine keeps all its standards — in the corrections of experienced cooks: a little less of this, never that, always the other — and transcribed here at last, and they hold because a portrait rather than a eulogy, because the dish is alive in Maine today and still made to this standard by living hands, and the standard is checkable at any table where the dish is served under its own name — a check the entry not only allows but invites the marks are the dish's tolerances and its testimony at once: the tolerances say how far it may bend, the testimony says it has not broken, and both are matters of record in Maine rather than matters of opinion anywhere

It lives in the record of the island and Down East kitchens. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the geography of Maine itself, written small, for wherever it lives, it lives because the place produces what the dish needs and the people to insist on it, in season and out — and beyond that point, geography, for such a dish, is destiny with better scenery — it lives in Maine because Maine is the kind of place that produces it, its waters and its winters and its stubbornness about doing things the kept way — and the entry therefore leaves both the dish and its meaning exactly where it found them

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Sea Moss Pudding shows the method of this whole book: the thing the reference says about everything it files — that it was here, that it is documented, and that it belongs to the people who keep it — said here with the same plainness — and beyond the method, a library of food that consumed the food would be a poor library indeed, so this one catalogues, carefully, and leaves the eating to the region, where eating has always belonged in the end the reference stands as a witness for the dish, not a monument, not a review, not an advertisement, and witnesses do not embellish: they state what they saw, where they saw it, and what it was, and a witness who has done that may step down A dish like Sea Moss Pudding is the argument for the reference itself: left to memory alone it blurs within a generation, but set down with its origin and its marks it can be found again exactly as Maine keeps it.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; regional food histories and community cookbooks of Maine.
