Lumber Camp Cooking

The cookhouse tradition of the Maine woods — bean hole beans, biscuits, pies in quantity, and tea strong enough to stand a spoon in. The food at Lumber Camp Cooking matters, but the gathering is the dish: Maine cooks it communally, serves it communally, and remembers it communally.

The lumber camp cook — the 'cookee's' master — was the camp's most valued man; a camp with a bad cook lost its crew, and the bean hole was dug beside every camp kitchen. This entry's record rests on load-bearing claims standing on two sources, drawn from state records, fair and fishery reports, church and community cookbooks, and the standard regional food histories — and beyond that pair, a claim-by-claim sizing, because entries in this canon grow or shrink with their documentation and never with the enthusiasm of their editors If a claim about Lumber Camp Cooking could not be found twice it is not in this entry, however often it is repeated at tables, because repetition is not corroboration and the reference has always known the difference

The food was fuel and morale at once: beans, salt pork, molasses, dried-apple pie, and biscuits called catheads for their size. The defining traits are the ones Maine's own cooks police, at the stove and at the table, by correction and by example, whenever a hand drifts from the standard, and they hold because the standard is written in no cookbook, and the traits above are the kitchen corrections themselves, transcribed at last into print for readers who were not raised within earshot of them to describe the dish loosely would be to lose it, because the dish is its details, and Maine cooks hold those details the way singers hold pitch — not by thinking about them, but by wincing when they are wrong — and the traits above are the wince, transcribed

It lives in the memory of the north woods and in every bean hole supper that descends from it. For the reader who left Maine and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: the things that hold across decades — what the dish is, where it comes from, and what defines it — rather than the things that rot by spring — and beyond that point, there is a kind of map this entry draws without paper — the towns and tables of Maine where the dish belongs, connected by nothing but the dish itself — and repeated across enough towns, the dish becomes a region on the map of the mind, and this entry is one point on that map, fixed so it can be drawn again

Read alongside the other entries of Maine, Lumber Camp Cooking shows the method of this whole book: the record is frozen when it is finished, corrected only for outright error, and this entry is written to that standard from its first line to its last — and beyond the method, the entry is the region's memory of itself, written by itself and outsourced to print so that forgetting has to work harder to erase it, while the dish, unbothered by its own documentation, goes on being made at home every canon ends up teaching its readers how to read it, and this one teaches a simple lesson through every entry: read for the record, not the rhetoric — a dish, a state, a set of marks, a home ground, with the rhetoric kept on a short leash throughout — so that a reader who finishes knowing the record has read it correctly A tradition is the only entry in this canon that its subjects could write themselves, and in a sense the people of Maine did: the reference only transcribed what their attendance has been saying for generations.
