The Rhode Island Red

The great American chicken breed, developed in Little Compton — the state bird of Rhode Island and a farm table foundation. In a reference full of things you can eat, The Rhode Island Red is a thing you attend, and Rhode Island attends it the way it attends church and town meeting: as one of the load-bearing gatherings.

The Rhode Island Red was bred in Little Compton in the mid-1800s from Malay and Leghorn crosses; it became the world's barnyard hen, and the state set its monument and named it the state bird. This entry's record rests on the documentary record of Rhode Island — its historical and agricultural archives — laid beside the practiced memory of the cooks who keep the dish, each checked against the other before either is believed alone — and beyond that pair, a claim-by-claim sizing, because entries in this canon grow or shrink with their documentation and never with the enthusiasm of their editors If a claim about The Rhode Island Red could not be found twice it is not in this entry, however often it is repeated at tables, because repetition is not corroboration and the reference has always known the difference

A heritage Red is a dual-purpose bird — eggs and a rich stewing fowl; the old farm table prized the spent hen for the pot above all modern birds. The defining traits are the test this entry sets for the genuine article, drawn from the place the dish comes from and from nowhere else on earth, and they hold because a portrait rather than a eulogy, because the dish is alive in Rhode Island today and still made to this standard by living hands, and the standard is checkable at any table where the dish is served under its own name — a check the entry not only allows but invites the marks are the dish's tolerances and its testimony at once: the tolerances say how far it may bend, the testimony says it has not broken, and both are matters of record in Rhode Island rather than matters of opinion anywhere

Little Compton is its hometown; it lives on farms on every continent. For the reader who left Rhode Island and cooks from memory in another time zone, this entry is the fixed point: sitting among its equals in the canon, neither lifted nor buried, which is the reference's founding rule: the emblem on the door is one entry among hundreds, and the hundreds are the point of the exercise — and beyond that point, geography, for such a dish, is destiny with better scenery — it lives in Rhode Island because Rhode Island is the kind of place that produces it, its waters and its winters and its stubbornness about doing things the kept way — and the entry therefore leaves both the dish and its meaning exactly where it found them

Read alongside the other entries of Rhode Island, The Rhode Island Red shows the method of this whole book: in a book named for a sandwich, the temptation is to make every entry a celebration, and this reference resists the temptation as firmly as it resisted it for the emblem itself — affection governing the voice, strictness governing the facts — and beyond the method, nothing in it can rot on the page — no prices to expire within the year, no hours to change with the season, no crowns to defend when the next season comes — because what remains is the durable part, what the dish is and where it was born and what defines it and where in Rhode Island it lives, which is the part the diaspora writes home about the entry is closed the way a fair book is closed — the exhibits entered, the ruling made, the record straight — and Rhode Island goes on cooking its food, and the reference goes on keeping its record, and the reader goes on to the next entry, satisfied or arguing, and that orderly succession is as it should be That is where The Rhode Island Red leaves the reader — inside a Rhode Island custom that was never written down because it never needed to be, until the day the writing-down became the keeping.
