Rhode Island — A Food Profile

Rhode Island is the smallest state in this canon and, pound for pound, the most original. Nowhere else in New England — maybe nowhere else in America — did so small a place generate so many dishes that exist nowhere else on earth: coffee milk, clam cakes, pizza strips, hot wieners, dynamites, cabinets, doughboys. Rhode Island food is what happens when a dense, immigrant, bay-cut little state cooks for itself for two centuries and never once asks the neighbors' permission.

The bay is the first kitchen. Narragansett Bay cuts the state in two and stocks it: the quahog in all its grades, from the littleneck on the half shell to the giant that fills the stuffie and the chowder; steamers, scallops from the salt ponds, oysters that were once America's most famous; squid from Point Judith in quantities that made Rhode Island the calamari capital, a title the legislature ratified by naming it the state appetizer. The chowder map alone is a small atlas of loyalties — clear in South County, red between, white in the north — in a state you can cross in an hour.

The shore dinner is the bay made ceremony: chowder, clam cakes without number, steamers, lobster, watermelon, served to hundreds in halls built for the excursion age, of which Rocky Point's was the cathedral. The clambake steams on the beaches; the fleet is blessed at Galilee; the May breakfast — a Rhode Island invention of 1867, jonnycakes and all — opens the church season. And the johnnycake itself, white flint corn ground on the state's own stones, is argued east against west with a passion that larger states spend on politics.

The city is the second kitchen, and it speaks Italian and Portuguese and French. Federal Hill gave the state its zeppole, its spinach pies and pizza strips, its snail salad and Sunday sauce; Woonsocket, once the most French city in America, gave it tourtière and the dynamite; the East Bay's Portuguese parishes gave it cacoila and chouriço. Greek diner families gave it the hot wiener, ordered all the way, off an arm, at two in the morning. This is not a melting pot; it is a row of distinct kitchens sharing one small, hungry state, each keeping its own feast days.

The calendar is dense as everything else here. Zeppole in March, May breakfasts in May, the fleet's blessing and the shore dinners of summer, doughboys at the fairs, the quahog in every month the water gives it. Rhode Islanders leave reluctantly and write home for coffee syrup. This record is kept in their honor and for their table — the smallest state's, and by its own cheerful account, the best-fed.

If Rhode Island were a menu, half of it would need a glossary and all of it would be worth the trouble: cabinets and gaggers' cousins the wieners, stuffies and dynamites, strips and cakes that are not cake. The state cooks in its own dialect, and the dialect is delicious. Rhode Islanders know exactly what they have. It is the rest of the country's loss that it has taken this long to write it down in one place.
