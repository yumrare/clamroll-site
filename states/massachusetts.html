Massachusetts — A Food Profile

Massachusetts is where New England's food first became a literature. The commonwealth had the ports, the printers, and the ports' appetite: Boston published America's early cookbooks, codified its chowders and bean pots, and shipped its tastes — salt cod, cranberries, baked beans in a can — to the rest of the region and the country. To read Massachusetts food is to read the region's table of contents, written in a hand that assumed, correctly, that everyone else would follow.

The state's geography is two kitchens sharing one name. The coast — Cape Cod, the Islands, the North Shore, the South Coast — is a shellfish and fish cuisine: the fried clam of Essex, the quahog of the Cape, the oyster of Wellfleet and Duxbury, the scallop fleet of New Bedford, the cod that hangs carved over the legislature. Inland, the farm kitchen runs on the older economy: cornmeal and molasses, salt pork and beans, orchard fruit from Worcester County's hills, and the steamed brown bread that completes the Saturday plate. The two kitchens meet in the chowder pot, which every Massachusetts cook makes both ways depending on the day's catch and the family's doctrine.

Onto this double foundation the state layered the food of its arrivals. The Portuguese of New Bedford and Fall River built a second canon entire — linguiça and kale soup, malasadas and sweet bread, the Holy Ghost festas — that is now simply Massachusetts food. The Irish gave Boston its boiled dinner and its hash. The Italians of the North End, the French of the mill towns, the Chinese-American cooks of Fall River with their impossible, beloved chow mein: each wave found a table already set and added a course.

What Massachusetts does best is codify. Boston cream pie, house rolls, the chocolate chip cookie, the official dessert arguments — this is the state that writes its food down, names it, and legislates its affections. Its dishes carry dates and birthplaces more often than any other state's, which makes it the natural anchor of a reference like this one. The record here is deep because Massachusetts kept it.

The living table still runs on the old calendar. May brings the first steamers; summer is the clam shack and the lobster roll argued two ways at the same window; October is cider donuts in the orchard towns and cranberry red on the bogs; winter is bean suppers in church basements, Indian pudding by the wood stove, and the Thanksgiving that Plymouth gave the nation, kept at home with particular pride. The diaspora that left for warmer work carries these dates like a second calendar, and it is for them, in part, that this record is kept whole.

If Massachusetts were a menu, it would open with the fried clam and close with the cream pie, and everything between would be argued about by people who have eaten it all their lives. That is the commonwealth's real contribution to the region's table: not any single dish, but the conviction that food is worth recording, naming, and defending — the conviction this whole reference is built on. The other five states cook from it more than they admit.
