Maine — A Food Profile

Maine's food is what the cold gives and what work makes of it. This is the region's working coast and its deep woods at one table: the lobster and the bean hole, the whoopie pie and the ploye, foods built by people who harvested, hauled, milled, and froze for a living and cooked like it mattered, because it did. Maine never had Boston's printers or Connecticut's peddlers; it had the harvest itself, and its canon is the harvest written down late and faithfully.

The coast is the first kitchen. The Gulf of Maine gives the state its defining animal and its defining argument — the lobster roll, cold and mayonnaise-dressed by Maine law of custom, against Connecticut's hot butter — and behind the lobster stands the full cold-water pantry: clams steamed and chowdered thin, crab picked Down East, shrimp in winter, oysters from the Damariscotta, sardines in the cannery century that built the eastern towns. The shore dinner, eaten where the boats land, is the coast's liturgy, and the red snapper hot dog is its comic relief, defended with total seriousness.

The second kitchen is the woods and the farm. The lumber camps dug the bean hole and fed crews on beans, biscuits, and pie in quantities that read like exaggeration until you price a winter's logging. Aroostook County grew the potato into a civilization — the harvest that closed the schools — and the St. John Valley kept an Acadian table entire: ployes for bread, cretons for the jar, fricot and tourtière for the feast, in French, at the end of the American road. Franco-American mill towns — Lewiston, Biddeford, Waterville — carried the same food south and made it Maine's second language.

And over everything, the wild harvest: the barrens' blueberries, raked in August as they have been for centuries; fiddleheads in the three green weeks of spring; dulse on the Down East rocks; spruce gum in the woods camps. Maine's canon is the least store-bought in New England, because Maine's larder was the landscape, gathered by people who knew exactly where they were.

The calendar is strict and beloved. Spring: smelts, fiddleheads, the first salmon with the first peas, kept as a Fourth of July memory of springs before. Summer: the shore dinner, the lobster, the red snapper, wild blueberries at the end of it. Fall: the potato harvest, the bean hole suppers, venison season. Winter: pea soup, tourtière at Christmas, the long keeping of the bean pot. A Mainer abroad reads this calendar the way others read a family Bible — for the names, and for the proof that home is still keeping time.

If Maine were a menu, it would be short, seasonal, and non-negotiable: what the boat brought, what the garden gave, what the woods offered, in that order. The state's genius is restraint — the lobster roll with almost nothing on it, the chowder with almost nothing in it — because the ingredients never needed help. Maine cooks like a place that trusts its own water and woods, and the trust has been earned every season for four hundred years.
