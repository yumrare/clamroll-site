Vermont — A Food Profile

Vermont is the only landlocked state in this canon, and it compensated by becoming the region's dairy, its sugar bush, and its conscience. No coast, no port, no fishing fleet: instead, the maple tree and the Jersey cow, the orchard and the gristmill, and a food culture built on two transformations — sap into syrup, milk into cheddar — that it performs better than anywhere else on earth and regulates like a religion. Vermont food is what New England eats when it comes in from the cold.

Maple is the first fact. Sugaring is the state's opening season, a month when the whole commonwealth smells of the evaporator and the roads fill with steam. The syrup grades are a state law and a flavor calendar; the byproducts — cream, sugar candy, sugar on snow with its pickle and its donut — are a cuisine entire. Vermont sweetens what its neighbors sweeten with molasses: its beans, its pies, its custards, its ham glaze. To understand the state's palate, understand that sweetness here is not imported. It is tapped, carried, and boiled within sight of the table.

The second fact is cheese. Vermont cheddar — aged, crystalline, unapologetic — is the dairy monument, and the state's law of the table pairs it with apple pie by statute's good-faith effort, one of the few food laws in America written with a straight face and a full heart. Around the cheddar the farmstead revival built a second dairy age: bloomy rinds, blues, goat cheeses from the hillsides, curds eaten squeaking at the creamery door, butter the color of June. The cow — the Jersey, fawn-colored and rich-milked — is the state's true emblem, whatever the license plates say.

The rest of the canon is the hill farm's: the Green Mountain potato, bred in Charlotte and famous for a century; the Gilfeather turnip of Wardsboro, sweet enough to be named the state vegetable; orchard apples from the Champlain shore, with boiled cider and cider jelly in the pantry; buckwheat cakes under hot syrup; chicken pie with nothing in it but chicken, by doctrine defended in print; dilly beans in the pantry, ramps in the April woods, fiddleheads on the riverbanks, trout in the Battenkill. The French border adds its tourtière and its sugar pie; the fairs, Tunbridge above all, judge the maple like a state championship, because it is one.

Vermont's calendar is the shortest and the sweetest. Sugaring first — before the roads are passable, the season that is a holiday. Then ramps and fiddleheads, the creemee stands opening at Memorial Day, corn and tomatoes in the valley gardens, the fairs of September, the harvest suppers, the first snow on which next year's sugar will fall. The Vermont diaspora is small and famously unwilling to be consoled by other states' syrup. This record is kept so that the consolation, when it comes, is at least accurate.

If Vermont were a menu, it would list two ingredients and a hundred forms: the maple and the cow, in every transformation patience allows. Syrup to sugar to cream; milk to butter to cheddar to curd. The state's cooking is an essay on what a place can do when it refuses to import its pleasures — when the sweetener grows on the hill and the cheese ages in the valley and the pie, by law's gentle insistence, arrives with its cheddar.
