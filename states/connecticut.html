Connecticut — A Food Profile

Connecticut is New England's inventor, and its food proves it. This is the state that argues — with documents — that it invented the hamburger, that perfected a pizza the nation now flies in for, that looked at Maine's lobster roll and answered with butter. Connecticut's table is the region's most argumentative and, bite for bite, maybe its most consequential: a small state where the shore, the river, and the city each produced a dish the rest of America now eats without knowing the address.

The shore is the first kitchen, and Long Island Sound its pantry: oysters from Norwalk that seeded the Atlantic world, clams in every grade, scallops landed at Stonington by the state's last great fleet, bluefish and blackfish and scup from the summer and fall water. Onto this pantry the shore built its monuments: the hot buttered lobster roll at Milford, which is less a sandwich than a rebuttal; the clambake in seaweed steam; the fried clam and the stuffed clam, argued at every stand from West Haven to Stonington.

The river is the second kitchen. The Connecticut River gave the state its shad — the spring run that fed the valley for centuries, celebrated in bakes and derbies, its roe a delicacy of a few weeks — and its intervale farms gave it corn, pumpkins, the cheese pumpkin of the old pies, and Wethersfield's red onions, once currency. Hartford, at the river's head of navigation, gave America the election cake: democracy, spiced and fruited, baked by the hundredweight. The valley's first farmers, the Pequot and Mohegan among them, gave the whole region its corn — the samp, the suppawn, the succotash under half the dishes in this book.

New Haven is the third kitchen, and the loudest. The apizza of Wooster Street — coal-fired, charred, clam-topped in its white masterpiece — is now ranked among the world's pizzas by people who have never seen Connecticut, and the city holds two more claims beside it: the hamburger's best-documented birthplace, and, in the steamed cheeseburger of nearby Meriden, the region's strangest and most defended burger. Connecticut food is a courtroom where every dish has a paper trail, and the eating is the verdict.

The calendar keeps the argument seasonal. Shad in the spring, with the derbies; the shore all summer, lobster rolls hot; fairs in the fall — Goshen judging the hills' baking; the oyster festivals of September; election cake in its season of memory; Thanksgiving in the valley where the pumpkin is a field crop. Connecticut's exiles argue about pizza in every city in America. This record is kept so they can argue from the documents.

If Connecticut were a menu, every dish would carry a footnote — a date, a town, a name, a claim staked and defended. No state's food arrives with more documentation or more confidence. The rest of New England teases Connecticut for it and eats the pizza anyway. The record, as this reference exists to show, is largely on Connecticut's side: the claims were kept because the dishes were worth claiming.

Sources: state historical and agricultural records; the entries of this reference.
