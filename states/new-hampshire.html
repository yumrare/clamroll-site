New Hampshire — A Food Profile

New Hampshire cooks between two giants and keeps its own counsel. It has eighteen miles of coast, a hundred miles of Connecticut River intervale, the White Mountains, and a mill-city French quarter — and it has turned each into a table. The state's food is the region's in miniature but never in copy: the same bean pot, sweetened with its own maple; the same tourtière, in Manchester's own accent; the same shore dinner, on the shortest coastline in New England and served with the pride of a state that wastes none of it.

The Seacoast is the first kitchen, and Portsmouth its capital — a fishing port older than the nation, whose chowder, fried clams, and Great Bay oysters carry the whole maritime canon in eighteen miles. Strawbery Banke was named for the wild strawberries on the river bank, and the Seacoast still marks its year in small, intense harvests: smelts through the winter ice, strawberries in June, the oysters coming back to a bay that is healing. Hampton Beach added the boardwalk course — fried dough, taffy, the clam basket eaten in the sun — when the mill cities began their summer migration to the water.

Inland, the farm kitchen runs on the intervale and the orchard. The Connecticut River's floodplain grows the corn; the hill orchards give cider, cider donuts, boiled cider, and apple butter; the sugar bushes give the state its sweetening and its March festival. The Canterbury Shakers, in their ordered village, taught the whole region how to dry, preserve, and season — their table is preserved in this canon as a debt acknowledged. And the grand hotels of the White Mountains once served mountain trout and venison to a thousand guests a night, proof that the state's wilderness had a cuisine the cities would travel for.

Manchester is the second capital, and its food is French. The Amoskeag mills drew Québec down the Merrimack, and the city's west side still keeps the full Franco-American canon: tourtière at Christmas, cretons in the jar, pea soup through the winter, and poutine — the border dish that Manchester adopted, celebrated, and made its own with a fervor Québec regards with amusement and respect. Berlin and the North Country keep the same table at the woods' edge, next to the game suppers and the lumber tradition.

The calendar is the farm's and the church's. Sugaring in March, sugar on snow at the open houses; the May of the first greens; summer on the Seacoast and at the lake; September's fairs — Deerfield above all — where the pies are judged like livestock; October's pumpkin pride, Keene's carved thousands, the turkey suppers in every town hall; winter's bean suppers and pea soup. It is a small state's table, and it is complete — which is exactly the New Hampshire way of doing anything.

If New Hampshire were a menu, it would be a church supper and a sugar house menu printed on the same card: beans and brown bread, tourtière and sugar on snow, trout and taffy at the edges. The state takes the region's canon personally — every dish in it has a New Hampshire telling, and the tellings hold. Small coast, small farms, long winters: the portions, visitors should know, are none of those things.
